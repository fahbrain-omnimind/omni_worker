Constructing AI power: anthropomorphising imaginaries of generative
AI by AI companies
Anastasia Glawatzki1
Received: 10 March 2026 / Accepted: 20 August 2026
© The Author(s) 2026

Abstract
In descriptions of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), companies often anthropomorphise their innovations by attributing human-associated characteristics or capabilities to them, e.g. intelligence, understanding or reasoning. I argue that
anthropomorphism can become a structuring part of sociotechnical imaginaries—visions of desirable futures attainable
through advances in science and technology. While imaginaries of GenAI have been addressed in existing research, both the
connection between anthropomorphism and imaginaries as well as the perspective of AI companies as creators of hegemonic imaginaries remain understudied. Drawing on a qualitative content analysis of 35 documents from OpenAI, Meta and
Mistral AI, I explored which anthropomorphising imaginaries are employed by AI companies and how anthropomorphism
constructs the technology as powerful in future societies. The analysis reveals that companies anthropomorphise GenAI
both in terms of content and language, creating three anthropomorphising imaginaries. In the imaginary of GenAI as a tool
with human capabilities, the companies construct a neoliberal future in which human cognitive capabilities will be embedded in GenAI to serve as an intelligent productivity tool. The imaginary of AI as an empowering helper draws a future
with human–AI-cooperation and transhumanistic enhancement. Finally, the imaginary of GenAI as an anthropomorphised
superintelligence assumes that GenAI will surpass humans and act autonomously through advanced cognitive abilities.
These imaginaries imply three types of AI power: instrumental, collaborative and autonomous. The imaginaries and their
according constructions of AI power fuel post- and transhumanist ideologies, depoliticise AI from the exploitative conditions of its creation, legitimise AI companies as authoritative creators of this technology and foster technical dependencies
as well as an uncritical appropriation and integration of AI. The findings contribute to critical AI research by (1) explicitly
reconstructing the anthropomorphising imaginaries of AI companies as under-researched yet increasingly dominant actors
in shaping AI futures, (2) broadening the critique of anthropomorphisation as an instrument of corporate power in steering
sociotechnical developments and (3) advancing a theoretical account for the effects of anthropomorphisation as embedded
in sociotechnical imaginaries.
Keywords Anthropomorphism · Generative AI · AI power · Sociotechnical imaginaries · Anthropomorphising imaginaries

Although scholars have recently begun to explore imaginaries of GenAI (e.g. Cohen et al. 2024; Dishon 2024;
van Es and Nguyen 2024), two research gaps persist. First,
existing works focus on anthropomorphisation through
users (e.g. Go and Sundar 2019; Klein and Martinez 2023;
Wagner and Schramm-Klein 2019), whilst the perspective of AI companies remains understudied. Since Big
Tech companies increasingly occupy a “hegemonic position in imagining and shaping future society” (Mager and
Katzenbach 2021: 233) through their private research and
development, an analysis of their future visions is urgently
required. Second, anthropomorphisation has so far been
investigated only as a sub-aspect of imaginaries (de Seta
and Shchetvina 2024; Markelius et al. 2024; van Es and
Nguyen 2024). However, the anthropomorphism-driven
construction of AI power with its far-reaching consequences for present and future societies calls for an explicit
analysis of anthropomorphising imaginaries. In this paper,
I address both desiderata by examining (1) which anthropomorphising imaginaries are employed by AI companies,
and (2) how anthropomorphism constructs the technology
as powerful in future societies. Through a qualitative structuring content analysis (Kuckatz and Rädiker 2022) of 35
corporate documents from OpenAI, Meta and Mistral AI,
I identified three anthropomorphising imaginaries: GenAI
as a tool with human capabilities, as an empowering assistant and anthropomorphised superintelligence. As I will
show, these imaginaries and their according constructions
of AI power fuel post- and transhumanist ideologies, depoliticise AI from the exploitative conditions of its creation,
legitimise AI companies as authoritative creators of this
technology and foster technical dependencies as well as
an uncritical appropriation and integration of AI. In light
of AI companies’ infrastructural power to realise their

imaginaries, it is crucial to discuss their normative implications and reimagine desirable futures with GenAI.

2 State of research
GenAI is a type of AI that creates new content based on
the patterns and relationships identified in its training data
(Brindha et al. 2025: 2). Technically, it often builds on
Large Language Models (LLMs) that recognise and produce natural language by predicting the probability of subsequent characters and strings in text (Bender et al. 2021:
611). Within this long history, not only the term artificial
intelligence has been contested (McCarthy et al. 1995; Wiener 1961/2019), but the capabilities and characteristics of
AI have also been intensely discussed. For example, scholars disagree on whether AI is able to think and understand
(Davis and Marcus 2015; Firt 2024), exert autonomy (Butlin
2024; Formosa et al. 2025) and act creatively (Boden 2009;
Franceschelli and Musolesi 2023) in a human way. This
debate largely draws on anthropocentric notions of intelligence, creativity and autonomy, revealing an ideological
divide between those who consider these capacities as technically reproducible and those who view the human preconditions of these qualities as evidence for AI’s limitations.
This foreshadows an anthropomorphising tendency in how
AI is conceptualised.
Anthropomorphisation—defined as the attribution of traditionally human-associated qualities to non-human objects
(Epley et al. 2007)—has been identified as a rhetorical strategy serving interest-driven, often capitalist agendas (Hunger
2023; Rehak 2021). I argue that AI companies deploy it strategically, framing their technologies as humanly intelligent
and autonomous to advance their commercial interests. The
anthropomorphic exaggeration of AI capabilities (Placani
2024) frames this technology as powerful, fostering trust and
motivating adoption whilst simultaneously fuelling hype and
attracting investment (Bender and Hanna 2025).
Previous literature has examined anthropomorphisation
of AI primarily from the perspective of users. For example, scholars have positioned conversational aspects of AI
as triggers for anthropomorphisation and have examined
the attribution of human characteristics—e.g. friendliness,
helpfulness, autonomy or intelligence (Laban 2021; Wagner
and Schramm-Klein 2019)—as well as human roles—e.g.
servant, partner or master (Sarigul et al. 2024; Schweitzer
et al. 2019)—to AI. Concerning the effects of anthropomorphisation, studies suggest that it positively affects the feeling
of social presence (Go and Sundar 2019), trust in technology
(Klein and Martinez 2023) and emotional connection to it
(Araujo 2018). While these works trace the mechanisms and
effects of anthropomorphisation on an individual level, they
do not account for anthropomorphisation as a structural part

of broader sociotechnical visions. However, I argue that this
perspective is essential to extend the anthropomorphisation
concept from an individual psychological process to a strategic practice in which technology companies envision future
societies and construct their technology as powerful in them.
To understand how anthropomorphisation gains traction in shaping sociotechnical realities, it can be situated
within the concept of sociotechnical imaginaries. Rooted in
science and technology studies and originally proposed in
the context of nation states (Jasanoff and Kim 2009) before
spreading into other areas such as energy, environment and
technology (Hendriks et al. 2025), this concept helps to
understand which sociotechnical visions of the future are
considered desirable by certain actors, how these visions are
negotiated, and which of them prevail and influence societal
decisions. Jasanoff (2015: 4) defines sociotechnical imaginaries as “collectively held, institutionally stabilised, and
publicly performed visions of desirable futures, animated
by shared understandings of forms of social life and social
order attainable through, and supportive of, advances in
science and technology”, entailing both visions of society
and of GenAI. Imaginaries function constitutively in reflecting the political structure and social order in which they
were formulated (Bareis and Katzenbach 2022: 863–864).
Simultaneously, they operate performatively because their
articulation generates expectations and structures actions
(Bareis and Katzenbach 2022; Hautala and Ahlqvist 2022),
consequently influencing economic, political and scientific
agendas (Richter et al. 2023).
In context of GenAI, Big Tech companies increasingly
shape powerful imaginaries (Mager and Katzenbach 2021).
This influential position can be traced back to two reasons.
First, their research ideas have higher contagiousness in
comparison with academia (Giziński et al. 2024: 13). Second, their technical innovations often precede regulation,
which grants AI companies freedom to realise their imaginaries without political constraints. Sociotechnical futures,
in this way, become commodified “as they are enacted and
performed by hegemonic technology companies and their
expanding infrastructures, services, and products” (Mager
and Katzenbach 2021: 232–233). This has significant consequences: through naturalising, neutralising and legitimising
technologies in their imaginaries, companies create impression of technological inevitability and prelude the discussion
of alternatives (Markham 2021).
Given the powerful position of AI companies in shaping AI imaginaries, the need for research becomes evident. However, only few studies have addressed corporate imaginaries to date, revealing abstract, futuristic and
fantastical visions of superintelligence alongside visions
of human–AI-cooperation or AI as an efficiency-oriented
force for innovation (de Seta and Shchetvina 2024; Hsiao
and Shorey 2025; van Es and Nguyen 2024). Previous

research on AI imaginaries has instead focussed on states
(e.g. Hoff 2023), media (e.g. Dishon 2024; Natale and Ballatore 2020), public sphere (e.g. Cohen et al. 2024) and AI
practitioners (e.g. Hautala and Ahlqvist 2022; Stanusch
and Rogers 2026). Furthermore, the role of anthropomorphism in imaginaries has not been sufficiently explored
yet. However, studies show that anthropomorphism is
central to the construction of a technological myth of
human-like AI, fuelling transhumanist discourses (Natale
and Ballatore 2020), creating technological hypes (Bender
and Hanna 2025) and influencing use of and political decisions upon the technology (Rehak 2021). It is therefore
crucial to explicitly analyse anthropomorphising imaginaries, with anthropomorphisation conceptualised as a constitutive, structural part of sociotechnical future visions.
I argue that by reconstructing AI companies’ anthropomorphising imaginaries, it becomes possible to reveal
and critically examine ideologies, values and images of
humanity which undergird and stabilise the social order
that these actors advance.

3 Methods and sample
My study aims at analysing anthropomorphising imaginaries
and constructions of power within AI companies through a
qualitative research approach. Since imaginaries only take
shape through their collective representation (Jasanoff 2015:
4), their analysis requires the perspectives of multiple actors
in order to derive shared visions. Simultaneously, a cultural
and political proximity between the actors must be ensured
in the analysis: since imaginaries are “a projection of a
political culture” (Bareis and Katzenbach 2022: 865), differences will emerge in the ways how Western versus Southern AI companies envision desirable futures. My analysis
focussed on Western companies, including cases from U.S.
and France. Within this cultural context, I aimed at a most
diverse sample that accounts for different approaches and
priorities in AI development to capture anthropomorphising
imaginaries in their heterogeneity. My sample consisted of
three companies developing GenAI:
• OpenAI, a U.S. AI research and development company

based in San Francisco (OpenAI 2025) with 800 million
weekly active users (Reuters 2026)
• Mistral AI, a French AI start-up based in Paris (Mistral
AI 2025a), which is often referred to as Europe’s most
promising competitor to U.S. models (Sun and Berthelot
2025)
• Meta, a U.S. technology company based in Menlo Park
(Meta 2025b), which provides its Llama models as open
source (Meta 2025a)

The anthropomorphising imaginaries of these three
companies are reflected in corporate documents and can
be reconstructed from them. Within a publishing period
between November 2024 and April 2025, I selected the most
recent documents from each company that met one or both
inclusion criteria. These criteria were (1) the explanation of
abilities, features or performance of the model and (2) the
discussion of the model’s context of use. Here again, my
goal was to ensure diversity: the final sample consists of
35 documents (see Fig. 1), including company updates and
“About us” pages, product announcements, research updates,
case studies and user guides/documentation. In three cases,
I used documents from outside the sample period: for Meta,
to broaden thematic coverage; for Mistral AI, due to a high
relevance of a 2023 company update; and for all companies’
user guides/documentation, as I sampled these living documents by content rather than recency. For better readability,
I will abbreviate the documents with a letter and a number
in the results section: O stands for OpenAI, M for Mistral
AI, and L for Meta (Llama). The number following the letter
indicates the document (e.g. O1, M2, L3). The full list of
analysed material is provided in the appendix.
To answer the first part of the research question—which
anthropomorphising imaginaries are employed by AI
companies?—I imported the documents into MAXQDA
and analysed them using Kuckartz and Rädiker’s (2022)
qualitative structuring content analysis approach. The hermeneutical, reflexive orientation of the qualitative analysis
allowed for a careful reconstruction of the anthropomorphising attributions to GenAI within the documents, as well
as the imaginaries these attributions give rise to. I initially
coded the material using deductive categories derived from
the theory and the state of research: (1) practices of AI use,
(2) promises of AI performance, (3) linguistic strategies of

For the second part of the research question—how
anthropomorphisation constructs AI as powerful in future
societies—I developed a typology of AI power. I drew on
Kelle and Kluge’s (2010) approach, which allowed me to
systematically derive these types from the GenAI imaginaries through a process of contrasting. First, I derived dimensions of power from the imaginaries of the AI companies.
Then, I contrasted the imaginaries along these dimensions to
derive central features of power and to interpret their meanings through further comparisons within and between the
imaginaries. The aim was to create internally homogenous
and externally heterogeneous idealised types of AI power
which represent the imaginaries of the AI companies.

4.1 Abilities‑ and characteristics‑based
anthropomorphisation of GenAI
The analysed AI companies anthropomorphise GenAI by
attributing human cognitive, communicative, social and personal abilities and characteristics to it, with the cognitive
ascriptions being the most prominent across the documents.
Terms such as “chain of thought” (O2: para. 2) or “smart[]”
(L3: para. 5) frame algorithmic processes by using human
cognitive metaphors. GenAI, so the companies’ argument
goes, leverages its knowledge as well as deep and systematic understanding and reasoning to comprehend the task
and give “accurate answers” (L7: para. 5). This anthropomorphising language conflates AI calculation and human
cognition, setting the expectation for human-like problemsolving and complexity handling. When ascribing intelligence to GenAI, the companies often present its cognitive skills as intrinsic. For instance, Mistral AI claims that
“LLMs, by nature, aren’t great at performing calculations”
(L8: Program-aided language models section). This rhetoric
of naturalisation positions GenAI as an independently evolving, even organic entity—a portrayal that negates the human
design of the technology and conceals its material costs and
consequences (Elish and boyd 2017: 2).
In addition to cognitive aspects, another domain of
anthropomorphisation in the documents concerns communication skills. By attributing GenAI linguistic adaptation
and formal responsiveness, the companies construct it as
a dialogic counterpart—a representation that scholars have
identified as an anthropomorphisation trigger on the part
of users (Wagner and Schramm-Klein 2019). This demonstrates parallels between the anthropomorphisation by
AI companies and users, with companies preconfiguring
anthropomorphising interpretations through their design and
description of technology—a mechanism commonly referred
to as scripts (Akrich 1992) or affordances (Hutchby 2001).
As a communicative agent, the companies characterise
GenAI as empathetic, friendly and humorous, yet also highly
responsive to the user’s needs. Meta, for instance, claims that
its Llama model is able “to switch casually between tonal
types, including but not limited to humour, empathy, intellectualism, creativity and problem-solving” (L10: Prompt
template section). This quote creates the impression of a
friendly and human-oriented GenAI, corresponding to the

self-representation of ChatGPT observed by van Es and
Nguyen (2024).
Regarding social skills, the companies draw on the argument of cognitive sophistication—especially understanding,
thinking, planning and reflecting—to attribute “instruction
following” (M4: Use cases section) and helping abilities to
GenAI in both everyday and professional contexts. While
this anthropomorphisation frames GenAI as a servant or
assistant under human control, the companies also describe
more technically advanced models as collaborators.
Finally, the AI companies anthropomorphise GenAI by
attributing a human personality to it. For instance, Meta
states that Llama is “an expert conversationalist” and “companionable and confident” (L10: Prompt template section).
This prescription of individual character to GenAI traits
allows it to narratively transcend its function as technology
and appear as a human-like subject.
Apart from these cases of anthropomorphisation, the
companies refrain from depicting GenAI in a human-like
way in four cases. First, anthropomorphisation in the analysed documents applies only to certain characteristics such
as intelligence, autonomy and communication, whereas
others—most notably creativity—remain outside its scope.
Second, many attributions of skills to GenAI emerging
throughout the documents retain a primary technical character. Third, when describing use contexts of GenAI, the companies often employ non-anthropomorphising terminology.
However, the surrounding context often lends these technical
descriptions a humanising tone. Fourth, when addressing
the responsibility for shaping GenAI—particularly regarding
safety—the companies also refrain from anthropomorphisation, positioning themselves as responsible agents for AI
development.

4.2 Imaginary 1: GenAI as a tool with human
capabilities
The anthropomorphisation of GenAI in corporate documents
gives rise to three anthropomorphising imaginaries, the
first of which depicts GenAI as a tool endowed with human
capabilities. It resembles the “smooth everyday” imaginary
identified by de Seta and Shchetvina (2024) in machine
vision companies, where a convenient, reliable, ubiquitous
technology is expected to make everyday life easier. In addition, it pictures a future in which human intelligence will be
replicated in a seemingly neutral, human-controllable tool,
which will be commodified with the promise to enhance
productivity and democratise human skills whilst simultaneously devaluing them. Building on the notion of usefulness,
the companies echoing this imaginary position the technology as fundamentally desirable, legitimising themselves as
indispensable actors in the neoliberal social order whilst
excluding the option of non-use.

AI & SOCIETY

Within this first imaginary, the companies construct a
future in which human cognitive abilities will be technically
encoded into GenAI, thereby increasing its performance and
enhancing the appeal of its use. Previously described anthropomorphic notions of cognition gain special relevance in this
context. Referring to intelligence as “build-in” (O3: para.
13) within their systems, the companies suggest that human
thinking, understanding, learning, reflection and knowledge
can, should and will be technically replicable and optimisable, echoing the general promise to automate the human
with sufficient data (Andrejevic 2020: 3–4). Intelligence,
in this vision, becomes a property of a technical tool—a
commodity managed and sold by private companies, with
anthropomorphism driving the commodification. This not
only provokes the question of whose intelligence is encoded
into the tool, opening space for racist and ableist conceptions
of intelligence, but also conceals the human, often exploitative practices of data collection and verification through
which AI is manually produced (Mühlhoff 2020).
Within their documents, the companies instrumentalise
cognitive anthropomorphisms for promises of enhanced performance. For example, Mistral AI associates the attribution of understanding to GenAI with the notion of “unprecedented accuracy” (M5: para. 3) in tasks such as document
analysis and image recognition. This anthropomorphismdriven imagination of a high-performance tool directly translates into promises of user value, motivating GenAI adoption
in present and future societies.
Although the companies acknowledge some technical
shortcomings in their current systems—for example, Meta
states that Llama may “hallucinate information or make up
facts” (L8: Reduce hallucinations section)—they imagine
a future in which GenAI’s abilities will be optimised and
flaws corrected. OpenAI, for instance, claims that they are
“continually refining the models’ reasoning capabilities with
images to be more concise, less redundant, and more reliable” (O2: para. 15). This vision of an almost-there future in
which current technical errors will be fixed shifts the focus
from current functions to abstract potentials (Elish and boyd
2017: 10). As Stanusch and Rogers (2026: 2) argue, it allows
the AI industry to absorb current critiques by “making the
future emerge as if it had already happened in the present”.
For present-day societies, this could lead to the AI trust
paradox: using GenAI despite its limitations and risks not
justifying trust, but doing so because of the prospect of better technology (Kreps et al. 2023).
The companies further envision a future in which
GenAI will serve as a neutral tool under human control
and execute human instructions without possessing intention or autonomy of its own. This characterisation is most
evident in Mistral AI’s framing of GenAI as a “practical
tool” (M2: para. 7). In this future vision, initiative, autonomy and intention are expected to remain with the user. For

instance, the companies claim that users and developers
can “shape and control the outputs for their particular use
cases” (O8: Instruction following section) through technical parameters and prompt engineering. This quote, whilst
presenting GenAI as a neutral tool for user and developer
interests, naturalises the design processes, decisions and
actions implemented by AI companies, depoliticising their
technologies and obscuring political and economic motivations and biases.
Deeply intertwined with this imagination of an intelligent
and neutral tool under human control is the societal role that
the companies envision for GenAI. Building on a neoliberal
society concept, they construct a future in which GenAI will
permeate all areas of life and increase the productive performance of the individual. For example, Meta’s promise
that GenAI will “save time and expand capacity” (L6: title)
outlines a future in which humans will be augmented by
AI to remain competitive and productive, paying obedience
to neoliberal principles of quantification, performance and
efficiency (Rehak 2021: 163). Other companies share this
vision: in an interview with OpenAI, Cameron Adams, Chief
Product Officer and Co-founder of Canva, states that “people with AI are going to do way better than people without
AI” (O7: heading 8). This optimistic argument of productive
value delivered by GenAI frames non-use as non-desirable,
as it will be penalised in the collective competition for performance. Under the guise of usefulness, the coupling of
performance and AI use creates FOMO, establishes compulsions to use and invisibly reinforces dependencies on AI
companies.
Imagining GenAI as a tool endowed with human capabilities also draws on a future in which human capacities will
be democratised through technology. OpenAI elaborates on
that promise in creative contexts by stating that “the early
stages of the creative process … are now accelerated and
democratised so that anyone with an idea can come up with
a visual” (O7: para. 16). The future suggested here is one
in which humans will be able to compensate deficiencies
in skills through GenAI use. However, the shiny argument
of democratisation overshadows unequal access to technology and veils negative consequences that occur when human
skills are replicated in GenAI, e.g. devaluation of human
creative labour (De Cremer et al. 2023) and AI-induced job
losses in the creative industries (Zhao 2024). In doing so,
this imaginary strengthens a techno-solutionist (Morozov
2013), corporate-driven understanding of democracy.

4.3 Imaginary 2: GenAI as an empowering assistant
The second anthropomorphising imaginary envisions GenAI
as a learning and helpful assistant controlled by humans
and integrated in all areas of life to compensate human
deficiency. In this context, “helpfulness” is defined by AI

AI & SOCIETY

companies who position themselves in realising this service for a passive and grateful humanity. This legitimises a
paternalistic, technocratic approach to the shaping of GenAI.
Throughout the imaginary, the companies oscillate between
anthropomorphising competence attributions that present
GenAI a helpful assistant and assurances of human control
and superiority that frame the societal future with GenAI as
safe and harmless.
A central assertion in this imaginary is that GenAI will
become human-compatible in the future. In contrast to the
first imaginary where anthropomorphisms are employed for
the narrative of usefulness, they are used here to picture
GenAI as a cooperative, helpful counterpart in future societies. For example, Mistral AI links the notion of “highquality pre-trained knowledge” to the promise of “nuanced,
evidence-based responses” (M2: para. 5). This illustrates
how the cognitive anthropomorphisation depicts GenAI as
a knowledgeable dialogue partner. Human-compatibility is
further reinforced through Meta’s anthropomorphisation
of GenAI as “natural, conversational, and, most important,
helpful” (L1: 2025 section). This attribution of conversational abilities to GenAI, whilst mirroring anthropomorphisation of AI found in user literature (Wagner and SchrammKlein 2019), outlines a future where interaction with GenAI
will be indistinguishable from human communication. Blurring ontological lines, this promise of natural conversation
conceals the data-based nature of GenAI technology, obscuring how its communicative aspects are designed to motivate use and enable data processing for monetary corporate
benefit.
The human-compatible character of GenAI in this imaginary is further developed in its depiction as a helper for
humans. The companies imagine a future in which GenAI
will be integrated within daily routines as a “a comprehensive AI assistant for your life and work” (M2: heading).
According to them, GenAI’s helping abilities extend from
general assistance in “manag[ing] tasks, milestones and
events to stay on top of everyday life” (M2: para. 15) to
professional and even emotional support. Through anthropomorphising GenAI as an entity able to “understand user
intent”, Meta claims that Llama would be aware of “the user
… looking for chit‑chat, emotional support, humour or venting” (L10: Prompt Template section). Anthropomorphising
GenAI as an empathetic listener comparable to a human
constructs a vision of a future in which GenAI will offer
psychological aid on par with humans. This development,
whilst questionable in light of emotional dependencies on AI
(Laestadius et al. 2024), raises broader questions about the
desirability of human substitution in emotional relationships.
Besides this imagined help by GenAI, the companies also
outline a future where the technology will complete tasks
autonomously. For example, OpenAI claims that GenAI can
“independently execute tasks on your behalf” (O3: para. 1),

anthropomorphising the technology as an actor guided, but
not controlled by humans. The companies’ suggestion to
delegate work to GenAI is again underpinned by a neoliberal
societal order, with the promise to “get the job done faster,
better” (M11: subheading) consolidating capitalist fantasies of maximal efficiency. However, this anthropomorphic
imagination of GenAI as an efficiency-boosting work assistant veils how the growing delegation of tasks to AI leads to
de-skilling, depriving humans of experiences with forgotten
skills and weakening resilience of communities through AI
dependencies (Valenzuela et al. 2024: 244). The criticism
must go even further, touching upon the pure imagination
of human liberation through GenAI. As Andrejevic (2020:
2) argues:
The machines won’t free us up to fish or philosophise—they will do these in our stead—and according to the priorities set by those who own and control
the automated infrastructure. This is automation not as
liberation but as total control.
Notably, the imagined role of GenAI as an empowering
helper goes beyond the neoliberal ideology. It entails an
argument of human deficiency: the pure need of a GenAI
assistant implies the premise of an exhausted or otherwise
limited human. Thus, the companies imagine a future in
which fallible, limited humans are to be enhanced by GenAI
in a transhumanist logic. In this context, OpenAI uses the
anthropomorphising attribution of intelligence to ChatGPT
to highlight how it helps in complex problem-solving: “ChatGPT’s enhanced visual intelligence helps you solve tougher
problems by analysing images more thoroughly, accurately,
and reliably than ever before” (O2: para. 3). Framing ChatGPT as an enhancer of human intelligence, the company
echoes Kurzweil’s (2006) transhumanist singularity thesis,
which expects human and artificial intelligence to merge and
produce an even more powerful entity. The implicit pointing
to human limitations in information processing is economised as a strategic argument for GenAI deployment. The
human is devalued to better sell the assistance capabilities
of GenAI.
Despite this powerful role of GenAI in future societies,
this imaginary depicts the technology as unfinished and
evolving, tied to a prospect of a future in which GenAI will
progress even further through human guidance. In this context, the companies anthropomorphise GenAI as a humanlike learner, a mentee that needs advice from a more experienced human. For example, OpenAI states that “a GPT
model is like a junior coworker” (O8: Prompting reasoning
models section) because it needs explicit instructions to
perform well. This anthropomorphising comparison with a
junior coworker projects learning potentials into the future.
Quotes like “help[] the language to think” (M10: Summarization section) demonstrate how companies expect users

AI & SOCIETY

and developers to actively support GenAI in this “learning”
process. Through this anthropomorphisation, they reframe
human training of GenAI as pedagogical care, reinterpreting
unpaid labour as desirable and obscuring how this labour
serves to optimise their products, generate profit and consolidate market positions. This extends human exploitation
for GenAI from invisible ghost work (Gray and Suri 2019)
to ubiquitous everyday GenAI interactions.

4.4 Imaginary 3: GenAI as an anthropomorphised
superintelligence
The third anthropomorphising imaginary constructs a transhumanist, technocratic future in which GenAI will not only
reach but surpass human-level intelligence and knowledge
through self-directed evolution. Here, anthropomorphism is
used in a dual form. On the one hand, attributions of intelligence promise intellectual partnership and collaboration
with GenAI, fuelling AI hype and positioning AI companies
as powerful creators of a technical superintelligence whilst
discouraging human shaping of AI and critical scrutiny of its
epistemology. On the other hand, they are instrumentalised
to depict an abstract AI threat, legitimising a protectionist
development agenda according to corporate interests.
Within this imaginary, the companies envision a future
in which GenAI will reach superior levels of intelligence.
Anthropomorphic notions of a “groundbreaking intelligence” (L5: Build with Llama section), tied to promises of a
“new class of problem-solving” (O3: para. 8), portray GenAI
as unprecedentedly powerful. This science-fiction driven,
futuristic rhetoric of a superintelligence can be classified
as a mechanism of AI hype, imbuing the technology with
superior qualities and presenting AI companies as “powerful—if not godlike—in their technical creation” (Bender and
Hanna 2025: 4). It is even strengthened by the anthropomorphising expectation of an independent cognitive evolution.
Meta, for instance, claims that due to its “advanced intelligence”, Llama 4 Behemoth was “the perfect choice to teach
the smaller Llama 4 models” (L3: Pushing Llama section).
Through anthropomorphising one model as a human-like
teacher for other models, the company constructs a future
in which GenAI progress will unfold independently from
humans. Resonating with Kurzweil’s (2006: 44) transhumanist notion of autonomous technical evolution, this future
vision displaces humans as active shapers of GenAI and
evokes a technocratic imaginary in which the future will
not be socially negotiated but determined by AI itself.
Anthropomorphising notions of AI intelligence are
closely linked to attributions of domain expertise, creating the vision of a future in which GenAI will act as a
human-like expert. For example, OpenAI claims that its o3
model “pushes the frontier across coding, math, science,
visual perception, and more” (O3: para. 2). The implicit

anthropomorphisation in this quote is that GenAI possesses
human-like knowledge in STEM fields. This anthropomorphism attributes epistemic authority to a technology that
generates text based on data and probability calculations
(Bender et al. 2021), reducing the meaning of expertise to
the amount of processed data (Elish and boyd 2017: 10)
and devaluing richer, situated ways of knowing in human
experts. Additionally, since the label “expert” contains normative assumptions about whose knowledge and experience
are valid and trustworthy (Díaz and Smith 2024: 358) and
creates an epistemic obligation to rely on the experts’ statements (Ferrario et al. 2024: 12), the attribution of an expert
role to GenAI neutralises criticism of its output and portrays
it as infallible.
In a future where GenAI is anticipated to reach a humanlike level of intelligence and expertise, its relationship with
humans is expected to change. The companies envision a
society in which humans and GenAI will collaborate as
equals. Anthropomorphising GenAI as a “thought partner”
(O3: para. 2) continues the argument of AI evolution from
the second imaginary, presenting the technology as fully
trained, mature entity that has left the roles of mentee and
assistant to think and solve problems alongside humans. This
partner role culminates in OpenAI equating GenAI with
human professionals on senior level: “A reasoning model is
like a senior co-worker. You can give them a goal to achieve
and trust them to work out the details” (O8: Prompting
reasoning models section). The anthropomorphising comparison here renders rust in GenAI as desirable given its
assumed human-like expertise. This not only exaggerates
GenAI performance whilst obscuring risks and limitations;
the label of trustworthiness also normalises the opacity of
its inner workings and de-prioritises human oversight and
control which are essential in light of bias and discrimination in and through GenAI (Methnani et al. 2021).
Within some documents, the partner role of GenAI
evolves into a teacher role, underpinned by the anthropomorphising narrative that the intelligence and competence of
technology will turn it into a superior and altruistic advisor
for humans. For example, Meta states that developers can
ask GenAI “to explain code, help detect and fix bugs” (L7:
2). The anthropomorphisation in this quote is subtle, framing GenAI more competent than the human counterpart in
routine tasks. “Explain this to me” (L8: Explicit instructions
section) thus becomes a linguistic shorthand that positions
humans as intellectually inferior to AI in societal futures.
This vision again echoes Kurzweil’s (2006: 26) transhumanist singularity thesis, which predicts that rapid technological
progress will soon subsume “all human knowledge and proficiency”. While this scenario casts humanity as threatened
by AI dominance, the anthropomorphising teacher metaphor
evokes safety and goodwill, reassuring that AI will guide
rather than overpower humans.

The companies further imagine a future in which GenAI
will act intentionally and autonomously, growing independent from humans. This becomes particularly visible in OpenAI’s argument of self-directed reasoning:
Reasoning allows the models to react and pivot as
needed to information it encounters. For example, they
can search the web multiple times with the help of
search providers, look at results, and try new searches
if they need more info. (O3: para. 13)
Anthropomorphising GenAI as an actor capable of
informed decision-making, critical reflection and iteration,
the company suggests that human contribution to AI will no
longer be required. The desirability of this vision is linked
to the notion of technical progress, implying that more technical independence results in higher innovation. However,
promoting GenAI as intentional and autonomous conceals
its dependence on human labour—e.g. programming, tuning, repairing, training and debugging (Rehak 2021: 161). In
addition, it raises the question of accountability by positioning technical self-regulation as a prerequisite for desirable
innovation, removing companies from the responsibility to
shape GenAI in accordance with ethical standards.
While the companies generally emphasise the desirability of GenAI autonomy, they imagine GenAI developing
dangerous capabilities in the future, which will necessitate
their intervention to save humanity from the technological
threat. For example, OpenAI predicts “AI Self-improvement
capabilities” (O11: para. 6) and the ability of GenAI for
“Autonomous Replication and Adaptation” (O11: para. 7) to
emerge and pledges to “measure progress on these capabilities, and build safeguards against the risks that these capabilities create” (O11: para. 4). Here, anthropomorphisms are
used to frame GenAI as powerful beyond human control,
evoking a science-fiction fantasy of Frankenstein’s monster
with human-like autonomy and superior capabilities rebelling against its human creators (Dishon 2024). This vision of
a future threat is thus instrumentalised by AI companies to

legitimise themselves as protectors of an abstractly vulnerable humanity.

4.5 Construction of AI power in future societies
The three anthropomorphising imaginaries give rise to three
constructions of GenAI power in future societies: instrumental, cooperative and autonomous (see Table 1). These types
build on each other, with the first type locating power mainly
in the hands of humans, the second assuming a shared power
between humans and GenAI and the third locating the power
mainly within GenAI. They differ along six dimensions: AI
performance, AI development, integration into human practices, type of human trust in AI, AI’s positioning in society
and legitimisation of AI power. In what follows, I will present these types of AI power and discuss their normative
implications for present and future societies.
Instrumental AI power: The first type of AI power corresponds to the imaginary of GenAI as a tool with human
capabilities. By anthropomorphising the technology with
cognitive abilities such as thinking, knowing, understanding and learning, the companies project expectations of high
performance onto it, thus constructing it as powerful in present and future societies. However, since this performance
is expected to emerge as a result of human optimisation,
GenAI’s power remains framed as under human control.
Besides this narrated performance as an intelligent tool,
the construction of instrumental AI power rests on its integration in human routines, which is encouraged through
anthropomorphism-fuelled promises of GenAI’s usefulness
to enhance productivity in neoliberal societies. Within these
use settings, the companies further construct GenAI’s power
by encouraging a functional trust into the technology. Here,
cognitive anthropomorphisms portray GenAI as trustworthy
due to its intelligence, suggesting that it will reliably complete tasks under human control.

powerful in future societies through positioning it as an

to GenAI advantages—e.g. the democratisation of creative

Cooperative AI power: The second type of AI power
corresponds to the imaginary of GenAI as an empowering
helper. Here, expectations of performance are shaped by
cognitive, communicative and social anthropomorphisms
that construct GenAI as both intelligent and human-compatible in present and future societies. By further anthropomorphising GenAI as a mentee or assistant, the companies frame
future performance improvements as outcomes of human
contribution, particularly human guidance in “thinking”,
“understanding” and problem-solving.
Both anthropomorphising roles of student and assistant construct a collaborative AI power by suggesting a
human–AI relationship in which humans “teach” GenAI
and GenAI supports humans in everyday and professional
tasks. Thus, AI power is expected to emerge in collaboration between humans and GenAI rather than through GenAI
use by humans. Within this collaboration, the companies
promote functional trust in GenAI by creating expectations
of it reliably executing tasks under human supervision. In
addition, they encourage affective trust in the technology by
employing the anthropomorphising rhetoric of a benevolent
and helpful GenAI assistant. Notably, both trust in and collaboration with GenAI are reflected in ChatGPT’s visual
self-representation as a “friendly AI assistant” identified
by van Es and Nguyen (2024). This suggests an overlap
between the AI power that the companies construct in their
imaginaries and the technology’s actual output, illustrating
the formative power of anthropomorphising imaginaries on
design.
Regarding GenAI’s societal position, cognitive anthropomorphisms construct the technology as an expansion of
human intelligence. The corresponding transhumanist promise to overcome human cognitive limitations with GenAI
legitimises its power in present and future societies. In
addition, the companies frame GenAI as socially desirable
through pointing to its potential to support and free humans
from work—an argument that further legitimises AI power.
Autonomous power: The third type of AI power corresponds to the imaginary of GenAI as an anthropomorphised superintelligence. Here, the companies use cognitive anthropomorphisms to suggest superior levels of
intelligence within GenAI, constructing it as an unprecedently high-performance, powerful technology disrupting
present and future societies. This argument is reinforced
through anthropomorphised intelligence attributions to
GenAI, positioning it as an expert or teacher capable of
directional and normative action. Unlike the first two types
of AI power, future performance improvements are here

framed as product of GenAI’s self-optimisation, premised
on anthropomorphic ascriptions of autonomy and intentionality. This contributes to the construction of autonomous AI power by presenting GenAI as independent from
humans.
Building on these anthropomorphising notions of autonomy and intelligence, the companies present work delegation
to GenAI as both desirable and efficient, framing it as powerful in successfully fulfilling tasks with only minor human
instructions. This narrative motivates both functional and
epistemic trust in GenAI. The functional trust in GenAI is
promoted through references to the companies’ safety precautions designed to protect humanity from the abstractly
dangerous, anthropomorphised superintelligence, whilst the
epistemic trust is fostered by the anthropomorphisation of
GenAI as an expert whose outputs merit confidence.
Echoing the findings by Hsiao and Shorey (2025: 411),
the companies position GenAI as a human substitute in
future societies. Through anthropomorphisms, they portray
GenAI as sufficiently human-like to replace humans whilst
simultaneously endowed with superior capabilities that justify this replacement. To legitimise GenAI’s power, the companies invoke the argument of a transhumanist AI evolution,
using anthropomorphising metaphors to construct GenAI’s
progress as technically determined. Notably, states also
often portray GenAI as disruptive and inevitable, citing the
increasing power of big tech companies (Hoff 2023). This
illustrates how corporate GenAI imaginaries—and the AI
power they construct—shape the imaginaries of other actors.
This anthropomorphisation-driven construction of AI
power has normative implications for present and future
societies. First, its underlying assumptions of GenAI intelligence, human compatibility and expertise foster an uncritical acceptance of—and trust in—the technology. Second, it
frames the use of and collaboration with GenAI as inherently desirable, stabilising the technology as an integral
part of individual routines and broader societal structures
whilst sidelining the discussion of alternatives. Third, the
construction of AI power calls for a sociotechnical realisation, promoting human labour for GenAI whilst simultaneously obscuring it to frame the technology as intelligent
and autonomous. This depoliticises GenAI by concealing
the exploitative practices of its creation. Fourth, this power
construction normalises post- and transhumanist ideologies
along with their corresponding conceptions of the human
and human–AI relationships. Fifth, it legitimises AI companies as creators of an ostensibly powerful technology and
strengthens their authority in shaping GenAI in line with
their corporate imaginaries. These implications demonstrate that the social desirability of GenAI is not given but
constructed by AI companies. This prompts the question to
what extent their imaginaries should be accepted—and how
alternative sociotechnical futures can be imagined instead.

5 Conclusion
In this paper, I have proposed the term anthropomorphising imaginaries as a conceptual synthesis of sociotechnical imaginaries and anthropomorphism, theorising how
anthropomorphising attributions structure sociotechnical
future visions. Drawing on a qualitative content analysis
of corporate documents from OpenAI, Meta and Mistral
AI, I have shown that the companies anthropomorphise
GenAI both in terms of content and language, leveraging
three anthropomorphising imaginaries. In the imaginary
of GenAI as a tool with human capabilities, the companies
construct a neoliberal future in which human cognitive
capabilities will be embedded in GenAI to serve as an
intelligent productivity tool. The imaginary of GenAI as
an empowering helper outlines a future with human–AIcooperation and transhumanistic enhancement. Lastly, the
imaginary of GenAI as an anthropomorphised superintelligence assumes that GenAI will surpass humans and
act autonomously through advanced cognitive abilities.
These findings suggest that anthropomorphising imaginaries construct not only a vision of GenAI, but also of
society itself, including human image, ideology, social
order and the relationship between human and technology.
Furthermore, I have identified three types of AI power that
the companies construct within their anthropomorphising
imaginaries: instrumental, cooperative and autonomous.
These power types emerge through the projection of specific performance capacities and developmental trajectories, cultivating trust in GenAI, promoting its integration
into human practices, positioning it within social structures and ultimately legitimising its expanding power.
Within their anthropomorphising imaginaries, the
companies promote largely techno-solutionist as well as
post- and transhumanist agendas, positioning GenAI as an
indispensable and desirable part of their promised futures.
However, I argue that AI companies not only articulate
anthropomorphising imaginaries but have the capacity to
materially shape sociotechnical futures. Their infrastructural power (Flensburg and Lai 2023) allows them to realise imaginaries in infrastructures of AI research and development—creating “technological, economic, political,
and behavioural lock-ins” (Böschen et al. 2024: 95) that
impede change and foreclose alternative future visions.
Especially in a regulatory vacuum, AI companies can
impose their imaginaries on society, thereby sidestepping
democratic negotiation. The AI race further exacerbates
this problem: in an environment where technical supremacy is the priority, visions and realisations of the future
will be determined not by their collective societal acceptance, but by the speed and resources of the AI companies.
This urges to critically consider normative implications of

corporate imaginaries and their corresponding constructions of AI power to counter Big Tech’s dominance and
reimagine desirable futures with GenAI.
The findings of my analysis have theoretical implications
for the concepts of imaginaries and anthropomorphisation.
While the concept of imaginaries was originally proposed
for nation states (Jasanoff and Kim 2009), AI companies
increasingly gain relevance in shaping sociotechnical futures
(Mager and Katzenbach 2021). In light of this, I argue that
the concept needs to be politicised and developed in a critically-normative direction, challenging its predominantly
descriptive use that risks uncritically reproducing and legitimising the societal implications of corporate imaginaries.
The same holds true for the concept of anthropomorphism.
In addition, linking both concepts to anthropomorphising imaginaries has helped moving anthropomorphisation
beyond the conscious or unconscious attribution of human
qualities to non-human entities (Epley et al. 2007) to a
strategic ascription that constructs power in the anthropomorphised object. Therefore, I propose to integrate actors
and outcomes of anthropomorphisation into the theoretical
concept. While prior scholarship has criticised the anthropomorphisation of technology as exaggerated and misleading
(Hunger 2023; Placani 2024), my analysis demonstrates that
this critique remains insufficient. It advances a new theoretical argument regarding anthropomorphisation’s effects,
which prior scholarship has often assumed without sufficient
theoretical grounding. Anthropomorphisation gains power
through its embedding in sociotechnical imaginaries—as a
structuring part of these, it shapes how technology is being
researched, developed, appropriated and regulated. Deployed
strategically by AI companies, it operates as an instrument
of hegemonic power in ordering present and future societies.
In sum, the findings contribute to the critical AI research
by (1) explicitly reconstructing the anthropomorphising imaginaries of AI companies as under-researched yet
increasingly dominant actors in shaping AI futures, (2)
broadening the critique of anthropomorphisation as an
instrument of corporate power in steering sociotechnical
developments and (3) advancing a theoretical account for
the effects of anthropomorphisation as embedded in sociotechnical imaginaries.
While enriching the theory and state of research in
anthropomorphism and imaginaries, the findings of this
study remain limited to the Western context. Companies
from other cultural contexts—such as the Chinese DeepSeek—may articulate different anthropomorphising imaginaries that not only shape not only their national AI research,
development, regulation and appropriation, but increasingly
influence Western imaginaries due to the international
popularity of their AI models. Future studies may thus
widen the analysis to a cross-cultural comparison, carefully

AI & SOCIETY

investigating the differences and similarities between the
anthropomorphising imaginaries.
