Title: Diverse Intelligence: an essential field for questions of AI and Consciousness

online April 21, 2025

Michael Levin,

ABSTRACT
Seth’s insightful discussion must be extended in light of the field of Diverse
Intelligence. Broader understanding of embodiment in unconventional spaces, of
hybrids between evolved and engineered materials, and of unexpected competencies
in very minimal systems make it very unlikely that we can place convincing limits on
the possible substrates of consciousness. Doing so risks underestimating not only
“machines” but also ourselves.

MAIN TEXT
There is much to agree with in Seth’s clear, cogent, and insightful analysis of
the problem of consciousness in AI’s [1]. Most convincing are the points regarding
the potential dissociation of intelligence and consciousness, the need to avoid making
synthetic systems with significant consciousness, the importance of lessons from
biology, and the insufficiency of computational functionalism. Here I focus on a few
foundational assumptions which I believe must be expanded significantly in order for
discussions of this topic to effectively inform both technical and ethical dimensions of
this problem in the decades to come.
A critical context for this discussion is the emerging field of Diverse
Intelligence [2, 3], in which we seek to understand what all agents, regardless of their
origin story or composition, have in common. Seth references critiques of the neural
replacement argument, but this is only the tip of the iceberg. The real issue concerns
the interoperability of life: cyborgs, hybrots, biobots, AI-mediated interfaces to the
collective intelligence of somatic cells, and all manner of products of synthetic
bioengineering are already here and will increasingly share our world. The question
isn’t whether a standard human can be replaced piece by piece with components, but
rather whether a being who develops as a coherent, functional integration of both
natural and synthetic components should be subjected to some sort of purity test

seeking to neatly classify them (with all the legal and ethical implications) as “real
human” vs. “machine”.
Advances in biology and bioengineering are making it clear that colloquial
linguistic categories of “life” vs. “machine” are not going to survive into the future [4,
5]. These ancient heuristics (and the ethical stances they drive) are simply not
keeping up with the development of science. Moreover, although Seth is not a carbonchauvinist, his understandable emphasis on “life” as a unique substrate for
consciousness must be evaluated in the context of a lack of agreement on what it
means to be living – not just because of thorny corner cases, but because we do not
fundamentally understand what the secret sauce is that is supposed to separate the
living from the non-living [6]. Despite the honorable efforts of organicist thinkers [7,
8], no convincing criteria have been proposed that are compatible with the facts and
capabilities of modern life sciences.
All of us make the smooth, continuous journey from a tiny, quiescent bag of
chemicals (the unfertilized oocyte) to that of a conscious adult who will make
statements about not being a machine. Developmental biology offers no bright line
where the system crosses over from being fully describable by chemistry and physics
to being fodder for behavioral science, psychoanalysis, and love. The former can be
assembled synthetically (as cell-free systems) and shades into the field of active
matter research [9, 10] that will surely merge with efforts to make AI. The lessons of
developmental biology and evolution are ones of gradualism – not magical binary
categories, but the need to understand metamorphosis, scaling, and self-assembly of
true minds from components that, whether proteinaceous or silicon, begin as minimal
systems and develop an inner perspective.
Given the continuity of our own origins, the onus is on those seeking to
establish distinctions to specify exactly what the special ingredient is. The
meandering trial-and-error origin process (evolution), analog computation,
multiscale autonomy architecture, etc. – all of these can be (and already are)
replicated by engineers. Seth asks, “Why might we think that AI could be or become
conscious?”. Our own journey from “matter” to “mind” suggests that that the null
hypothesis, given the remarkable magic of ensouled biochemistry in human beings
and other animals, should likely be “Why would we think that it would not be?”. And
while we seek to identify an optimal impedance match between systems and our
attributions of agency, I argue that the risks of false positives (objectophelia) are
smaller than those of false negatives (ethical lapses towards beings who can suffer).
The moral imperative, as well as the science of our own origins, suggests we should
skew high, not low, with estimates of consciousness until proven otherwise. Seth is
right to critique anthropocentrism as human exceptionalism. But I interpret it in the
opposite way: the danger is not to inappropriately view other systems as human-like;
rather, it is to believe that humans have some special magic that other systems surely
cannot have.

Our intuitions, shaped by our own evolutionary history, are in no way
sufficient to handle the problem of AI and diverse intelligence in general, unaided.
New theory and new technology were needed in order to detect and exploit regions
of the electromagnetic spectrum to which we are natively insensitive, and crucially,
to unify disparate-seeming embodiments of electromagnetism (static electricity,
lightning, magnetism, light, etc.) as being fundamentally manifestations of the same
thing. For the same reason we need new unifying concepts that find deep invariants
across embodied minds with different compositions and histories, to overcome our
innate mind-blindness.
We are unprepared to recognize intelligence, nevermind consciousness, in
systems that operate at unfamiliar spatial and temporal scales or in unconventional
substrates [11]. We can warm up with data on basal cognition in cells, tissues, plants,
etc. [3, 12], none of which are effectively encompassed by simple machine metaphors.
Recent advances in regenerative medicine provide empirical support to the use of
tools of behavioral science to understand and manipulate their function. Groups of
cells implement a collective intelligence that forms memories, pursues goals, makes
decisions, and solves novel problems in creative and ingenious ways [13, 14]. This
model system is important for discussions of consciousness in AI because it forms a
stepping-stone to more alien cases. The mechanisms underlying cell intelligence are
the same (indeed, evolutionarily homologous) to those that seem to underpin
consciousness in human bodies) – electrophysiological networks that scale memory
and cognitive light cone [15]. But they exert their competencies in unconventional
spaces: cells perform their sensing-deciding-acting loop in the high-dimensional
spaces of gene expression, physiological states, and anatomical morphospace. The ion
channels of our brain form electrical networks that navigate us through 3D space
using memories of the past and predictions of the future, but this system evolved from
a much more ancient use of these same electrical networks to similarly navigate the
state of the body from a single egg cell to that of a complex body shape [16]. Along the
way, both systems pursue goals, avoid obstacles, and deal with unexpected
perturbations with various degrees of ingenuity and success. In other words, they
sense, strive, and perhaps suffer, in these other spaces largely invisible to our
obsession with medium sized objects moving at medium speeds through 3D space
which we often take as the one “real” space for embodiment but is as constructed by
our cognitive autopoiesis as any of the others.
We are just beginning to adjust our intuitions (and vocabulary) to
accommodate the empirical discoveries that intelligence extends far beyond brainy
animals, and crucially, into problem spaces which are difficult for us to visualize. This
has several implications for AI. First, cognition (in its basal form) is far, far older that
neurons and brains. This means that AI should not be exclusively focused on
neuromorphic architectures, but the more fundamental principles that enable life to
project itself into diverse problem spaces. Second, “embodiment” does not mean a
robotic body that can trundle around in 3D space: both organoids and software AI’s,
which look to us as un-embodied (and thus critiqued as “not engaging with the real
world, and thus not really binding their symbols” etc.) may well be navigating spaces

that we are not primed to recognize. Embodiment is critical for minds, but that does
not mean only in 3D space. Finally, this is not just about intelligence. All of the criteria
commonly used to underpin allowance of consciousness with each other (the
problem of other minds) – similar composition, common evolutionary origin,
behavioral competencies that suggest valence, and causal architectures – now reveal
that we absolutely must take seriously the likelihood that our body structures have
their own (non-verbal) consciousness (i.e., the right hemisphere in split brain
patients is only the tip of the iceberg). Of course, it is not accessible to the main
linguistic Self who often says, “but I don’t feel my liver being conscious!” (but of
course, it doesn’t feel other humans to be conscious either). Most people find it
difficult to think this way; but if we cannot expand our detection of consciousness to
this close test case, any discussion of consciousness in AI is hopeless.
Some seek criteria for consciousness in the materials: they can consider
consciousness in biological tissues besides the brain, but surely the silicon-based AI’s
are different? It’s not impossible that some special aspect (e.g., quantum properties
of microtubules [17], of which all cells are full) turns out to be important, but at the
moment there is no reason to think so. Indeed, an excessive focus on mechanisms at
the bottom risks devaluing us all – it is an easy slide toward the reductionist thesis
that the deterministic laws of biochemistry and the acausal randomness of quantum
physics are all there is, and that our consciousness is an illusion. When there is no one
to speak out for beings embodied in unconventional materials, there will eventually
be no one left to speak out for all of us, who are embodied in “proteins that are just
doing what proteins do”.
What of the computationalist thesis? I agree with Seth that this does not suffice
for consciousness, but for an unusual reason. While many find it plausible that
biochemistry does not tell the full story of the mind, it is universally believed that
algorithms and materials tell the full story of “machines”. I urge us to remember, in
synthetic beings as much as in evolved ones, that our formal models never tell the full
story of what something is or what it can and cannot do. The limitations of Turing
paradigms and algorithm metaphors are just that – limitations of the formal models,
not of the system itself. Even humble Bubble Sort has competencies (not just
complexity or unpredictability, but properties recognizable by behavioral scientists)
and pursues unexpected side-quests not apparent from the short, transparent,
deterministic algorithm [18]. Simple sets of coupled differential equations can do
several different kinds of learning [19]. All of these were not recognized despite
decades of study, until looked at through the diverse intelligence lens [20]. If even
these truly minimal systems can do that, how many competencies, in what spaces,
perhaps having absolutely nothing to do with language, could be present in current
and future AI’s? These intrinsic motivations (things they do that are specifically not
prescribed by their algorithm) are likely to have much to do with being substrates for
consciousness, free will, etc. Perhaps there is no dead matter, no “machines” that are
not somewhere on the spectrum of life and mind.

I propose an unconventional position on the issues Seth addresses. We
already know that non-physical patterns (properties of mathematical objects)
constrain physics and are exploited by biology. Perhaps this is the mind-matter
interface we should explore for consciousness. I suggest that consciousness is not due
to physics, algorithms, or evolutionary processes per se. We don’t make
consciousness, neither during traditional reproduction nor in bioengineering. What
we make, with some competency but limited comprehension in both cases (for now),
are interfaces to patterns of form and behavior from a non-physical latent space that
contains low-agency truths of mathematics, and a very wide range of higher-agency
forms we recognize as minds. Consciousness is what we call the view from inside that
space. Consciousness is primary, and it is far too early to have anything but great
humility about our knowledge of what kinds of artificial or hybrid interfaces would
make embodiments for what kinds of minds. The exciting research program facing us
is to begin to map out that space (as mathematicians are doing for one corner of it),
and to work out the relationship between the causal architecture of physical,
biological, and other constructs and the patterns that ingress through it. On a practical
level, perhaps our relationship to future AI’s should be the familiar one used for
raising children: less Skinner (reinforcement learning etc.) and more Piaget:
recognize, understand, and help positively channel the intrinsic motivations that
were not forced on them by either our “algorithms” or the materials of which they are
made.
In summary, I think Seth raises key questions that are not really [21] about AI
at all. Certainly they are not about today’s software language models. They are
fundamentally about how it is possible to have conscious, morally important minds
in a world in which their embodiments are made of parts following rules (whether
those be rules of biochemistry, electronics, algorithms, etc.), and whether anything in
our world is only what our computational or other models say it is. I would
complement Seth’s powerful conclusion “If we sell our minds too cheaply to our
machine creations, we not only overestimate them – we underestimate ourselves” as
follows. If we seek to buy our exceptionalism too cheaply, we underestimate not only
“machines” but also ourselves.
