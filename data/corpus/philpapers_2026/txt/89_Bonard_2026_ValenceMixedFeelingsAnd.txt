1 Introduction
Affective episodes, including emotions, moods, urges,
pleasures, and pains, mobilize organisms to deal with
events that may be important to them (Dukes et al. 2021).
These episodes are typically associated with either pleasant or unpleasant feelings. However, the richness of human
experience cannot be reduced so easily. Indeed, a growing
body of evidence shows that individuals report experiencing pleasant and unpleasant feelings simultaneously (for a
meta-analysis of 63 studies, see Berrios et al. 2015). These
experiences, commonly referred to as mixed feelings, have
been relatively understudied and pose several challenges to
emotion theories (Cacioppo and Berntson 1994; Larsen et
al. 2001; Larsen and McGraw 2011; Massin 2014; Vaccaro
2024; Vaccaro et al. 2024).
The general aim of this article is to clarify how appraisal
theories of emotion, and in particular the Component Process Model (CPM; e.g., Scherer, 2001), can explain mixed
feelings, while identifying the main controversies and the
theoretical and empirical questions that remain open. Going
beyond the levels-of-valence framework proposed by Shuman et al. (2013), the article makes four main contributions.
First, it clarifies the explanandum by distinguishing different
types of mixed feelings and focusing on the most demanding form: single affective episodes directed at a single object

2 Explanandum: Mixed Feelings
This section clarifies the phenomenon to be explained:
mixed feelings, which we define in terms of experiencing
positive and negative valence simultaneously. We begin
then by outlining what we mean by valence.

2.1 Valence
The word valence is extremely polysemic and is used by
scholars to designate many different things, even within a
single academic branch (Colombetti 2005; Walle and Dukes
2023; Walle and Dukes, in press). Here, we will purposely
restrict our use to just one sense of that word.
By valence, we mean a positive or negative phenomenal
property, that is, a feature of what it is like from a firstperson perspective to consciously undergo a certain experience that feels good (or pleasant) or bad (or unpleasant). In
other words, valence is a property of a conscious, subjective

feeling. For instance, what it is like to feel pain or sadness
involves negative valence, what it is like to feel pleasure or
joy involves positive valence, and mixed feelings involve
both positive and negative valence simultaneously.
Rather than describing a case where there is neither
positive nor negative feeling as neutral valence, we would
simply say there is no valence. By contrast, if a particular
feeling involves equally strong positive and negative feelings, this counts as a mixed feeling of perfect ambivalence.
All conscious affective episodes involve feelings that are
positively valenced, negatively valenced, or mixed, with the
possible exception of neutral surprise – whose neutrality is
itself debated (Noordewier and Breugelmans 2013). While
examples of mixed feelings will be provided further below,
here is a non-exhaustive list of types of affective episodes
that can be positive, negative, or mixed:
● Emotions, such as joy, admiration, pride, fear, shame,
sadness, and anger.
● Moods, such as feeling cheerful, irritable, or free-floating anxiety, which resemble emotions but lack a clearly
delimited intentional object.
● Pleasures and displeasures tied to activities, such as
playing chess, conversing, or reading, as well as, on the
negative side, tedious chores or administrative tasks.
● Affective episodes resulting from satisfied or frustrated desires, such as the pleasure of winning or satisfying one’s hunger, and the displeasure of rejection or
frustration.
● Hot desires or cravings, which are valenced prior to satisfaction or frustration, such as pleasant erotic desire or
an intrusive urge to check one’s phone.
● Bodily pleasures and pains, including comfort and discomfort, such as the pleasure of a bath or massage and
the pain of burns, cuts, or headaches.
● Diffuse affective feelings without established substantive terms, such as a sense that something is wrong, a
feeling of eeriness, or a negatively charged atmosphere.
This list is not exhaustive, but it suffices to delineate what
we mean by “affective episodes” and “valence.”

2.2 Mixed Feelings
Mixed feelings, the mixtures of positively and negatively
valenced feelings, can be understood in three increasingly
restrictive senses.1
In the broadest sense, mixed feelings are affective episodes that are, at any given time, experienced as positive
and negative. Here, any simultaneous combination of a positive and negative affective episode counts as a mixed feeling. For example, having a headache while enjoying a good
novel qualifies as a mixed feeling in this sense.
In a second, narrower sense, mixed feelings are two or
more affective episodes simultaneously directed at a single
object whose valences are opposing. An example would be
feeling joy while listening to a song, while simultaneously
realizing that one feels guilty about listening to it, given the
artist’s despicable personal views or behavior (see Goffin
and Cova 2019). In the previous example, the headache
is not directed at the novel, and thus does not qualify as a
mixed feeling in this second sense.
The third, narrowest sense concerns affective episodes
directed at a single object, where the subject experiences a
single kind of affective state that is itself both positively and
negatively valenced. Here, an example might be the feeling of nostalgia directed at happy memories of a bygone
period, where pleasure and pain are fused. The feeling is not
a juxtaposition or alternation between joy and regret, but a
unified affective state. Mixed feeling in this sense excludes
the preceding song example, since it involves a combination
of joy and guilt, two clearly distinct kinds of affective state
that the subject can distinguish as such.
The defining criterion of this narrowest sense is phenomenological unity. In such cases of mixed feelings, the subject
does not experience two emotions as distinct affective ingredients, whether successively or simultaneously. Rather, the
episode presents itself as a single affective state whose positive and negative dimensions are constitutively integrated.
This criterion concerns the structure of conscious experience and does not imply that the underlying processes are
themselves unitary. A useful analogy is provided by visual
perception. The visual system relies on partially distinct
mechanisms for extracting colors, local contours (edges),
orientations, junctions, depth relations, and other features
of a scene. Yet these computations are not ordinarily experienced as separate perceptual acts: their outputs contribute
to a single, unified visual experience. Likewise, several

mixed feelings involving other types of affective episodes.
Mixed feelings raise specific difficulties for two of the
three main theoretical traditions in the psychology of emotion: basic emotion theories and psychological constructivism. While basic emotion theories can easily explain mixed
feelings in the broader sense (see Ekman, 1992 pp. 194–
195), they struggle to explain mixed feelings in the narrowest sense because the latter seem not to be reduceable
to combinations of basic emotions (cf. Ortony and Turner
1990). The main problem for the psychological constructivism tradition is that it uses a univariate notion of valence that
cannot directly capture mixed feelings (Cacioppo and Berntson 1994). For instance, a univariate scale of valence cannot empirically capture the difference between no valence
and a perfectly ambivalent mixed feeling: an identical net
valence score of “0” would register for both of these very
different experiences (Gasper 2026). On a bivariate scale,
one where there are independent measures of positive and
negative feeling, no valence would register as “0” for both,
whereas perfect ambivalence would register as two identical, non-zero scores.
Basic emotion theorists and constructivist theorists might
respond to these remarks in various ways, for instance by
denying the existence of mixed feelings in the narrowest
sense (cf. Massin 2014). However, our goal here is not to
focus on these responses but on how appraisal theories – a
third major tradition in the psychology of emotion – can
account for mixed feelings. As we will see, it offers an elegant account of their nature.

3 Explanans: Appraisal Theory and ProtoValences
In this section, we present appraisal theory and show how
it can explain mixed feelings. The notion of proto-valence,
introduced by Shuman et al. (2013)3, will play a major role.

3.1 Appraisal Theory
The central contribution of the appraisal theory tradition
(for a review, see Ellsworth 2025) is an answer to the following question: How are emotions elicited? In particular,
how can we explain that the same stimulus elicits different
types of emotions in different people or in the same person
at different times while very different types of stimuli may
elicit the same type of emotion?
To account for this complexity in the relations between
types of stimuli and types of emotional responses, appraisal
theory posits a cognitive intermediary: the appraisal process. For instance, imagine that when a mouse enters the
restaurant, Sam suddenly feels afraid, Maria is amused, and
Bob remains calm. Appraisal theory claims that Sam, Maria,
and Bob react differently because they appraise the presence of the mouse in different ways. Sam appraises it as a
threat, triggering fear. Maria appraises it as incongruous to
see the wild animal in the expensive restaurant, triggering
amusement. Bob appraises it as irrelevant to his interests
and remains indifferent. Similarly, the fact that very different types of stimuli can elicit the same emotion is explained
by the hypothesis that they are appraised in the same way –
for instance, a remark and a slap can elicit anger if both are
appraised as offensive.
In short, appraisal theory holds that emotions are elicited by appraisals of objects as sufficiently relevant to our
motivational states or concerns. By motivational states or
concerns, we mean needs, goals, preferences, values, ideals,
desires, wishes, inclinations, aspirations, appetites, interests, motives, aims, internal reasons, or any other source of
motivation accessible for an evaluation.
In contemporary versions of appraisal theory, emotions
are conceived as synchronized changes across five components: appraisal, physiological processes, action tendencies,
motor expressions, and subjective feelings (see e.g., Sander
et al. 2005). Changes in the first component – appraisal –
trigger and determine changes in the others. For example,
Sam’s appraisal of the mouse as a threat causes physiological changes (e.g., sweating, adrenaline release, increased
heart rate), changes in action tendencies (preparation to

flee), motor expressions (wide eyes, raised eyebrows, open
mouth, trembling voice, backward posture, raised hands),
and subjective feelings (the feeling of fear). The feeling
component is thought to consist of phenomenal and partial
access to changes in the other components. In the case of
fear, the associated negative feeling typically is the result
of the appraisal of danger, bodily arousal, readiness to flee,
and withdrawn posture. According to these versions of the
theory, it is thus the appraisal of the situation that constitutes
the main causal explanation of the valence of the emotional
episode.
It could be argued that appraisal theory was already
defended in antiquity, for instance by Aristotle, for whom
emotions (pathē) were judgments (or appraisals) accompanied by bodily changes, action tendencies, and pleasure
or pain (see his Rhetoric, Book II, Chaps. 1–11). Anger,
for example, was conceived as the judgment that an injustice has occurred, accompanied by a boiling of the blood,
a desire for revenge, and pain. This general approach was
later developed by many philosophers, including Aquinas,
Hume, Brentano and some of his students, and was taken up
by psychologists from the 1960 s onward, notably by Magda
Arnold (1960) and Richard Lazarus (1966).4
One of Lazarus’s major contributions is the distinction
between two types of emotional appraisal, which he termed
primary appraisal and secondary appraisal (Lazarus 1966).
Primary appraisal concerns stimulus relevance. It addresses
questions such as: Does this stimulus constitute a threat, a
loss, or an opportunity? Secondary appraisal concerns coping capacities. It addresses questions such as: How should
I respond to this stimulus? Do I have the resources to deal
with it?
Lazarus initially distinguished primary from secondary
appraisals to explain emotional stress (Lazarus 1966). Stress
was hypothesized to involve not only a primary appraisal
that a stimulus is obstructive to one’s concerns but also a
secondary appraisal that one’s ability to cope is low. If the
stimulus was appraised as obstructive to one’s concerns
but that one’s coping potential were appraised as high, this
would not generate stress, according to Lazarus. Consider,
for example, the following case. Someone insults Joanna.
Her primary appraisal is that the insult is contrary to her
concern to be respected, but, because she can respond with
a joke she has ready to diffuse the situation, her secondary
appraisal is that the insult is easy to handle. She therefore
does not feel stressed or overwhelmed as she feels capable
of responding adequately to the disrespect, thereby restoring
the situation from her perspective.

3.2 The Component Process Model (CPM)
The CPM decomposes the appraisal process into an ordered
sequence of “stimulus evaluation checks”, each concerned
with a specific type of information relevant to the organism. Whereas Lazarus distinguished only two appraisals,
the CPM distinguishes four major appraisal checks, each of
which is subdivided into further checks. As we will see, this
fine-grained structure allows for a sophisticated explanation
of mixed feelings by distinguishing multiple proto-valences
that can jointly contribute to the ambivalence of a single
emotional episode.
Here are the CPM’s four major appraisal checks (Scherer
2001):
(A) Relevance. To what extent is this event relevant to me
or my reference social group?
(B) Implications. What are the implications or consequences of this event, and how do they affect my wellbeing and my short- and long-term goals?
(C) Coping potential. How well can I cope with or adjust to
these consequences?
(D) Normative significance. What is the importance of
this event for my self-concept and for social or moral
norms?
According to the CPM, appraisal processes consist in
rapid, rough, typically unconscious answers to these four
questions. The answers determine whether an emotion is
experienced and, if one is, then it determines the type and

intensity of that emotion. They do so by causing changes
in the other components of emotion: physiological changes,
action tendencies, motor expressions, and subjective feelings – including the valence of the emotion.
Each of the four major appraisal checks is subdivided into
more precise checks. The central hypothesis we will discuss
below (proposed by Shuman et al. 2013) is that several of
these checks determine positive or negative proto-valences,
which, in turn, determine the overall valence of an emotion. This provides a framework for explaining mixed feelings. We will also discuss potential challenges faced by this
hypothesis. These challenges are principally inspired by an
alternative appraisal theory: Agnes Moors’s goal-directed
theory of emotion (Moors 2022; Chap. 7)5.
We now turn to CPM’s sets of appraisal checks (A)–(D),
focusing on those that determine proto-valences.
Before, note that, throughout the article, the terms “positive” and “negative” are applied in a sightly different way to
valence and proto-valence. Valence is positive or negative
in virtue of its phenomenal pleasantness or unpleasantness,
whereas proto-valence is positive or negative in virtue of its
tendency, other things being equal, to contribute to positive
or negative conscious valence.6
3.2.1 (A) Relevance
The first set of checks aim to determine whether what is
happening warrants attentional and emotional mobilization.
This stage includes checks of novelty, intrinsic relevance,
and concern relevance (modifying slightly the terminology,
see Scherer (2013). The appraisal of intrinsic relevance can
directly determine valence, unlike the appraisal of novelty
and of concern relevance. We thus focus on the former here.
The intrinsic relevance check evaluates the situation in
itself. It is intrinsic in the sense that it is not a function of
the concerns represented by the subject, unlike the concern
relevance check. This is a more basic, more primitive check,
which “simply” compares the situation to a set of typically
low-level (e.g., sensorimotor) or over-learnt representations,
and directly assigns it a positive or negative proto-valence.
Intrinsic relevance evaluations are either genetically inherited or established through learned automatic

aversion elicited by a snake. Perhaps, though this has not
been studied, each type of intrinsically relevant stimulus
corresponds to a distinct type of proto-valence and, in turn,
causes different types of conscious valence.

3.2.2 (B) Implication
The second set of checks – implication – includes checks
of causality (e.g., is the cause of the situation an agent or
an inanimate source?), outcome probability (what is the
likelihood of such-and-such consequences?), expectancy
discrepancy (do the consequences diverge from what was
expected?), concern conduciveness (are the consequences
favorable or unfavorable to my concerns?), and urgency
(does the situation require an urgent response?).
From the standpoint of valence, the most central check
here is concern-conduciveness. It evaluates whether the
situation promotes, obstructs, or threatens the subject’s represented concerns (goals, needs, values, etc.). A situation
appraised as conducive or obstructive to the realization of
one’s concerns produces, respectively, a positive or a negative proto-valence.
As with intrinsic relevance, though it is not directly predicted by the CPM, one may hypothesize that different types
of concerns are associated with different types of protovalence. Indeed, the unpleasantness associated with a threat
to one’s safety – obstructiveness to one type of concern –
seems phenomenologically distinct from the unpleasantness
associated with obstructiveness to other types of concerns
– such as a disrespect for justice or the separation from a
loved one. Similarly, the pleasure of receiving a compliment
is phenomenologically different from the pleasure of hearing a good joke or from that of contemplating an athlete’s
excellence, and each corresponds to a situation conducive
to a different type of concern. It is even possible that each
type of (basic, final, non-instrumental) concern corresponds
to a distinct type of proto-valence and thus produce different positive or negative overall valence, though this has not
been tested either.
In all cases, according to the CPM, intrinsic relevance
checks and concern-conduciveness checks are distinct
mechanisms. Both can generate positive or negative protovalence and both can thereby contribute to the overall
valence of a single emotional episode. This makes it clear

Consider, for example, the feeling of the sublime directed
at an overwhelming natural phenomenon, such as the experience of an immense mountain or a violent storm observed
from a position of relative safety (see also Sect. 2.2). The
sublime illustrates not only the co-occurrence of opposing proto-valences but also their contribution to a unified
affective episode. The beauty of the scene may generate a
positive proto-valence through intrinsic relevance. At the
same time, its overwhelming scale may be appraised as
threatening to safety, as exceeding the subject’s power, or
as momentarily resisting control, thereby contributing negatively to the episode. These positive and negative evaluations and their opposing proto-valences need not produce a
distinct experience of admiration alongside a distinct experience of fear. The subject may instead undergo one feeling
of the sublime, in which something like fascination and felt
diminishment are inseparably integrated. Indeed, the negative contribution is not merely an accidental cost attached
to an independently positive experience. The sense of being
overpowered partly constitutes the distinctive positive value
of the sublime: without it, the experience might remain
beautiful or impressive, but would no longer have the same
affective character.
Another example mentioned earlier is that of pleasurable bodily pains from intense muscular stretching or deeppressure massage. A negative proto-valence derives from
nociceptive intrinsic relevance, while a positive one stems
from perceived benefits to interests or performance, a type
of concern conduciveness. In both cases, this would result
in a singular affective episode, a mixed feeling, arising from
a mixture of positive or negative intrinsic relevance checks
and concern-conduciveness checks.
According to the CPM, a single situation can be appraised
as conducive to some concerns and obstructive to others,
and thus lead to a mixed feeling. Think, for instance, of the
ambivalent mood of departure described above (Sect. 2.2).
The mood may be partially exhilarating due to the positive
proto-valence arising from an appraisal of favorable prospects (a type of concern conduciveness) and at the same time
painful due to a negative proto-valence from the appraisal of
the probable loss of relationships (a type of concern obstructiveness). These positive and negative proto-valences may
determine conscious feelings in such a way that the mood’s
overall valence is constitutively ambivalent. The mood is
not experienced as two distinct affective states, one positive

3.2.3 Potential challenge
At this stage, it is legitimate to ask: given that stimuli
appraised as intrinsically relevant are also typically stimuli
that are conducive or obstructive to our concerns, why distinguish intrinsic relevance from concern conduciveness?
The reason is that the two checks are assumed to involve
two distinct types of mechanisms. Intrinsic relevance is a
stimulus-driven mechanism (Dickinson and Balleine 1994;
Moors 2022), a kind of automatic mechanism that does not
take the subject’s motivational states into account. It is, in a
sense, blind to representations of the subject’s concerns, and
is therefore far more inflexible than concern conduciveness.
The latter is a goal-oriented mechanism (Dickinson and
Balleine 1994; Moors 2022). As such, it can change online
through the acquisition of new information relevant to the
subject’s concerns, and is more malleable through learning.
Finally, it is hypothesised to be slower: whereas intrinsic
relevance has been suggested to be evaluated around 100
milliseconds, concern-conduciveness would be evaluated
closer to 160 milliseconds (Grandjean and Scherer 2008).
It should nevertheless be noted that the question remains
open whether a given affective response is elicited by a
stimulus–response mechanism (such as intrinsic relevance)
or by a goal-oriented mechanism (such as concern conduciveness). Even the hypothesis that spider-like shapes
are intrinsically relevant is debatable (LoBue and Adolph
2019). Moreover, an appraisal theory that emphasizes the
role of goal-oriented mechanisms, such as Agnes Moors’s
goal-directed theory of emotion (2022, Chap. 7), explains
as many appraisals as possible in terms of concern conduciveness rather than intrinsic relevance (see also Bonard,
2026b). For instance, even an innate response to a reward
like sugar could be conceptualized as goal-directed and
concern-relevant. More conceptual and empirical work is
needed to settle such questions. For now, the balance of

3.2.4 (C) Coping potential
Whereas implication checks determine what the situation
entails for the subject’s concerns, coping-potential checks
assess the extent to which the subject is able to deal with,
or control, the implications, has the power to modify their
course, or to adjust to them. This stage includes checks of
control, power, and adjustment.
The control check evaluates the extent to which the situation and its consequences can be influenced by an agent.
When control is possible, the power check evaluates the
extent to which the subject themselves can influence the
situation, as opposed to external control. Sources of power
can be multiple – it includes the subject’s physical strength,
money, knowledge, and social attractiveness, for example.
According to Scherer (2001), the critical difference between
an appraisal that elicits anger and one that elicits fear is
whether the subject evaluates their power to manage the
threat as high (anger) or low (fear). The third coping-potential check is adjustment. It evaluates whether the agent may
adapt to the consequences of an event or learn to live with
them.
As already seen in the discussion of Lazarus’s secondary appraisal, coping potential can crucially modulate the
overall valence of an emotional episode. As in the earlier
example of Joanna’s joking response to an insult, a situation appraised as obstructive to a concern may fail to generate negative valence, or generate only attenuated negative
valence, if the subject appraises themselves as having the
power to modify it or the capacity to adapt to it. This can
give rise to mixed feelings (see above).
It is possible that the power check generates its own
proto-valence. The hypothesis is that appraising one’s power
as high produces a positive proto-valence, potentially associated with conscious feelings of empowerment, efficacy, or
competence, whereas appraising one’s power as low produces a negative proto-valence, potentially associated with
feelings of helplessness, inefficacy, or incompetence. This
hypothesis is defended, among others, by Shuman et al.
(2013) (see also Keltner et al. 2003).

rather than a genuinely generative role. In this respect, it
is more similar to the probability or urgency checks than to
the checks of intrinsic relevance or concern conduciveness.
That is, the power check may amplify or attenuate already
existing proto-valences, without itself producing a positive
or negative proto-valence.
Consider the case of anger. In a typical episode of anger,
the subject appraises the situation as obstructive to a concern
such as the need to be respected, which generates a negative
proto-valence. Appraising one’s power as high – the capacity to respond, sanction, or restore the situation – may modulate the valence arising from this concern obstructiveness.
The high power attenuates the negative proto-valence associated with the threat, limiting its affective impact. Power
thus appears as a means to achieve or protect concerns, and
it is in this sense that it shapes the overall valence of the
affective episode. On this interpretation, however, power
does not elicit its own proto-valence.
Here is an alternative way to formulate this idea. The
protection of one’s concerns is itself an instrumental concern (or, equivalently, an instrumental desire, see Bonard
2026b). When the subject appraises their power as high,
they appraise the situation as conducive to the protection
of their concern. This generates a positive proto-valence.
This proto-valence however, is not generated by the power
appraisal itself insofar as it is a function of the non-instrumental concern that is thus protected – in the last example,
the need to be respected.
If power did generate its own positive proto-valence,
then every episode of anger involving an appraisal of high
power that reaches consciousness should include a positively valenced dimension. As noted, Scherer (2001) argued
that every episode of anger is characterized by high power,
as do other appraisal theorists.8 It is, in their view, what fundamentally distinguishes anger from fear. But anger would
then be constitutively ambivalent: negative due to concern
frustration and positive due to perceived power. This prediction does not match the ordinary phenomenology of anger,
which is not, as such, a systematically mixed emotion. Few
episodes of anger, it seems, are cases of enjoyable anger.9

3.2.6 (D) Normative significance
Normative significance involves two checks: internal standards and external standards. The internal standards check
evaluates the extent to which an action or event conforms
to the subject’s self-ideals, in particular with respect to
self-image and ideals of excellence. The external standards
check assesses the compatibility of the action with norms,
expectations, and obligations perceived as upheld by a relevant social group, especially the moral norms of groups of
which the subject identifies as a member.
Shuman et al. (2013) propose that both checks directly
generate proto-valences, positive or negative, depending on
whether the situation is appraised as conforming to or violating the relevant normative standards. These proto-valences
are taken to be particularly important for the positive feelings of pride or admiration and for the negative feelings
of shame, guilt, embarrassment, contempt, or indignation
(Scherer 2001).
With respect to external standards, it seems clear, for
instance, that the negative valence of guilt derives, at least
in paradigmatic cases, from the subject’s appraisal that
they have violated norms accepted by their group, typically
moral norms (though exceptions may exist, such as survivor
guilt). Beyond moral codes, one can also imagine contempt
toward a customer in a high-end restaurant because they
fail to observe dress codes or table manners, thus violating
external standards.
In conjunction with other checks, these positive and
negative proto-valences can give rise to mixed feelings. A
familiar example is that of guilty pleasure, where pleasure
derives from intrinsic relevance or concern conduciveness,
but is negatively tinged by guilt arising from the incompatibility of the enjoyable activity with normative standards
(Goffin and Cova 2019).
A perhaps more interesting example – because it more
clearly qualifies as a mixed feeling in the narrowest sense,

rather than a mere superposition of distinct affective episodes – is cringe amusement from above (Sect. 2.2). When
a subject is confronted with a scene of social awkwardness,
provided the situation is not too serious, the situation may
be appraised as conducive to some of the subject’s concerns: its incongruity is entertaining or interesting, thereby
generating a positive proto-valence. At the same time, the
situation is appraised as violating social standards, such as
etiquette or norms of self-presentation, generating a negative proto-valence. Crucially, in cases of cringe amusement,
these two appraisals do not give rise to two distinct affective
states, but to a single feeling directed at a single object, in
which the pleasure of amusement is constitutively bound up
with the discomfort it elicits.
3.2.7 Potential challenges
At this stage, we wish to raise two conceptual challenges
regarding the evaluation of normative significance.
The first challenge lies in the difficulty to clearly distinguish internal from external standards. This is so for
two reasons. The first reason is that many internal standards plausibly originate from socialization. For example,
whether a subject feels pride or shame about their physical
appearance reflects what they consider important for their
self-image – i.e., their internal standards. Yet it is plausible
that these standards were internalized through social learning about what counts as a valued or devalued appearance
in their reference group (a form of affective social learning, Clément and Dukes, 2017). In this sense, an internal
standard ultimately derives from an internalized external
standard. The second reason is that, for external standards
to trigger emotion, the subject must have appropriated or
internalized them. Mere knowledge that a situation violates
group norms is not sufficient to cause a valenced response;
the norm must matter to the subject. For instance, the norm
that burping in a fine restaurant is impolite will only elicit
shame if the subject has internalized it. Once a social norm
is considered important and is internalized by the subject,
it becomes unclear in what sense it is still an external standard. Thus, for both these reasons, it is unclear whether
internal and external standards constitute distinct sources of
proto-valence.
The second challenge concerns distinguishing the evaluation of normative significance from concern conduciveness.
It can be formulated as such: normative standards are part of
a subject’s concerns and, thus, normative significance is just
a type of concern conduciveness evaluation, akin to evaluating whether a situation is conducive to one’s security or

The CPM offers three complementary responses to this
second challenge. First, normative significance may differ
in the type of concerns involved: concern conduciveness
pertains to personal goals, desires, and well-being, whereas
internal and external standards involve norms that go beyond
personal concerns, including moral and social norms.
However, while true, this is not the only possible way
of cutting the cake: concerns could equally be categorized
as instrumental vs. intrinsic, aesthetic vs. non-aesthetic, or
epistemic vs. non-epistemic. Why privilege personal vs.
non-personal concerns?
CPM’s second response is that a distinction can be made
at the level of cognitive architecture (Scherer 2001; Grandjean and Scherer 2008). Because it involves more demanding processes, the evaluation of normative significance is
thought to occur later – hundreds or tens of milliseconds
after the evaluation of implication – although, to our knowledge, this hypothesis has not been tested.
The problem with this response is that many evaluations
the CPM classifies under concern conduciveness may also
rely on distinct neural mechanisms. These evaluations could
therefore be distinguished based on cognitive architecture
as well (Sander 2025). For instance, Panksepp (1998) proposes at least seven subcortical neural systems underlying
emotions shared with other species, including those related
to sexual desire, play, care, and information seeking. If
true, there would be distinct neural mechanisms assessing
whether a situation favors or disfavors these types of concerns. Does this imply that concern conduciveness evaluations should be separated according to these mechanisms?
If not, why should normative significance evaluations be
treated differently?
CPM’s third response is that the evaluation of normative
significance is distinct because it is unique to our species,
or at least to great apes (Scherer 2001). Such evaluations
require sophisticated cognitive resources, such as the conceptual representation of norms, that are inaccessible to
most, and perhaps all, nonhuman animals. While previous checks suffice to differentiate anger, fear, sadness, or
joy, they cannot generate shame, guilt, contempt, admiration, pride, or indignation; emotions that may be unique to
humans. The additional ingredient required for these emotions is the evaluation of normative significance.
Although this response is probably the strongest of the
three, especially as it is reinforced by CPM’s previous two

4 Proto-valence, overall valence, and
consciousness
In this section, we briefly highlight a largely open question
concerning the possible relations between proto-valence,
overall valence, and consciousness.
Overall valence is understood as the general, phenomenally conscious valence of an affective episode, in which
positive and negative valences can mix, producing mixed
feelings. We proposed, following Shuman et al. (2013), that
proto-valences contribute or determine overall valence.
However, we left open what exactly this means. What is
the precise relation between proto- and overall valence? At
least four options exist, some compatible with each other
(Bonard 2025; Sect. 4.2).
1. Causal relation. Evaluations – and the proto-valences
they generate – cause the overall valence of the affective
episode. Proto-valences are then non-conscious states
that, cumulatively, produce the conscious overall affective tone. In this view, overall valence may either represent the evaluation outcomes (as in option 4, below) or
may simply be their causal result, without representing
them (akin to Huxley’s epiphenomenalism according to
Dennett (1991).

Valence, Mixed Feelings, and Appraisal Processes

2. Identity relation. Overall valence is identified with conscious access to the proto-valences themselves. For
example, feeling positive overall valence would simply
be feeling the proto-valence of concern-conduciveness
or intrinsic-relevance evaluation. The difficulty is that
appraisal theories usually assume evaluations are fast,
automatic, and largely non-conscious, making unconditional conscious access problematic.
3. Identity with attentional condition. This option is more
nuanced and avoids the above difficulty by holding
that the identity relation applies only when attention
is directed toward underlying evaluations. Typically,
attention during an affective episode is on the evaluated
situation (object, event, person), not on the evaluative
processes themselves. However, when attention can be
redirected – e.g., in reflective or introspective contexts
– proto-valences become phenomenally accessible and
constitute the experienced overall valence. This aligns
naturally with attention-based theories of consciousness
(e.g., Prinz 2012). The problem, here, is how to explain
overall valence when we don’t pay attention to the evaluative process.
4. Representational relation. Overall valence could correspond to a conscious representation of non-conscious
proto-valences. Conscious valence would then arise
from monitoring and integrating the outcomes of multiple appraisal processes, rather than from direct conscious
access to those processes themselves. This option shares
strengths and weaknesses with contemporary representational approaches to consciousness—including Global
Workspace, Higher-Order, and Attention-Schema theories—which differ in important respects but share the
idea that conscious experience depends on integrated
representations of underlying non-conscious processes
(for a review, see Graziano et al. 2020).
These options are not mutually exclusive. Different relations may operate depending on the type of affective episode, the subject’s attentional focus, or the level of reflective
complexity. Furthermore, they are not exhaustive: other
plausible relations could be proposed. The central challenge remains to account for how a plurality of heterogeneous evaluations yields a unified affective experience with
conscious valence, while allowing that the heterogeneous
evaluations are unconscious by default.
Although the CPM does not explicitly settle this issue,
it clearly commits to a causal relation: appraisal outcomes
generate changes across the other emotion components,
including the feeling component and its overall valence.
This causal relation should therefore be regarded as the
CPM’s minimal commitment. Beyond this minimal commitment, the CPM also lends support to a representational

relation. Grandjean, Sander, and Scherer (2008, 486), for
instance, describe subjective feeling as performing a monitoring function that integrates information about changes
across the other emotional components and builds an integrative conscious representation. We therefore take the CPM
to support a view on which conscious valence both results
causally from underlying appraisal processes and represents their integrated effects, while leaving open the precise
theory of consciousness through which this representation
becomes conscious.The four options above concern the
general relation between proto- and overall valence, but at
least two further questions remain, largely orthogonal to this
issue. The first question relates to the mechanisms by which
multiple proto-valences are integrated into a unified overall
valence. As noted by Shuman et al. (2013), integration can
follow diverse processes, from exhaustive weighted combinations of all available proto-valences to more frugal or
selective strategies, where some proto-valences are ignored
or hierarchized. These integration mechanisms determine
which proto-valences actually contribute to overall valence
and with what weight, without settling the ontological or
psychological nature of proto-valence. They are thus, in
principle, compatible with any of the four options above.
The second question, distinct from both the proto–overall relation and the integration mechanisms, concerns how
proto-valences manifest phenomenologically when producing mixed feelings. In other words, even if multiple protovalences contribute to overall valence, how is this plurality
experienced by the subject?
Following Colombetti (2005), two phenomenal models
of affective mixing can be distinguished. In the “duck–rabbit” model, named after a famous ambiguous image where
it is possible to discern either a duck or a rabbitt, the subject alternates between two distinct affective experiences or
apprehends them as co-present without merging them into a
single experience. The “sweet–sour sauce” model describes
cases where positive and negative valences are experienced
as unified within a single feeling, while remaining partially
phenomenally differentiable. These models can apply to
the phenomenological relation between proto-valences and
overall valences, if we grant that proto-valences can at least
sometimes be consciously perceived.
Two models can be added at each extreme of this duality.
The “Necker cube” model corresponds to strict phenomenal
alternation, where perceiving one proto-valence excludes
the other at any moment. Conversely, the “color mixing”
model describes experiences in which proto-valences are so
intimately blended that they become phenomenologically
indistinguishable, leaving only an irreducible global affective tone: the overall valence.
In sum, for the CPM or other appraisal theories to give
a complete account of mixed feelings, they must articulate

13

C. Bonard et al.

at least three distinct levels: (i) elicitation of proto-valences
by different evaluations, (ii) their integration into an overall
valence, and (iii) their phenomenal blending, as experienced
by the subject.

5 Conclusion
A central result of the preceding discussion is that appraisal
theory can explain mixed feelings without treating them
either as the mere co-occurrence of several conscious emotions or as states with a single net valence. The CPM decomposes the elicitation of affect into heterogeneous evaluative
processes whose outputs may make positive and negative
contributions to one affective episode. When these protovalences are integrated into overall valence and phenomenally blended, the result may be a conscious episode that is
structurally ambivalent (in its intentionality) yet experientially unified (in its phenomenology).
In order to summarize how appraisal theories, and the
CPM in particular, can plausibly account for mixed feelings,
let us give what we take to be plausible interpretations of
the examples with which we began (Sect. 2.2) using the five
types of proto-valences distinguished above (Sect. 3.2).
● The feeling of the sublime. A positive proto-valence
arises from aesthetic pleasure linked to the intrinsic relevance of the beautiful phenomenon, combined with a
negative proto-valence from appraisals of low power
and threat to safety (see Sect. 3.2. (B).
● Enjoyable anger. A negative proto-valence stems from
appraisals of obstructiveness to concerns such as the
need to be respected or from violations of normative
standards, while a positive proto-valence arises from appraisals of high power, potentially a power that is conducive to another concern, like self-assertion (see Sect.
3.2. (C).
● Cringe amusement. A positive proto-valence comes
from favorability to epistemic or playful interests, and a
negative one from the violation of normative standards
(see Sect. 3.2. (B).
● Musical melancholy. A positive proto-valence comes
from the intrinsic relevance of aesthetically pleasant
music while a negative proto-valence comes from appraisals related to a loss that is metaphorically perceived
in the music (Schaerlaeken et al. 2019; Bonard 2026c).
● The ambivalent departure mood. A positive proto-valence arises from favorable prospects and/or increased
power, and a negative one from the probable loss of relationships (see Sect. 3.2. (B).
● Pleasurable bodily pains. A negative proto-valence
derives from nociceptive intrinsic relevance, while a

13

positive one stems from perceived benefits to concerns,
like performance (see Sect. 3.2. (B).
In all these cases, mixed feelings are explained by the copresence of positive and negative proto-valences. These
proto-valences arise either from distinct appraisal checks
– intrinsic relevance or concern conduciveness, and, perhaps, power or normative standards – or they arise from a
single ambivalent check – e.g., a situation that is conducive
to some concerns and obstructive to others. These protovalences can result from the appraisal of the same situation
and be integrated, within the stream of consciousness, into a
single unified affective episode, constituting mixed feelings
(in the narrowest sense distinguished in Sect. 2.2).
Although five proto-valence sources are distinguished by
the CPM (Shuman et al. 2013), several challenges arise concerning these distinctions:
1. Intrinsic relevance vs. concern conduciveness. While
some argue all valence derives from concern conduciveness (Moors 2022; Chap. 7), the distinction is theoretically and empirically defensible and plausibly separates
two types of proto-valence.12
2. Power. Power may modulate concern-conduciveness
valence without generating its own proto-valence.
3. Internal vs. external standards. Internal standards often
reflect external standards, and only internalized external
standards trigger emotion, complicating the distinction.
4. Normative standards vs. concern conduciveness. Both
involve goal-oriented mechanisms; isolating normativestandard concerns may be arbitrary given other distinct
concerns.
In terms of future directions this research could take,
explaining mixed feelings requires distinguishing three
analytical levels: (i) the proto–overall valence relation
(causal, identity, attention-dependent, or metacognitive),
(ii) integration mechanisms of proto-valences into overall
valence (weighted, heuristic, or dynamic), and (iii) the phenomenal blending of proto-valences in conscious experience. Together, these analytical levels clarify the diversity
of valenced affective episodes and the complexity of affective mixing, while opening largely unexplored research
avenues.13

In sum, appraisal theory – and the CPM especially –
explains mixed feelings without multiplying emotions or
reducing ambivalence to a net valence. Instead, it provides
a structured and explanatorily fertile framework for understanding how positive and negative valence can be simultaneously generated, integrated, and experienced within a
single affective episode.
