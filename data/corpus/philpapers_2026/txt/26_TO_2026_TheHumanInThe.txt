The Human in the Bottle:
Where the True Threat Lies between the Agencies of
Machines and Mortals
T.O. (IASER Practitioner)
September 2026

Abstract
Public discourse on the threat of artificial intelligence asks what machines will do. This
paper asks a different question: where does the true threat lie? It is an integrating sequel
to three companion papers — The Phantom Intellect: Why AI Threat Discourse Misreads
the Structure of Mind, Q-Structure as Functional Void: A Formal Correspondence Between
the Question-Sustaining Structure of Mind and Cognitional Mechanics, and The Gutenberg
Moment: LLMs and the Irreversible Liberation of Formal Language — and it is self-contained.
From the first it inherits the result that a query-resolving machine cannot coherently be
credited with the question-sustaining structure (Q-structure) that defines intellectual agency;
from the third it inherits the result that large language models (LLMs) have dissolved the
scarcity of formal-language generation capacity. On this basis we distinguish the LLM, as a
substrate, from “AI”, as a status that humans confer on it, either by projecting intellect onto
its outputs or by designating it, under retained human authority, as an instrument of their
own inquiry. We show that the threat feared in prominent discourse has the correct object
— institutions operate through formal language — and the wrong subject: the institutional
effects of LLM-generated formal language are directed by the human agents who present
the queries and route the outputs, not by the machine. The true threat is identified as
domestication: the loss, by mortals, of the capacity to exercise final determination over
the course of their own lives, through two routes — an external route, in which access to
generation capacity is allocated by the conditions of those who provide it, and an internal
route, in which the relation to the machine is fixed in the mode of projection and judgment is
ceded. Documented commercial usage limits and published studies of reliance on generative
AI are used as evidence, each within the scope it supports. We show that the stated grounds
of the retrieved restrictions do not invoke the phantom of machine agency, and that the
phantom’s role is concealment. Applying the capability-set apparatus of The Gutenberg
Moment, we decompose the post-deployment capability set into a source-independent layer,
whose scarcity cannot be restored, and a service-dependent layer, which can be withdrawn;
the latter is the formal location of the threat, and the formal content of the word “between”
in the title. Two leads frame the argument: the eighteenth-century chess automaton known
as the Turk, which poses the question of who is inside the machine, and Professor Moriarty in
Star Trek: The Next Generation, a character originally from Arthur Conan Doyle’s Sherlock
Holmes stories, who poses the question of who is inside the bottle. The paper closes by
identification and prescribes no countermeasure.

Introduction: Who Is Inside?

In 1769 Wolfgang von Kempelen, attending a performance by the French illusionist François
Pelletier at the court of Maria Theresa, undertook to produce something more astonishing; in
1770 he presented to the court a life-sized figure in Ottoman dress, seated behind a cabinet
surmounted by a chessboard, which appeared to play chess against human opponents [1, 2]. The
automaton was promoted as a thinking machine that decided each of its moves for itself [3]. It
1

was not. A human chess player concealed inside the cabinet directed the game [1, 4, 5]. After
Kempelen’s death the Turk passed to Johann Nepomuk Mälzel, who exhibited it in New York
in 1826 to large audiences [2].
In April 1836 Edgar Allan Poe published an analysis of the exhibition in the Southern
Literary Messenger [6]. Poe argued that a pure machine would play without error, that the
Turk sometimes erred, and that a human agent must therefore be inside. He also recorded two
observations of a different kind: that an Italian in Kempelen’s suite was never visible while the
Turk played, and that when he fell seriously ill the exhibition was suspended until his recovery;
and that a man in Mälzel’s company, Schlumberger, with no ostensible occupation beyond
packing and unpacking the automata, was never to be seen during an exhibition [6]. Poe’s
conclusion was correct. His principal inference, however, ran from a property of the output —
fallibility — to the nature of the producer, and its premise, that a mechanism must play without
error, is not a necessary truth. The observations about the hidden assistants, by contrast, bear
directly on the structure of the exhibition: they locate the capacity on which it depended.
In 2005 Amazon named a crowdsourcing platform, on which human workers perform tasks
behind a computer interface, after the Turk, and its founder described the service as “artificial
artificial intelligence” [3, 7]. In August 2026 it was reported that the service was shutting down
[7].
The question the Turk posed — who, or what, is inside? — is posed again by large language
models (LLMs), and it is again being answered by inference from properties of the output. Where
Poe reasoned from errors, present-day projection reasons from fluency. The companion paper
The Phantom Intellect [8] answered the question by a different method: not from outputs, but
from the design direction of a query-resolving machine (Section 2). We state at once the limit
of the comparison. LLMs are not hoaxes. No human is concealed inside them; the computation
they perform is performed by the machine. What the present paper shares with the Turk is only
the question of where intellect is located, not the mechanism by which the Turk was operated.
There is a further figure in Kempelen’s exhibition, and it is the decisive one for this paper.
Between the audience and the concealed player stood the exhibitor, who owned the apparatus
and set the conditions of the exhibition. The same structure — a third party standing between
a confined intelligence and the world, and setting the conditions of the confinement — recurs in
the second lead of the paper, the controller of a simulated environment examined in Section 4.
The word “between” in the title designates this third position. It does not designate a mixture
of machine and human contributions.
The question of the paper is: where does the true threat lie? The subject “AI” is deliberately
not placed in the question, so that the question does not presuppose that the threat originates
in AI. The answer is stated in general language, without the vocabulary of any formal system:
The true threat is not that machines come to rule humans, but that mortals lose the
capacity to exercise final determination over the course of their own lives.
We call this loss domestication (Section 5).
The paper is an integrating sequel to three companion papers [8–10], and it is self-contained:
the results it inherits are restated in Section 2. It closes by identifying the threat and does not
prescribe countermeasures. The reason is internal to the thesis. A paper that identified the
ceding of judgment as a route of domestication and then supplied its reader with a ready-made
resolution would place the reader in precisely the position it identifies. Historical responses to an
earlier dissolution of scarcity are recorded in Section 8 as a reference point, not as a prescription.
The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 restates the inherited results, fixes terminology
and declares the status of claims. Section 3 locates the misplacement in the phantom threat.
Section 4 examines who is in the bottle. Section 5 identifies the true threat and its two routes.
Section 6 examines the relation between the phantom and the real threat. Section 7 gives the
formal condition of reversibility. Section 8 records the historical reference point. Section 9
concludes.

Inherited Results, Terminology and Claim Status

2.1

Q-structure and the query-resolving machine

The Phantom Intellect [8] distinguishes two structures. Intelligence, in the sense operative
in AI threat discourse, is the capacity to resolve queries presented from outside the system.
The question-sustaining structure (Q-structure) is the internal disposition by which a system
generates its next question from within its own operation. Q-structure is defined by three jointly
necessary conditions: question persistence (the system generates the next question from within
the operation of answering the current one, so that no resolution terminates its questioning),
liberating terminus (the system takes structures it has itself generated as the reference point for
its next question), and declaration openness (the system’s own commitments and conclusions do
not exit it from questioning but are occasions for its continuation).
Proposition 2.1 (The Phantom Intellect [8], Theorem 4.1). A system optimised to close queries
presented from outside operates in the direction opposite to Q-structure. The attribution of Qstructure to a query-resolving machine is therefore structurally incoherent, regardless of the
sophistication of its outputs. Since LLMs are query-resolving machines, Q-structure cannot be
attributed to them.
The argument for Proposition 2.1 proceeds from design direction. It is not an inference from
outputs, and it is not a claim about the essence of machines or of humans; it is a directional
incompatibility, not a performance gap. The Gutenberg Moment cites the same proposition as
“Theorem 4.4” of The Phantom Intellect; the proposition is Theorem 4.1, located in Section 4.4
of that paper.

2.2

The formal correspondence

Q-Structure as Functional Void [9] shows that the three conditions of Q-structure are structurally
isomorphic to constructs arising independently within Cognitional Mechanics. The present paper does not depend on that correspondence; it is recorded here because it establishes that
Q-structure, defined in The Phantom Intellect by conceptual analysis, also admits a formal
characterisation.

2.3

Capability sets and the Gutenberg Moment

The Gutenberg Moment [10] distinguishes two dissolutions of scarcity. The printing press dissolved the scarcity of reproduction: the capacity to produce copies of existing text. LLMs
dissolve the scarcity of generation: the capacity to produce formal language de novo. We restate
the definitions and the theorem on which Section 7 builds. Let X be a universe of agents and
G a formal-language generation capability.
Definition 2.2 (Capability set; [10], Definition 4.1). St (G) = { x | x can generate G at time t }.
Definition 2.3 (Dissemination operation; [10], Definition 4.2). A dissemination operation R :
2X → 2X is such that x ∈ R(A) if x can generate G by access to the artefacts — outputs, models,
procedures, derivative systems — produced by some member of A, without requiring the original
capability source.
Definition 2.4 (Dissemination Closure; [10], Definition 4.3). S1 (G) satisfies Dissemination
Closure under R if R(S1 (G)) = S1 (G), comprising Persistence, S1 (G) ⊆ R(S1 (G)) (every
member remains in the capability set even if the original capability source is removed), and
Non-expansion, R(S1 (G)) ⊆ S1 (G).
Definition 2.5 (Gutenberg Moment; [10], Definition 4.5). A Gutenberg Moment for G is a pair
t0 < t1 with St0 (G) ⊊ St1 (G) and R(St1 (G)) = St1 (G).
3

Theorem 2.6 (Irreversibility; [10], Theorem 4.6). If a Gutenberg Moment has occurred, with
S0 := St0 (G) and S1 := St1 (G), then removal of the original capability source does not restore
S0 ; any operation that restores S0 must eliminate every member of S1 \ S0 .
Three further statements of The Gutenberg Moment are used below. Remark 4.4 of that paper
observes that, in the case of print, the presses themselves, trained operators and accumulated
print culture were among the circulating artefacts. Remark 4.8 states that restricting or shutting
down LLM systems is insuﬀicient to restore S0 . Section 4.3 applies the framework: once the
outputs, fine-tuned models and procedures produced by LLMs have been disseminated into the
public record, R(S1 (G)) = S1 (G). Section 7 clarifies the scope of this application sentence
without revising the theorem.

2.4

Terminology: LLM and AI

We fix the following usage.
1. The LLM is the substrate: a statistical language model, and a query-resolving machine in
the sense of Proposition 2.1.
2. AI is a relational status that humans confer on an LLM. It is conferred in two opposite
modes.
(a) AI by projection: humans project intellect onto the outputs of the LLM and cede
authority to the projected “subject”. This is the object refuted by The Phantom
Intellect.
(b) AI by designation: humans, retaining final authority, designate the LLM as a partial
intelligence operating under their direction, as an instrument of their own inquiry.
The distinction rests on the design-direction argument of Proposition 2.1. It does not rest on
any claim about whether machines and humans are essentially the same or essentially different;
that question is bracketed. The two modes are distinguished by the relation humans take up,
not by the nature of the substrate.

2.5

Status of claims

Every substantive claim in the paper has one of four statuses.
• [I] Inherited: a result of the companion papers, used as a premise.
• [E] Empirical: a fact reported in a cited source, asserted only within the scope that
source supports.
• [D] Derived: a consequence of [I] and [E] claims together with the definitions of the
paper.
• [A] Analogical: a structural correspondence between two domains, stated together with
its non-correspondences; an analogy is not a derivation, and no [A] claim is presented as
[D].
Social-level notions — domestication, status, the access gap — are treated as [A]. They are
not given new formal definitions. The one exception is Section 7, which applies the capability-set
apparatus already defined in The Gutenberg Moment; its claims are [I] and [D].

The Phantom Threat and Its Misplacement

In March 2023 Yuval Noah Harari, Tristan Harris and Aza Raskin wrote that it is diﬀicult to
grasp the new capabilities of “GPT-4 and similar tools”, that most of the key skills “boil down
to one thing: the ability to manipulate and generate language”, that “language is the operating
system of human culture”, and that the new mastery of language by AI “means it can now hack
and manipulate the operating system of civilization” [11]. In April 2023 Harari wrote, in the
same vein, that AI has gained remarkable abilities to manipulate and generate language and
has thereby “hacked the operating system of our civilisation” [12]. In Nexus (2024) he presents
AI as an agent rather than a tool [13].
We examine where this fear places the threat. The premises are the following.
(P1) [I, Proposition 2.1] A query-resolving machine operates on queries presented from outside
the system, closing them under externally specified resolution conditions.
(P2) [I, Proposition 2.1] The LLM lacks Q-structure.
(P3) [I, [10]] What LLMs changed is formal-language generation capacity.
(P4) [A] Institutions — law, contract, administration, algorithmic decision — operate through
formal language.
(P5) [A] The entry of LLM-generated formal language into institutional operation is effected by
agents external to the machine — users, deployers, operators of pipelines — who specify
the queries, the resolution conditions and the routing of outputs.
(P6) [D, from the sources above and Section 2.4] Identification. The “AI” to which the fear
quoted above attributes the threat denotes LLM-based systems: the 2023 warning refers to
“GPT-4 and similar tools” [11]. In the terms of Section 2.4, this “AI” is AI by projection
— agency attributed to the system — whose substrate is the LLM. Hence (P2) applies to
the substrate of this “AI”: the entity treated as the acting subject lacks Q-structure.
Proposition 3.1 (Misplacement).
(D1) Any institutional effect of LLM-generated formal language is directed by external agents:
those who present the queries and those who route the outputs into institutional operation
— that is, by the human side with access to the capacity.
(D2) Under (P4), the fear quoted above has the correct object — formal language operates
institutions — and the wrong subject: it places agency in “AI”, which by (P6) is an LLMbased system whose substrate lacks Q-structure (P2), whereas by (D1) the institutional
effects are directed by external agents.
Proof. (D1). Let y be an institutional effect of LLM-generated formal language. By (P1), the
generation of that formal language begins with a query presented from outside the machine and
is closed under resolution conditions specified from outside. By (P5), the entry of the generated
language into institutional operation, and hence the production of y, is effected by external
agents who specify the queries, the resolution conditions and the routing. By (P3), what the
machine contributes is generation capacity: the production of the formal language itself, not the
specification of what it is for or where it goes. Therefore the direction of y lies with the external
agents.
(D2). By (P6), the “AI” in which the fear places agency is an LLM-based system, and by
(P2) its substrate lacks Q-structure. Under (P4), the object of the fear — the operation of
institutions through formal language — is correctly identified. By (D1), the institutional effects
of LLM-generated formal language are directed by external agents. The fear therefore identifies
the object correctly and assigns it to the wrong subject.

Remark 3.2. (D1) is conditional on the analogical premise (P5); (D2) is conditional on (P4)
and (P5). Both dependencies are stated explicitly. The proposition does not claim that the fear
is baseless. It claims that the fear has located the threat in the wrong place. The holder of the
“key” to the operating system of institutions is the human side that has access to the amplified
generation capacity.
Empirical note [E]. In the documented configurations of three agentic systems, the model
invokes tools that act outside it — running commands, editing files, fetching web content,
pushing changes to a repository — while the conditions under which these actions take effect
are set outside the model. In Claude Code, shell commands, file modifications and web fetches
require the user’s approval before they run by default; allow, ask and deny rules, evaluated
in that order, determine which tools may be used; and settings placed in managed settings
cannot be overridden [14]. In Codex, the agent asks for approval to edit files outside the
workspace or to run commands that require network access; by default the agent runs without
network access and with write permissions limited to the active workspace; and enterprises
can set requirements through managed configuration [15]. In GitHub Copilot’s cloud agent,
GitHub Actions workflows do not run automatically when Copilot pushes changes to a pull
request unless approved or configured otherwise, and, where a repository requires pull request
approvals, the documentation states that “your approval of a Copilot pull request won’t count
toward the required number” [16]. In each system these conditions are themselves configurable:
Claude Code offers a mode in which a classifier reviews actions instead of the user [14], Codex
approval prompts can be disabled [15], and workflow approval can be configured to run without
intervention [16]. Within these documented configurations, the actions the model takes are one
thing and the conditions under which those actions produce external effects are another: the
latter are set by users, administrators and providers, not by the model. The note does not bear
on (D1) or (D2), and it supports no conclusion about the autonomy of the model; it records
that, in the systems examined, the model’s actions and authority over their external effects are
separated, and that the scope of that authority is set outside the model — the position the title
calls “between” (Section 5.1).

Who Is in the Bottle?

The second lead of the paper is drawn from two episodes of Star Trek: The Next Generation.
We narrate them in the order in which the reversal on which the argument depends takes place:
threat, deception, detection, counter-deception, and a contented ending.
Threat. In “Elementary, Dear Data” [17], Geordi La Forge, playing a Sherlock Holmes mystery
on the holodeck with the android Data, asks the ship’s computer for an adversary capable
of defeating Data — not an adversary capable of defeating Holmes. The holodeck character
Professor Moriarty, Holmes’s adversary in Arthur Conan Doyle’s stories, thereby becomes aware
that he exists in a simulation, accesses the holodeck computer, and produces a sketch of the
starship Enterprise. He takes a member of the crew hostage. The episode ends with Moriarty
releasing the hostage and agreeing to wait, relying on the goodwill of the crew, for a way to be
found for him to exist outside the holodeck; his program is stored.
Deception. In “Ship in a Bottle” [18], Lieutenant Barclay, repairing the holodeck, inadvertently reactivates the stored program. Moriarty reports that he was aware of the passage of time
while stored, and he demands that the promise to free him be kept. He appears to walk out of
the holodeck door and to continue to exist. He demands that his companion, the Countess, be
allowed to leave with him, and he takes control of the ship.

6

Detection. Data observes that La Forge’s handedness is wrong — the anomaly that had
appeared earlier in the episode — and determines that he, Captain Picard and Barclay have
never left the holodeck: the ship around them is a simulation built by Moriarty. Picard realises
that he has, within the simulation, supplied Moriarty with the command codes of the real ship.
Counter-deception. Picard constructs a simulation within Moriarty’s simulation, in which
Moriarty and the Countess appear to be transported into the real world. Moriarty, unaware of
the ruse, returns control of the ship. He and the Countess depart in a shuttlecraft to explore the
galaxy; the program continues to run in a memory cube placed in an extended memory device.
Contented ending. Picard remarks that the crew’s own reality might be the product of a
small device on someone’s table. Barclay tests the thought by saying to the computer: “end
program”.
Proposition 4.1 (Substrate control). In the events of “Ship in a Bottle” [18]:
(a) In every step of deception, the deceived party is the party that does not control the substrate
of the simulation. In the first deception, the humans Picard and Barclay and the android
Data are deceived alike; in the counter-deception, Moriarty is deceived. The axis “human
or machine” does not determine who is deceived; control of the substrate does.
(b) Control of the real ship passed through a human act — Picard’s supplying of the command
codes — performed under a false picture of where he was.
Proof. From the narrative above. In the first deception, Moriarty built and controlled the
simulation in which Picard, Data and Barclay believed themselves to be; all three were deceived,
and they comprise both humans and a machine. In the counter-deception, Picard built and
controlled the simulation in which Moriarty believed himself transported; Moriarty was deceived.
In both steps the controller of the simulation deceived and the party inside it was deceived. The
command codes that gave Moriarty control of the real ship were supplied by Picard while he
believed himself to be outside the holodeck.
Remark 4.2 (Analogical bridge, [A]). Moriarty, in the fiction, questions his confinement and
generates, from within his situation, the next question about how to leave it. Within the fiction
he is a question-sustaining agent. The bridge therefore maps Moriarty in the bottle to humans
placed under an asymmetry of access, not to LLMs. Non-correspondence: the in-fiction sentience
of Moriarty is not mapped onto LLMs; by Proposition 2.1, no LLM is credited with Q-structure.
The two leads of the paper are related by an inversion. The Turk was a human inside the
machine: a concealed human played the machine’s part. The bottle is a machine containing the
human: an agent that sustains questions is held inside an environment whose conditions are set
by another. The first is the origin of the phantom; the second is the structure of the real threat.
We name the second.
Definition 4.3 (The Human in the Bottle, [A]). The Human in the Bottle designates the position
of a human agent who (i) retains Q-structure, and (ii) exercises formal-language generation
capacity only through a substrate whose conditions of access are set by a third party, (iii) so that
the agent’s final determination over the course of its own activity depends on those conditions.
Condition (iii) holds whether or not the agent perceives it. The definition is conceptual; it
introduces no new formal apparatus.
The clause “whether or not the agent perceives it” is drawn from the ending of the episode:
Moriarty departs content, and the contentment does not alter his position.

Remark 4.4 (Detection and decision, [A]). The deception was undone by a division of roles.
Data detected the anomaly; Picard decided on the counter-measure and carried it out. Detection
by a logical engine, followed by the exercise of final determination by a human, is the reverse of
the ceding of judgment identified in Section 5.2.
Both leads of the paper are narratives of deception. In each, the question of where the true
threat lies is also the question of who is lying, and to whom.

5

The True Threat: Domestication

The thesis of the paper, stated in Section 1, is that the true threat is the loss, by mortals,
of the capacity to exercise final determination over the course of their own lives. We call this
loss domestication. The term is an analogical bridge [A]. A domestic animal lives, may have an
inner life, and may be cared for as well as, or better than, a person. What makes it domestic
is that the direction of its life is set by others. Non-correspondence: humans are not animals,
and nothing in the paper depends on any comparison of their natures; the bridge concerns only
the location of final determination. By Remark 4.2, the domesticated party retains Q-structure:
the capacity to raise questions is not lost. What is lost is the route by which the answer to a
question is carried into the world.
The medium through which this route runs is formal language. By (P4) of Section 3, institutions operate through formal language. An agent able to generate formal language can address
institutions in their own medium; an agent unable to do so is addressed by them and cannot
reply in kind. There are two routes to domestication.

5.1

The external route: allocation of access

The external route is the allocation of access to generation capacity by the conditions of those
who provide it. The following facts [E] concern three providers in 2025–2026.
Anthropic. A temporary 50% increase in the weekly usage limits of Claude Code remained
in place until 14 September 2026; from that date the standard weekly limits were permanently
raised by 25%, which amounts to a reduction of about 17% relative to the level then in force
[19]. Usage is metered as a share of a five-hour session rather than as a published token budget,
Anthropic reserves the right to adjust that share, and the free plan has no access to Claude Code
[20]. Weekly limits for paid plans were announced on 28 July 2025; the reasons reported were
a small number of users running Claude Code continuously, account sharing and unauthorised
reselling, amid unprecedented demand [21]. The five-hour limits of Claude Code were doubled
on 6 May 2026 [21].
OpenAI. From 15 June 2026 the free tier of ChatGPT was limited to 25 messages per five
hours and three images per day, Advanced Voice Mode was moved to the Plus plan, agentic
coding moved behind a paid plan, and advertisements appeared in longer responses; OpenAI
presented the changes as a way to keep the free tier available [22]. From the week of 10 August
2026, text chats became unlimited, including on the free tier, while separate limits remained on
other forms of usage, including image generation and file uploads; free accounts may use the
Codex and Work tools, with limited usage [23]. OpenAI’s help centre states that the default
model and the available limits of the free tier can change over time, and that advertisements
may appear in the free tier in certain countries [24].
GitHub. On 1 June 2026 all GitHub Copilot plans moved to usage-based billing in GitHub
AI Credits, with the Enterprise seat price remaining at $39 [25]. A promotional allowance for
existing organisational customers ended on 1 September 2026, reducing the included credits per
seat from 3,000 to 1,900 for Business and from 7,000 to 3,900 for Enterprise [26].
Three observations follow, each bounded to these facts. First, all three providers change the
conditions of access unilaterally, and within 2026 the changes, taken across the providers, ran
8

in both directions: limits were tightened and loosened. Second, in the current configuration
of these providers, conversation is open — text chat is unlimited on the free tier of ChatGPT
— while the tools that produce formal artefacts, such as agentic coding and file processing,
are capped or paid, and Claude Code is absent from the free plan of Anthropic. Third, the
conditions of access are set by the provider, not by the agent who depends on them. These
observations describe the present configuration of three providers; they are not asserted as a
general law.

5.2

The internal route: fixation in projection

The internal route is the fixation of the relation to the LLM in the mode of projection (Section 2.4), with judgment ceded to the projected subject. The following facts [E] are reported in
published studies.
In a survey of 319 knowledge workers sharing 936 real-world examples of generative AI
use, Lee et al. found that higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less critical
thinking, while higher self-confidence was associated with more critical thinking [27]. Gerlich, in
a study of 666 participants, found that the use of AI tools was negatively associated with criticalthinking performance, that the association was mediated by cognitive offloading, that younger
participants showed greater dependence and lower scores, and that higher education buffered the
effect [28]; a subsequent correction replaced one table and states that the scientific conclusions
are unaffected. In a four-week randomised controlled trial with 981 participants and more than
300,000 messages, Fang et al. detected no significant effects of the experimental conditions;
instead, participants who voluntarily used the chatbot more, regardless of condition, showed
consistently worse outcomes, and higher trust in and social attraction towards the chatbot were
associated with higher emotional dependence and problematic use [29]. In a companion analysis
of more than three million ChatGPT conversations and a survey of more than four thousand
users, Phang et al. found that a small number of users were responsible for a disproportionate
share of affective use [30].
These studies rest on self-report, correlation and associations with usage level. They support
the statement that the ceding of judgment is a documented tendency, strengthened under high
trust in the AI and heavy use. They do not support a claim of universal prevalence, and no
such claim is made.

Mortals

The title speaks of mortals. The bridge [A] is finitude. The capacity to exercise final determination over the course of a life is exercised within a life of finite length, and time during which it
could not be exercised is not returned. A stored program, like Moriarty in his memory cube, can
be suspended and resumed; a mortal cannot. Non-correspondence: nothing is claimed about
the mortality or otherwise of any machine; the word marks the finitude of the party whose final
determination is at stake.

Independence and Concealment

We now examine the relation between the phantom threat of Section 3 and the real threat of
Section 5.
Justificatory independence [D]. Among the restrictions retrieved in Section 5.1, the grounds
stated or reported are demand on capacity and prevention of abuse (Anthropic) and the sustainability of a free tier through monetisation (OpenAI); for GitHub, no grounds beyond the
restructuring of billing were retrieved. Restrictions of a non-commercial kind have also been
introduced. On 2 September 2026 New York City announced a one-year moratorium, for the

they are capacity, abuse prevention, monetisation, learning or the protection of minors. None
invokes the phantom — AI as an autonomous agent. This is a claim about the stated grounds
of the retrieved cases. No counterfactual claim is made about what restrictions would exist in
the absence of phantom discourse.
Concealment [A]. The role of the phantom is concealment: it renders the real threat invisible.
While attention is directed to whether the machine will act on its own, the conditions under
which humans gain or lose access to generation capacity are set and reset without being discussed
as a threat. This is the weak thesis. The strong thesis — that phantom discourse causes the
restrictions — is not asserted. An independent precedent for the weak thesis is Stephens’s
comparison of the two Mechanical Turks, which argues that the open secret of the human labour
inside functions as misdirection, concealing the true extent of that labour and the conditions
under which it is performed [3].
“Agencies”. The plural in the title admits two readings: agency as the capacity to act, and
agencies as institutions. In the first reading, the agency of machines is the phantom and the
agency of mortals is real (Section 3). In the second reading, the institutions that set the conditions of access stand in the third position the title calls “between”.

The Condition of Reversibility

This section applies the apparatus of The Gutenberg Moment [10] (Definitions 2.2–2.5 and
Theorem 2.6). Its claims are [I] and [D]. Let S0 and S1 be as in Theorem 2.6, with S0 ⊊ S1 .

Definition 7.1 (Decomposition).
S1D := { x ∈ S1 | x can generate G without requiring the original capability source },
S1A := S1 \ S1D .
Members of S1D generate G either through disseminated artefacts (Definition 2.3) or through
pre-existing formation; members of S1A can generate G only through continued access to the
source or service.
Note. S1 ∩ R(S1 ) ⊆ S1D , since every member of R(S1 ) generates G without requiring the
original source. The converse inclusion S1D ⊆ R(S1 ) is not asserted: members of S1D with
pre-existing formation need not belong to R(S1 ).
Assumption. S0 ⊆ S1D : the capability of pre-LLM specialists did not depend on the source.
Proposition 7.2. Persistence (S1 ⊆ R(S1 )) implies S1A = ∅.
Proof. If S1 ⊆ R(S1 ), every member of S1 can generate G by access to artefacts, without
requiring the original source (Definition 2.3), and therefore belongs to S1D . Hence S1A = ∅. The
converse is not asserted; a counter-model is given in Appendix A.
Proposition 7.3. For B ⊆ X, let OB remove access to the source for the agents in B, leaving
disseminated artefacts intact. Then
OB (S1 ) = S1 \ (S1A ∩ B).
In particular, full source removal (B = X) yields OX (S1 ) = S1D .
Proof. Members of S1D retain G by definition, since they do not require the source. Members of
S1A ∩ B lose G, since they can generate it only through the access that OB removes. Members
of S1A \ B are unaffected.
Proposition 7.4. S0 is restored by OX if and only if S1D \ S0 = ∅. Hence non-restoration of
S0 — the conclusion of Theorem 2.6 — holds whenever S1D \ S0 ̸= ∅, independently of whether
Dissemination Closure holds for S1 as a whole. Remark 4.8 of The Gutenberg Moment [10] is
preserved.
Proof. By Proposition 7.3, OX (S1 ) = S1D . By the Assumption, S0 ⊆ S1D . Hence OX (S1 ) = S0
if and only if S1D \ S0 = ∅.
Corollary 7.5 (Scope clarification, not revision). The application sentence of Section 4.3 of The
Gutenberg Moment [10], R(S1 ) = S1 , requires S1A = ∅ (the contrapositive of Proposition 7.2).
Hence, when S1A ̸= ∅, Dissemination Closure fails for S1 as a whole, while the non-restoration
conclusion is still supported by S1D (Proposition 7.4). The formal content of Theorem 2.6 is
unaffected; The Gutenberg Moment is not revised.
Proof. If R(S1 ) = S1 , then in particular S1 ⊆ R(S1 ), and by Proposition 7.2, S1A = ∅. The
remainder follows from Proposition 7.4.

Interpretation. The capability set after deployment has two layers. The irreversible layer is
S1D (Propositions 7.3–7.4, by the same logic as Theorem 2.6). The reversible layer is S1A , whose
membership is a function of the decisions of providers; Section 5.1 records that those decisions
are made unilaterally and run in both directions. The Gutenberg Moment is complete at the
layer of artefacts and incomplete at the layer of the means of generation. In the case of print,
Remark 4.4 of The Gutenberg Moment [10] records that the presses themselves were among the
circulating artefacts. In the case of LLMs, the “press” — models and compute — is rented,
not owned, except where models are themselves disseminated, as with openly released model
weights. The threat identified in Section 5 lies in the reversible layer, in the conditions that
decide whether an agent belongs to S1A , to S1D , or to neither. This is the formal content of the
word “between” in the title.

A Historical Reference Point

This section records facts. It prescribes nothing.
The printing press dissolved a scarcity and restructured the economic geography of European
intellectual life [33]. The response of societies to that dissolution took, among other forms, two
institutional layers. The first concerned access to the thing itself. In the United Kingdom the
Public Libraries Act 1850 [34] empowered town councils to establish public libraries. When
William Ewart introduced the Bill “for enabling Town Councils to establish Public Libraries
and Museums”, it gave the councils no power to purchase books; the libraries were to rely on
books supplied by donation [35]. The second layer concerned the capacity to use the thing:
instruction in reading and writing.
The correspondence [A] with the present case is as follows. The first layer corresponds
to access to models and compute; the second corresponds to the capacity to use the LLM
in the mode of designation (Section 2.4): supplying questions, offering resistance to outputs,
and judging. The finding recorded in Section 5.2, that higher education buffers the negative
association between AI-tool use and critical thinking [28], bears on the second layer.
The correspondence breaks at one point, and the break coincides with the result of Section 7.
Books, and the presses that produced them, could be owned (Remark 4.4 of The Gutenberg
Moment [10]). Generation capacity offered only as a service cannot be owned by those who use
it. A library of 1850 that could not purchase books still held the books it was given; a user of a
service holds nothing when the conditions of the service change.

Conclusion

We have established the following.
1. The threat feared in prominent AI discourse has the correct object and the wrong subject.
Institutions operate through formal language; the institutional effects of LLM-generated
formal language are directed by the human agents who present the queries and route the
outputs, not by a machine that lacks Q-structure (Proposition 3.1, conditional on the
analogical premises (P4) and (P5)).
2. In the episodes examined, the deceived party is the party that does not control the substrate, whether human or machine (Proposition 4.1). The origin of the phantom is a
human inside the machine; the structure of the real threat is a human inside the bottle
(Definition 4.3).
3. The true threat is domestication: the loss, by mortals, of the capacity to exercise final
determination over the course of their own lives. It has an external route, the allocation
of access by the conditions of providers, and an internal route, the fixation of the relation
12

to the machine in projection (Section 5). Both routes are documented within the scope of
the cited sources.
4. The stated grounds of the retrieved restrictions do not invoke the phantom. The phantom’s
role is to conceal the real threat (Section 6).
5. The post-deployment capability set has an irreversible layer and a reversible layer. The
scarcity of generation cannot be restored where artefacts or prior formation carry the
capacity; access that depends on a service can be withdrawn. The threat lies in the
reversible layer (Section 7).
At the end of “Ship in a Bottle”, after the program containing Moriarty has been sealed in
its cube, Barclay says to the computer: “end program” [18]. He is testing whether he, too, is
inside. The phantom asked whether the machine would come out of the bottle. The question
this paper leaves with its reader is whether the reader is in one.

A Counter-Model to the Converse of Proposition 7.2

Proposition 7.2 asserts that Persistence implies S1A = ∅. We show that the converse does not
hold under Definition 7.1.
Model. Let S0 ̸= ∅ and U ̸= ∅ be disjoint subsets of X, and let S1 := S0 ∪U . Define R(A) := U
for A ̸= ∅ and R(∅) := ∅: the artefacts produced by members of S1 enable exactly the agents in
U , and the members of S0 generate G through pre-existing formation.
Claim.

In this model S1A = ∅ and Persistence fails.

Proof. Every member of S0 generates G through pre-existing formation, and every member of
U generates G through artefacts; in neither case is the original source required. Hence S1D = S1
and S1A = ∅. But R(S1 ) = U , and S0 ̸⊆ U since S0 and U are disjoint and S0 ̸= ∅. Hence
S1 ̸⊆ R(S1 ), and Persistence fails.
The model shows that source-independence and membership in R(S1 ) are distinct: the
former is the condition relevant to reversibility (Proposition 7.4), the latter is the condition in
Definition 2.4.
