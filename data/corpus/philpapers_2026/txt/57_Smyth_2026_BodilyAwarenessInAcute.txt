Bodily awareness in acute fatigue: the corporealization of
the minimal performative body
Daniel Reid Smyth1
Accepted: 16 September 2026
© The Author(s) 2026

Abstract
Knowing when one feels fatigued, and not just tired or expending effort, matters.
When fatigue is correctly detected, physician-patient misunderstandings can be
avoided (Long et al., 2022), occupational safety concerns can be addressed (Van
der Linden, 2011), and injury as well as loss of well-being can be reduced in athletic contexts (Saw et al., 2016). Although existing empirical definitions of acute
fatigue include feeling the need to rest and a mismatch between expended effort
and actual performance (Behrens et al., 2023; Skau et al., 2021), the present article
offers an applied phenomenology to enrich such operational definitions. It does so
by building on Katherine Morris’s study (2019) and suggests that acute fatigue involves a disruptive layering of multiple modalities of bodily awareness. The present
work introduces these modalities (the transparent, performative, lived, and material
bodies) before outlining their interaction during what Thomas Fuchs calls corporealization (2002; 2005b). Corporealization during acute fatigue is then analysed as a
twofold disruption, whereby what I call the minimal performative body both feels
its movement as wrong and encounters the material body. Appealing to this aspect
of acute fatigue’s phenomenology, I argue, helps to differentiate it from experiences
of tiredness, on the one hand, and chronic fatigue and exhaustion, on the other.

1 Introduction
Research on the semantics of fatigue indicates that language users consider it a
distinct concept (Long et al., 2022; Pigeon et al., 2003). Most recently, Long et al.
(2022) applied a semantic differential technique to four sleep-related terms: tiredness,
sleepiness, drowsiness, and fatigue. Using a Likert-scaled questionnaire, they plotted
responses along “a multidimensional semantic space” according to the independent
dimensions of evaluation, potency, and activity. The evaluative dimension designates
how positively or negatively appraised a given sensation is. Potency refers to its
impact (e.g., how heavy, strong, sudden), and activity refers, for example, to how
involuntary or voluntary the sensation feels (Long et al., 2022, p. 1236). Fatigue was
found to have a significantly higher negative appraisal than the other three terms, and
to a lesser extent, a higher degree of potency and activity. They consequently hypothesized that the higher negative connotations of fatigue may be due to its association with “undesirable health states” (p. 1239). Further studies on subjective reports
amongst marathon runners confirm that tiredness is often positively appraised and
can indicate a productive training session, whereas acute fatigue involves processes
of degradation — both in terms of impoverished concentration (e.g., running into
things) and “faltering form and posture” (Olson et al., 2018, p. 2004).
Numerous inventories are in place to clinically measure perceived fatigue. To
date, over 250 such instruments have been documented (Boolani et al., 2019). However, the majority treat fatigue and energy as a unified bipolar mood: fatigue is a lack
of energy and the latter, an absence of fatigue. The few instruments that track differences in fatigue and energy levels (such as the Profile of Moods Scale) contradict
this assumption and suggest fatigue has no complementary opposite state to which
it can transition, such as vigour (Boolani et al., 2019). This unipolar character of
fatigue largely follows from clinical cases of subjects experiencing fatigue yet feeling energetic, with separate dopamine and serotonin mechanisms thought to explain
this difference (Boolani et al., 2019).1 Of the small subset of current tools that measure fatigue as a unipolar state, a descriptive definition is often absent. The rating of
fatigue scale, for instance, measures the magnitude of perceived fatigue but does not
define it (Micklewright et al., 2017).
Unified operational definitions of perceived fatigue are consequently the exception, but a promising proposal can be found in Skau et al. (2021; see also Behrens
et al., 2023). There, the diverse taxonomy of fatigue (including motoric, cognitive,
state, trait, chronic, and pathological) is addressed through a single set of definitions.2 Under this unified framework the authors first reject defining fatigue as tiredness, weariness or exhaustion, as these other terms are observed as distinct sensations
(Aaronson et al., 1999; Skau et al., 2021). The authors further argue that defining

To accommodate the many kinds of fatigue, they generalize their definition: “Sensation of X fatigue is the sensation of (i) feeling the need for X rest, or (ii) mismatch
between X effort expended and actual X performance”, where X can be any domain
such as “cognitive/mental/physical/emotional” (Skau et al., 2021, p. 7). In other

for a longer duration (which they leave open to each field to determine) then it contributes to chronic fatigue (p. 7). These objective changes in fatigability, combined
with the prolonged duration of the above sensations, define longer-lasting varieties
of fatigue. Finally, pathological fatigue is characterized as an increase in trait fatigue
after the onset of a given disease, disorder or trauma (p. 7).
Rather than restating this operational definition, the present study adds a firstpersonal correlate to better elucidate what a sensed mismatch between effort and performance feels like in lived experience. It also refines Skau et al.’s account by arguing
that, at least in acute fatigue, this sensed effort-performance mismatch and the need
for rest are co-constitutive rather than either being independently sufficient. Furthermore, it addresses what empirical work identifies but leaves unexplained, namely
why fatigue’s affective character skews negative and existential relative to tiredness
and sleepiness (Long et al., 2022).
To deliver on these aims, Section 2 introduces Katherine Morris’s “Toward a
Phenomenology of Fatigue”, which holds that tiredness, acute fatigue, and chronic
fatigue differ in kind rather than degree (2019, pp. 141, 148). Section 3 argues that
her rest-sleep contrast misses how tiredness differs from sleepiness and how the urge
to rest is not unique to acute fatigue. Section 4 then explores counterexamples to
her claim that fatigue tends to foreground the body while tiredness backgrounds it.
Extending Morris’s approach, Section 5 introduces additional pre-reflective modalities of bodily awareness, including the transparent, performative, lived, and material
bodies, as well as the noetic and noematic bodily feelings that constitute them. Drawing on Fuchs’s notion of corporealization and Young’s account of skilful action, Section 6 presents this study’s core proposal: that acute fatigue is the corporealization of
what I call the minimal performative body. Section 7 extends this account to chronic
fatigue and exhaustion, and Section 8 concludes.

2 Morris’s account of tiredness, fatigue, and chronic fatigue
Although recent interest in the phenomenology of fatigue is growing (Ciocan, 2025;
Rathe, 2025; Uljée, 2026; Zeiler et al., 2026), my starting point remains Katherine
Morris, who proposes the thesis that I will also defend: namely, that tiredness, fatigue
and chronic fatigue all experientially differ by kind rather than degree (Morris, 2019,
pp. 141, 148). Morris begins her study with a Sartrean rendering of how everyday
“objects of the world” indicate tiredness through their manner of appearance (2019, p.
138). As she quotes from Being and Nothingness, tiredness is apparent, for instance,
through the “‘difficulty [with which] the words are detached from the undifferentiated ground which they constitute; they may tremble, quiver; their meaning may be
detached only with effort’” (Morris, 2019, p. 138; Sartre, 1986, p. 332).4 Similarly,
the road home appears further, longer, harder and the things that usually entice us
while awake no longer do so when tired. Morris describes this experience of difficulty and indifference as a slackening of “the intentional threads that bind [us] to the
world” (Morris, 2019, p. 138). For her, the loosening of our intentional grasp on the
world is not unique to tiredness. Fatigue, but also boredom and many other affective
experiences, can involve a sense of loosened attachment to the world.
However, one distinguishing aspect of tiredness is that it involves “a kind of
background awareness” of the body (Morris, 2019, p. 138). For example, we tacitly
notice how our “eyelids becom[e] heavy and drooping” while the words on the page
quiver (Morris, 2019, p. 138). In terms of its temporality, Morris also finds that tiredness has “a lived diurnality, which we might represent as more like ‘early/late’” (pp.
138–139). While earliness and lateness may differ across cultures (e.g., siestas), she
claims that tiredness is most often situated within a “passage of time from morning
to evening” (p. 139). Tiredness, that is, appears when we notice the day is getting late
(or when it is too early). Lastly, for Morris, tiredness has a bodily resonance, through
which others can make us tired (e.g., yawning), but their energetic contrast can also
jolt us out of our own lassitude (p. 139).
By contrast, Morris finds that the fatigued body is primarily foregrounded. As she
writes, “one may, in fatigue, quite explicitly feel one’s body as a burden to be hauled
up the stairs” or else feel an explicit “sensation of effort” (p. 139). The fatigued body
therefore appears as an object of intentional awareness, which gets further localized
and objectified through bodily aches “in a way that tiredness usually [does not]” (p.
140). Fatigue’s action tendency is also distinctive as a bodily project to rest rather
than sleep. Morris further acknowledges fatigue’s peculiar temporality. It “accumulates over a period of time” and “dissipates according to its own temporality” (Morris,
2019, p. 140). In its social dimension, Morris suggests fatigue may be less susceptible
to improvement through being around energetic others (Morris, 2019, p. 140).
One initial appeal of Morris’s study is how it implies a neat, intermediate placement of fatigue between tiredness and pain. Like tiredness, fatigue can be characterized as a loosening of our engagement with the world. But unlike tiredness, it stands
out through its foregrounded painful aches and the explicit awareness of the body’s

between tiredness and fatigue, its elegance and prima facie persuasiveness deserve
emphasis.
Having described tiredness and acute fatigue as different kinds of experience,
Morris moves to chronic fatigue. She highlights how those suffering from chronic
fatigue describe their condition metaphorically as inhabiting “a different planet” and
as losing trust in one’s own body (pp. 141–142). The body in chronic fatigue is even
“‘personified as an aversive agent’”, whereas in other cases it is presented as numb
or as a paralysed mass of flesh (p. 142). As for its temporality, chronic fatigue presents as “‘nearly always being present’” (Morris, 2019, p. 142). For many sufferers,
the temporality of chronic fatigue is marked by an unbridgeable cleft between the
life that was possible before the onset of their condition and the kind of life possible
after its onset (p. 143). This leads to a sense of isolation and lack of belonging when
being amongst others (Morris, 2019, p. 143). Morris closes her study by contrasting
the lived experiences of chronic fatigue with the stigmatizing cultural assumptions of
official discourse on the condition (pp. 144–148).

3 The bodily projects to sleep and rest
I now want to explore some key challenges for Morris’s account, while continuing
to draw on the empirical definitions above. The first concerns the difference between
the projects to sleep and rest. Morris distinguishes fatigue and tiredness on account of
their different action urges or, as she calls them, bodily projects. Tiredness involves
a “felt need to sleep”, whereas fatigue tends toward rest (p. 137). Some might object
that sleep is simply a form of rest and so the distinction lacks precision, but this concern can be alleviated. Phenomenologically, the urge to sleep appears as a felt bodily
expansion that tends toward unconsciousness (Schmitz, 2011, p. 15) and as the loss
of distance between oneself and things (as “out there”) to the point of indistinction
(Merleau-Ponty, 2010, p. 144). By contrast, the urge to rest is simply the impulse to
not undertake a given activity or else to stop one that is already underway. Understood as such, Morris’s introduction of bodily projects promises to reliably separate
fatigue from tiredness. Her approach is all the more compelling given that the project
to rest aligns with empirical definitions of acute fatigue as the sensation of feeling
the need to rest (Behrens et al., 2023, p. 11; Skau et al., 2021; Van der Linden, 2011).
The challenges for Morris’s account lie elsewhere. For one, feeling tired does not
mean feeling sleepy. Although Morris is aware that tiredness has multiple meanings
and that “we should not assume that [these different meanings] are entirely independent”, she nonetheless focuses her analysis on tiredness, understood as a readiness for

sleep (2019, p. 137). While I agree with Morris that there are multiple senses of tiredness, my main concern is that we can ordinarily feel tired without feeling sleepy. This
can be inferred from how concept users distinguish tiredness, fatigue, and sleepiness.
Long et al., for example, confirm prior studies (Pigeon et al., 2003) by recording
increases in the negative appraisal of tiredness compared with sleepiness. Tiredness
consistently ranked as an “intermediate” term between sleepiness and fatigue, with
researchers hypothesizing that tiredness could be deployed as a “more generic term
… when an individual is either too tired to be more discriminating or is experiencing
both sleepiness and fatigue” (Long et al., 2022, p. 1240). Bracketing these hypotheses, sleepiness and tiredness are observed as generally distinct both “in their phenomenology” (i.e., qualitative differences in first-person accounts) as well as in their
“quantified connotative meaning” (Long et al., 2022, p. 1240; Hossain et al., 2005).5
Further phenomenological examples corroborate the conclusion that tiredness differs from sleepiness. Consider the experience of reading after dinner. Imagine you
have been reading for some time; it is getting late, dark out, and the traffic noise has
dulled. As you continue to read, tiredness appears just as Morris describes it. Your
tiredness is apparent through the difficulty with which the words are grasped on the
page and your attention starts to slip (2019, p. 138). In the background you may feel
your heavy eyelids and a softening grip on the book, and while rereading the same
passage multiple times, the urge to stop reading begins to appear. Yet despite feeling
tired you feel no expansive tendency toward unconsciousness, nor a loosening reference to the observable world (Merleau-Ponty, 2010, p. 141). Perhaps it is still hours
before your usual bedtime. Perhaps you are overstimulated from too much caffeine or
screentime. Regardless, there is no accompanying need to sleep. Nor are there further
signs of fatigue. You are not aching or in pain. The heft of the body is not explicitly
felt. You desire no further explanation for why you feel this way (it is simply late).
In such cases it seems that tiredness, like acute fatigue, involves only an urge to rest.
Besides the phenomenological separation between tiredness and sleepiness,
another challenge for Morris’s account is that the project to rest blurs with other
action urges, such as the impulse to stop, which some philosophers attribute to feelings of effort (Bermúdez & Massin, 2023). If that is the case, the bodily project to
rest cannot help us distinguish the phenomenology of fatigue from effort. To get at
this second challenge, consider Juan Pablo Bermúdez’s work on the feeling of effort
(2025). Bermúdez’s conclusion is relevant insofar as effort is said to be “the experience of resisting an impulse, which in turn represents an action as costly and to-bestopped” (Bermúdez, 2025, p. 100). During experiences of effort, we feel urged to
terminate — and take respite from — demanding actions. Yet if rest is understood
as action-terminating, then fatigue and effort would share the same bodily project.
This does not mean Morris is wrong to attribute the urge to stop to fatigue. Besides
support from Skau et al., Dimitri Van der Linden (2011) likewise defines the perception of acute mental fatigue entirely through “an urge to stop exerting further effort

4 The foreground-background distinction
Besides the bodily project distinction, Morris suggests another key difference
between tiredness and acute fatigue. Acute fatigue is said to foreground the body in
a way that contrasts with the backgrounded body in tiredness (2019, p. 139).9 Bodily
heft and aches, for example, are experienced more prominently in acute fatigue. Morris does not specify the exact way in which this bodily foregrounding appears. She
only notes that one may “quite explicitly feel” the fatigued body, while the tired
body appears through “a kind of background awareness” (2019, pp. 138–139). However, I take there to be two main readings of this foreground-background distinction,
and I will argue that on either of them foregrounding alone cannot be what defines
acute fatigue. After considering each reading, I will then attempt to build on Morris’s
insight that bodily awareness undergoes important modifications in acute fatigue.
Turning to the foreground-background distinction, we might first take Morris to
follow the Sartrean division between non-thetic and thetic awareness, which roughly
approximates to pre-reflective and reflective experience. In Being and Nothingness,
Sartre illustrates the difference between non-thetic and thetic forms of consciousness
by describing the situation of counting cigarettes on a table (Sartre, 2022, p. 11).
Immersed in counting, we intend the number of cigarettes as the object of experience. But as we intend this number, there remains a background consciousness of
oneself doing the counting. For Sartre, the body is primarily experienced in this tacit,
non-thetic form of awareness (p. 410). It is a condition of access that is transparently

To say that tiredness appears in the background is therefore to say the tired body
appears in its usual mode, as the pre-reflective situation we presuppose while intentionally relating to something else. In thetic awareness, consciousness reflectively
intends the body in a way that parallels how it is for others (e.g., when looking down
at one’s hands). Yet Sartre insists that we can be reflectively aware of the body without objectifying it as a mere thing among things. This leads him to introduce the
concept of “the psychological body” (p. 451). Although Morris does not mention it,
Sartre claims that with the psychological body localized aches and other interoceptive phenomena appear as distinct affective objects (p. 451). The ache we notice in
reflection, for example, is transcendent but with minimal distance: “It is not yet in
any way known, because the reflection that seeks to grasp the painful consciousness
is not yet cognitive” (p. 451). Instead, we intend such aches as specifically localized
(e.g., a headache) and yet they adhere closely to non-thetic awareness, as what is
merely “disclosed through [our] pain” (p. 450). Whereas standard bodily transparency involves the body as that through which the world appears (e.g., the sliding
words and increasingly distant hilltop), one’s aching body is an intimately posited
affective object that provides just enough reflective distance to suffer (rather than
thematically grasp) our fatigue.
This way of reading Morris’s distinction is tempting for numerous reasons. For
one, it bypasses a strong requirement of reflective positing in which the fatigued
body would appear as something that I stop and ponder over. The psychological
body arguably recovers a more minimal reflectiveness that can unfold amidst ongoing actions rather than taking the form of contemplation. For another, the modal
distinction between transparent and psychological bodies is pragmatic. In practice,
we can separate the affective objects of our attention (e.g., aching legs) from what
we experience pre-reflectively (e.g., the nearly invisible body holding the cigarettes
we count). If tiredness and fatigue were to accurately track this modal distinction, it
would be reasonable to expect most people to spot the difference between them, at
least under normal epistemic conditions.
However, there is another way to read the foreground-background distinction
that avoids reflection entirely. In Giovanna Colombetti’s view, a bodily feeling can
occupy the foreground, not because it is an intentional object, but because it presents
the lived body with high degrees of luminosity (2011, p. 305). As she writes,
the foreground is not attended as an object — it is not observed or reified in
any way. In foreground bodily feelings, my body is clearly subjectively lived.
It comes to the front of awareness, it can even be overwhelming in its physical
presence, but it does so in a non-mediated and non-reflective way. (p. 304)
Backgrounded bodily feelings are likewise pre-reflective but “somehow ‘less present’ to oneself” than foregrounded ones (p. 305). Colombetti consequently maintains
that the two only differ by the relative degree to which backgrounded bodily feelings are “more recessive” and foregrounded bodily feelings, more self-presenting
(p. 305). Perhaps even more than Sartre, Colombetti’s account enables us to speak of

acute fatigue as bodily foregrounded without conceding that it is necessarily reflective or about my heavy and aching body.
Whichever interpretation we favour, the main challenge is that both readings fail
to track certain subvarieties of tiredness and fatigue, as the following two counterexamples illustrate.
First, there is the case of being awoken by a loud noise. After awakening to the
sound, you may find yourself quickly noticing how late it is and how tired your body
feels, at which point the body becomes foregrounded. Perhaps such foregrounding is
only a non-mediated self-intimation of your crusty, squinting eyelids and the diffuse
urge to not leave bed. Perhaps these bodily feelings even become unpleasant affective objects that you mildly suffer with full reflective awareness. Yet you suffer them,
because while you desire to sleep, the bodily urge to do so is absent. If we agree that
we are still speaking of tiredness, then I think we must concede that it, too, can foreground the body in both a pre-reflective and reflective sense.
Moving in the opposite direction is a second case, whereby fatigue involves primarily backgrounded bodily feelings. Imagine you are lifting a very heavy object.
It takes all your effort to overcome the resistance of the heavy object, and shortly
afterward ordinary activities start to feel more effortful than usual. Importantly, this
felt difficulty is diffuse and lacking any reflective object or localization. It is not that
new aches in your arms or legs are “popping out”, as when “a cramp in the foot suddenly grab[s] your attention” (Colombetti & Ratcliffe, 2012, p. 146). Instead, there
is a background feeling of emptiness and lag, which exercise physiologists typically
explain as central nervous system fatigue (Gandevia, 2001; Tornero-Aguilera et al.,
2022). Although the ensuing actions become more effortful than expected, this need
not make you lose trust in the body or feel as though you are inhabiting another
world, as in chronic fatigue. Endurance athletes are said to experience such central
nervous system fatigue intermittently, temporarily, and unproblematically after long
events or stressful competition calendars (Tornero-Aguilera et al., 2022, p. 2). Acute
fatigue, then, can also appear through backgrounded bodily feeling that recessively
colours “the affective quality of [the] situation” (Colombetti, 2011, p. 302). Simply
experiencing the body as foregrounded cannot be what tells us when we are fatigued
in such cases.

5 Other forms of bodily awareness
Let us take stock of what Morris’s account offers. Although it suggests a parsimonious placing of acute fatigue between tiredness and pain, fatigue’s distinctive phenomenology remains difficult to parse. The bodily project to rest aligns well with
empirical accounts of fatigue (Behrens et al., 2023; Skau et al., 2021) but overlaps
with experiences of tiredness and effort. The distinction between the backgrounded
and foregrounded body also comes apart through counterexamples. In this section, I
will build on Morris’s insight that changes in bodily awareness are core to fatigue’s
phenomenology. Doing so, however, requires new categories to pick out certain
modalities of pre-reflective experience that were missed in the previous analysis.
Here, I turn to two additional resources. The first is Colombetti and Ratcliffe’s (2012)

nuanced taxonomy of bodily feeling, which they apply to experiences of depersonalization, but which proves highly relevant to the present discussion. The second is
Legrand’s (2007) conception of the performative body, which includes both a feeling
of right movement (Legrand & Ravn, 2009, p. 400) and a pre-reflective experience
of the body as subject-agent (Legrand, 2007, p. 506). Together, these two resources
supply the axes of the present taxonomy: the first demarcates the kinds of constitutive bodily feeling at play, and the second clarifies the overall modality of the body
thereby experienced. Through their combination they yield four forms of bodily
feeling that the rest of this section builds toward. These are: (1) transparent noetic
feeling, (2) performative noetic feeling, (3) lived noematic feeling, and (4) material
noematic feeling. With this taxonomy, we can then specify which forms of bodily
awareness tiredness and acute fatigue each involve.
Turning to the first, note that when we experience tiredness through quivering
words and steep hills, bodily transparency involves more than an absence of awareness. Instead, noetic feelings are what provide a specific kind of “aware[ness] of our
body as that through which we experience the world, namely as a feeling, seeing, and
touching body” (Colombetti & Ratcliffe, 2012, p. 147). Cases of tiredness in which
we experience the world through heavy eyes and quivering words on the page are primarily shaped through noetic bodily feelings. Yet despite lacking intentionality, these
feelings contribute to the body’s enduring presence through an affective colouring of
experience (Colombetti, 2011, p. 297).
Colombetti and Ratcliffe address how the lived body (Leib) can also include intentional feelings, which they call “noematic” (2012). In touching one’s own hand, for
example, the touched hand can be intended as a living organ that feels “pleasant,
annoying, tickling, surprisingly sudden, painful, etcetera” (Colombetti & Ratcliffe,
2012, p. 146). In this way, the transparent body that is constituted through noetic feeling becomes intentional through the noematic feeling while remaining a lived body
(Leib) (2012, p. 146; see also Colombetti, 2014, p. 115). Moreover, such noematic
feelings “need not be at the centre of our attention” (Colombetti & Ratcliffe, 2012, p.
146). Although noematic feelings can intend the lived body (Leib) in a similar way
to Sartre’s psychological body, turning to Colombetti and Ratcliffe’s account helps
us avoid equating intentionality with reflective positing. For this reason, I will now
speak only of noematic bodily feeling, as doing so leaves open the degree of attentiveness and foregrounding that accompany the intended kinaesthetic sensations.
Besides the transparent body, there is also the performative body. Legrand first
introduces the concept of the performative body (building on Gallagher, 2005, p.
74) to account for how expert dancers experience the body “pre-reflectively as a
subject-agent” (2007, p. 506). In the case of skilled dancing, she notes that both the
transparent and performative bodies are co-present. While the world is experienced
in a bodily way (qua transparent body), the body is experienced pre-reflectively in a
non-reifying manner (qua performative body) (2007, p. 507). Neither the transparent
nor the performative body is here understood as an “intentional object of consciousness” (2007, p. 504; see also Colombetti, 2014, p. 118). The performative body is
thus constituted by certain kinds of noetic feeling that integrate the experiences of the
world with experiences of one’s body (Legrand, 2007, p. 510). To describe this difference in noetic feeling between the transparent and performative bodies, consider

what Legrand and Ravn call “sensing from inside” (Legrand & Ravn, 2009, p. 400).
Through this form of awareness, the interviewed ballet dancers “emphasize that their

In Legrand and Ravn’s study, it is primarily the positive pole of movements feeling right or natural (rather than wrong or unnatural) that is reported (p. 400). Because
such feelings are normative in character, I suggest adding a negative pole to performative noetic feeling. For a movement to feel right, there are implicit conditions of
success and thus conditions of violation (i.e., non-success). Together, such conditions
constitute what I will refer to as a movement’s correctness conditions. Correctness
here carries no commitment to propositional content or direction of fit. The conditions concern how a movement should unfold, whether the standard stems from
one’s own volition, habituated skill, or social norms. That a movement has violation
conditions does not by itself show that it can feel off, unnatural, or wrong. However,
evidence suggesting that violation conditions can be experienced through bodily feeling is found in cases of performance choking. Experiences of faulty movements (e.g.,
a mistimed golf swing), for instance, are said to include “sensations of imbalance,
tension, or loss of rhythm” (Toner et al., 2016, p. 304).11
Although performative noetic feelings are more prevalent in the case of expert
movers, Legrand allows for some level of performative awareness amongst “normal non-body expert people” (Legrand, 2007, pp. 505–6). To emphasize the greater
prevalence of this kind of backgrounded performative noetic feeling in daily life, I
will depart from prior accounts and conceptualize action as broadly skilful whenever
its correctness conditions feature in the experience of its performance. Non-skilful
actions, such as casually strolling or picking up groceries, can be done habitually and
without any sensitivity to foot or grocery item placement. Provided you get some
fresh air and don’t squish the bananas, these everyday actions are done well enough.
But when the pavement ices over on the walk or grocery packing becomes a tightly
regulated art form, those same actions become skilful. Foot placement now ranges
from feeling sturdy to awkward, and grocery bagging, from orderly to chaotic. Without being “a particularly sharp pre-reflective experience”, the tacitly sensed rightness
or wrongness of skilful action points to a more minimal form of performative awareness (Legrand, 2007, p. 505).

My claim is that acute fatigue, based as it is on a sensed effort-performance mismatch, entails both skilful action and, with it, (at least) minimal performative awareness. To be fatigued and not just tired on a stroll, I must be conscious of the way in

them one may well grow tired. What this implies is that I can feel tired during and
after successful effortful action but not fatigued (for which I would need to sense an
effort-performance mismatch). Additionally, it rules out that non-skilful actions could
be experienced as fatiguing, since without experienced correctness conditions there
is nothing against which movement could register as wrong. The same holds for more
cognitive actions like typing out reports at the office, where both sentence formation

movement-oriented sensing of the body in skilful action that makes the evaluative

this characteristic vividness.
Besides transparent and performative noetic feeling and lived noematic feeling,

6 Corporealization during acute fatigue
How are we to understand acute fatigue’s double involvement of the performative and
material bodies? Performative awareness lacks a noematic register, whereas material
noematic feeling is precisely what renders the body thing-like. This suggests that the
two forms of awareness pull in opposite directions. One way to resolve this tension
is through Thomas Fuchs’s account of how the lived body (Leib) can be disrupted by
the material body (Körper). To be sure, Fuchs does not primarily provide a phenomenology of fatigue. His comments belong to a wider analysis of psychopathologies,
and fatigue mainly illustrates a broader thesis he carries over to clinical phenomena

movement, which is disrupted by (ii) the noematic feeling that now intends the material body as underperforming.17

wish to cover over the critical feminist thrust of her work. The categories she develops are meant to be diagnostic of the problematic ways in which feminine bodies
are socially habituated. Still, her descriptive categories are illuminating for our purposes insofar as they describe changes in bodily awareness that unfold during skilful
action: from throwing, running, climbing, and hitting (Young, 1980, p. 143), to lifting
heavy things (p. 142), and jumping (p. 144). In all these actions, her concern is with

unlike in acute fatigue, are not necessarily accompanied by a felt need for rest.

With the concept of existential enclosure, Young illuminates the spatiality accompanying the temporal process of breaking down. Transforming into a mode that resembles inert physical objects rooted into their own locations, the movement-oriented
space of the lived body is disrupted. Instead, bodily awareness becomes materialized
by feeling increasingly stuck over here and separated from the over there. Existential
enclosure thus affects the very spatiality of the performative body, as the latter loses
its bearings in the otherwise fluid kinaesthetic sensations guiding its movements. In
action, tiredness is sedating, but there is a sense in which we are simply here. We are
not cut off from over there. In fatigue, existential enclosure makes our movements
feel like they are in the wrong place, heading in the wrong direction, or not landing
where they should. Moreover, such enclosure corporealizes the minimal performative body by modifying presence into thing-like inertness.
Taken together, these three moments of ambiguous transcendence, inhibited intentionality, and existential enclosure further our schematic analysis of how the performative body can be corporealized in fatigue. This deepened account reveals the
phenomenological structures behind acute fatigue’s sensed effort-performance mismatch (Skau et al., 2021), how the performative awareness of fatigue differs from
tiredness, and how these descriptions can apply to various subvarieties (e.g., cognitive, motoric, central fatigue). These normatively laden disruptions also indicate why
fatigue’s affective profile is more negative than that of tiredness (Long et al., 2022).
Tiredness is lived primarily through the transparent body and without the same concerning (or potentially embarrassing) awareness of underperformance that accompanies performative corporealization during acute fatigue.

7 Chronic fatigue and exhaustion
How does the present account of acute fatigue relate to chronic fatigue and exhaustion? Recall that Morris takes chronic fatigue as a distinctive kind of experience,
marked by feelings of inhabiting a different planet, social isolation, and lost trust
in one’s own body. Recreational marathon runners similarly single out exhaustion
as an experience distinct from tiredness and fatigue (Olson et al., 2018). This last
section addresses how the present definition of acute fatigue accommodates these
descriptions. Based on them we can argue that chronic fatigue and exhaustion differ
from tiredness and acute fatigue through their distinct change in what Ratcliffe calls
“existential feeling” (Ratcliffe, 2012, 2020).
For Ratcliffe, existential feeling denotes “all-enveloping” feelings, in particular
the sense of reality and belonging (2020, p. 250). Importantly, existential feelings are
not intentional states directed at this or that object. They are pre-intentional ways of
finding oneself in the world, shaping the form of experience that intentional states
presuppose (Ratcliffe, 2012, p. 37). To understand the kind of experience he has in
mind, consider his example of someone losing their job:
Consider someone who loses an important life project, such as a job that she
cares deeply about. She might complain that … a whole system of practical
significance focused around the job has collapsed, and the entities it involves

therefore look somehow strange. However, the relevant experience could take
two different forms. In one scenario, the person loses many instances of experienced possibility. She retains the capacity to encounter things as offering p,
even though many things that used to incorporate p now lack it. An alternative

where … her world no longer includes the possibility of anything offering p.
Only the latter is what I would call a change in existential feeling, a change in

If we are to understand existential feeling along these lines, then tiredness and acute
fatigue would not themselves be changes in existential feeling. Tiredness and acute
fatigue involve only the loss of token instances of experienced possibilities, such
as the possibility of finishing a book or winning a race. When fatigue interrupts our
chances of finishing a book, we may continue a few pages or paragraphs more. When
it disrupts our ability to proficiently climb the hill, we may still struggle to the peak,
just with far more effort, at a slower pace and, as I have argued elsewhere, with the
constrained ability to playfully engage in the current activity (Smyth, 2026a).
Yet chronic fatigue and exhaustion are elsewhere described as the loss of reality
(Zeiler et al., 2026, p. 14) and inhabiting “a different planet” (Morris, 2019, p. 141).
That is to say, they involve changes in existential feeling. Gone is the very possibility
of fully belonging to the world as others do or as one did before the onset of chronic
fatigue syndrome. Gone, too, is the possibility of trusting one’s body and of identifying with the frequent disruptions experienced at the level of the transparent body.
Exhaustion, whether transient (as in the depleted marathon runner) or prolonged,
likewise involves the loss of the very possibility of action. When one is exhausted,
the world appears categorically untraversable. There are only undoable tasks, unclimbable hills, and so forth. While exhaustion lasts, nothing offers itself as doable
or performable.
By contrast, if we did not already minimally belong to the world and its possibilities, acute fatigue and tiredness would be foreclosed. The world slows down in
tiredness, and the body begins to waver in fatigue. But the world is not at a standstill,
and we do not simply collapse (Smyth 2026b, p. 432). Tiredness and acute fatigue
thus presuppose a certain sense of ability. Hence, when Morris claims that fatigue
involves a general existential feeling associated with our “lapse or diminishment in
our capacity to go on” (2019, p. 138), we can argue that acute fatigue is not itself an
existential feeling but presupposes one. The existential feeling against which fatigue
unfolds is its minimally preserved sense of ability.20 A diminished sense of ability,
in other words, is not yet a different kind of feeling; it is the same existential feeling
in weakened form. Although the existential feeling of ability captures neither the
unique phenomenology of tiredness nor of acute fatigue, it helps to separate them
from chronic fatigue and exhaustion. In terms of empirical definitions, the distinctive
change of existential feeling in chronic fatigue provides a phenomenological descrip20

8 Conclusion
This paper defines acute fatigue as the corporealization of the minimal performative
body. In closing, I want to defend the decision to build on what is otherwise a passing
claim in Fuchs. Consider first that corporealization fits well with Long et al.’s hypothesis that fatigue may be frequently associated “with pathological experiences” (2022,
p. 1239). The breakdown and wrongness of movements in performative awareness
are an initial indication that fatigue feels like something is going wrong with the body
without thereby leading us to believe we are ill. At the same time, Fuchs’s discussion of corporealization explicitly understands the material body as the “anatomical
object of physiology and medicine” (2002; 2005b, p. 108). Historical accounts also
tie fatigue to an increasingly medicalized body. Georges Vigarello links its emergence as a distinctive feeling to the “electric fluid” economy of 18th-century Europe
(2022, p. 106).21 Robert Hockey similarly notes an “explicit association of fatigue
with energy loss” at the end of the 19th century (2013, p. 45). The cultural prominence of fatigue and medicalized conceptions of the material body would thus appear
to be co-emergent.22 The layering of subject and object in corporealization is also
gestured at in applied phenomenological research on chronic fatigue. Patients with
severe cancer-related fatigue, for example, describe the fatigued body as at once “a
subject that is feeling and acting and as a material object that is felt and observed,”
with the two “closely intertwined” (Bootsma et al., 2023, p. 509). These convergent
considerations support the claim that acute fatigue implicates the material body and
allow us to differentiate acute from chronic experiences by distinguishing between
the minimal performative body that is operative during acute fatigue and the change
in existential feeling that occurs in chronic cases.
Looking ahead, the present study opens onto questions concerning how gendered
bodily comportment and fatigue may intertwine. Whether and how perceived fatigue
differs by gender remains underexamined. Existing studies on sex-based differences in physical performance, for instance, indicate that female exercise subjects
demonstrate smaller drops in performance during certain isometric contraction tests
(Hunter, 2014, p. 780), yet there are also suggestions that pain perceptions during
exercise may nonetheless be higher for women (Hunter, 2014, p. 782). How internalized norms and cultural assumptions about gender-based capacities interact with
perceived fatigue against this complex empirical backdrop is unresolved. As future
