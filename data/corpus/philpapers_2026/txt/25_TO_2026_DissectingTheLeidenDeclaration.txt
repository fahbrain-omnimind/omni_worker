Dissecting the Leiden Declaration:
The Missing Bridge from Mathematical Validity to
Human Authorship
T.O. (IASER Practitioner)
September 2026

Abstract
The Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics, published on 2 June
2026 and endorsed by the International Mathematical Union, responds to the challenges
that artificial intelligence poses to mathematical research. It grounds its recommendations
in what it calls the characteristic values of mathematical research: the certainty and understanding conferred by proof, the attribution of results to specific authors, the transparency
and independent verifiability of arguments, proper evaluation against shared standards, and
the understanding and judgment of autonomously guided communities. This paper neither rejects the Declaration nor evaluates the risks of artificial intelligence. It examines
the internal structure of the Declaration, and asks whether the epistemic values that concern mathematical knowledge are distinguished from the institutional values that concern
mathematical practice. We establish three claims of non-entailment: the epistemic values
do not entail human authorship, under any formalization that speaks only of results and
their arguments; the requirement that a responsible subject exist does not entail that the
generator of a result be human; and the path of legitimacy the Declaration describes, results
attributed to named authors and published in peer-reviewed venues, is neither a necessary
nor a suﬀicient condition of validity. Each claim of non-entailment is established by an
explicit model construction, and its finite instances are verified by exhaustive enumeration.
The central thesis is that the Declaration may legitimately defend both kinds of values, but
it does not thereby establish that the latter are conditions of the former. The Declaration’s
reasonable protections — disclosure, responsibility, verification, vigilance against plausible
but false arguments — are retained, and are shown to become stronger once their epistemic
and institutional components are separated. The dissection is itself conducted under the
Declaration’s own epistemic values: every step rests on the published text of the Declaration, every inference is open to independent verification, the use of automated tools is
disclosed, and responsibility for the content rests with the author. The paper is therefore
not an external critique of the Declaration but an application of its epistemic standards to
the Declaration itself. Its validity, if it has any, does not depend on the identity of its author.

Introduction

1.1

The Declaration

The Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics was published on 2 June 2026
[1]. It was drafted by sixteen researchers from fifteen universities, following the NIAS–Lorentz
Workshop on Mechanization and Mathematical Research held in Leiden in September 2025,
and it is endorsed by the International Mathematical Union (IMU) [2]. The Declaration addresses individual mathematicians, mathematical organizations, policymakers, and commercial
developers of artificial intelligence, and it issues recommendations to each [1].
The Declaration is significant for a reason that is independent of whether one agrees with
it. It makes explicit what the mathematical community says it wishes to preserve. A value that
1

is made explicit can be examined: its content can be stated, its relations to other values can be
traced, and the inferences that lead from it to a recommendation can be checked. This paper
undertakes that examination.

1.2

The grounding sentence

The Declaration states the ground of its recommendations in a single sentence:
“We base our recommendations on what we take to be characteristic values of mathematical research that we have a joint interest in preserving.” [1]
The sentence fixes the logical form of the document. The recommendations are presented as
following from a set of values, and the values are presented together, as one set. Whatever
relations hold among the values therefore bear on whether the recommendations follow from
them.

1.3

The question

The question of this paper is:
What exactly is being protected, and which claims about mathematical knowledge
actually entail which claims about mathematical practice?

1.4

Position and scope

The paper does not ask whether the Declaration is desirable, and it does not deny the practical
problems the Declaration identifies. It does not evaluate the risks of artificial intelligence, and
it does not attribute motives to the authors or signatories of the Declaration. It examines the
text. Where the paper quotes the Declaration, it quotes the published text; where it quotes a
supporter of the Declaration, it says so, and it does not treat a supporter’s words as part of the
Declaration.
The questions addressed here belong to the philosophy of mathematical practice, the cluster
of approaches that treats philosophical issues arising from actual mathematical practice, including the social dimensions of mathematical knowledge and the challenges raised by computerassisted proof, formalization and new AI tools [3]. The method is a structural analysis of a
normative document: the Declaration is decomposed into its components, and the inferential
relations among them are tested.

1.5

Reflexive position

The dissection is conducted under the Declaration’s own epistemic values. Every step of the
argument rests on the published text of the Declaration, and every inference is presented in
a form that a reader can check independently of the identity of the writer; the three central
claims of non-entailment are, in addition, established by explicit counter-models and verified
by exhaustive enumeration (Appendices A and B). The paper follows the Declaration’s recommendations to individual mathematicians: the use of automated tools, including large language
models, is disclosed in the Acknowledgements; responsibility for the correctness of the arguments and for the completeness and accuracy of citations rests with the author; references are
given with identifiers, and matters that could not be confirmed in primary sources have been
excluded from the text; and the paper is itself a participation in the public discourse the Declaration invites [1]. The central arguments are formulated and adopted by the author, and they
were cross-checked by independent systems before completion, in line with the standards the
Declaration asks organizations to apply to results obtained with automated tools [1].

(R) If the reasoning of this paper is valid, and the paper is made public without

1.6

Organization

Section 2 separates the values of the Declaration into two domains. Section 3 shows that
the inference from the first domain to human authorship is not valid without an additional
premise. Section 4 separates attribution from verification. Section 5 separates responsibility
from authorship. Section 6 examines the path of legitimacy described by the Declaration.
Section 7 states the Declaration’s reasonable protections and shows that they are compatible
with the central thesis. Section 8 states what the dissection reveals, and Section 9 concludes.

2

The Declaration’s Two Domains

The Declaration lists five values [1]. In its own words:
(V1) “Mathematical proofs are regarded as conferring the highest degree of certainty to their
conclusions, as well as imparting understanding of why their conclusions are true.”
(V2) “Results are attributable to specific authors who take credit for their discovery and assume
responsibility for their correctness.”
(V3) “Mathematical arguments are regarded as transparent and subject to independent verification. They may be extremely long or diﬀicult, but in principle no proprietary knowledge
or equipment should be required to understand them.”
(V4) “Mathematicians share a concern for proper evaluation of mathematical work relative to
shared standards of depth, diﬀiculty, and significance.”
(V5) “Mathematics produces not only a body of results, but also understanding, clarity, and
judgment among the communities of mathematicians who have shaped them, often in the
context of their own autonomously guided research.”
The five values are not of one kind. To separate them, we use a criterion that the values
themselves make available.
Definition 2.1 (Author-invariance). A value is author-invariant if whether a given result satisfies it is unchanged when the producer of the result is changed while the result itself — its
statement, its argument, and the relation of the argument to the statement — is kept fixed. A
value that is author-invariant is called epistemic; a value that is not is called institutional.
The criterion asks a single question of each value: does it concern the result, or does it
concern who produced the result and in which community?
• V1 concerns what a proof confers on its conclusion. A proof that confers certainty and
understanding does so whoever wrote it. V1 is epistemic.
3

• V3 concerns the transparency and independent verifiability of an argument. The Declaration’s own formulation excludes any dependence on proprietary knowledge or equipment;
independent verification is, by definition, verification that does not depend on the producer. V3 is epistemic.
• V2 concerns the attribution of a result to specific authors who take credit and assume
responsibility. Whether V2 is satisfied depends on who the producer is. V2 is institutional.
• V4 concerns evaluation by mathematicians relative to shared standards; V5 concerns understanding and judgment among the communities that have shaped the results, in the
context of their own autonomously guided research. Both concern the community within
which results are produced and assessed. V4 and V5 are institutional.
We write
E = {V1, V3},

I = {V2, V4, V5}

for the epistemic values, which concern mathematics as knowledge, and the institutional values,
which concern mathematics as a practice. The classification of V1 and V2 is verified formally in
Appendix A.1: in the finite model used there, a change of producer never changes the truth value
of the epistemic premises, and frequently changes the truth value of the attribution condition.
Definition 2.2 (Epistemic language). Let LE be a first-order language with a sort R of results
and any predicates and functions on R that concern statements, arguments and their relations,
but containing no symbol for the agents related to a result (gen, sub, ver, cor, Human) and no
symbol for the route of publication (Named, Reviewed). Let L be LE extended by a sort A of
agents, the predicate Human on A, the functions gen, sub, ver, cor : R → A assigning to each
result its generator, submitter, verifier and corrector (Section 5), and the predicates Named,
Reviewed on R.
Definition 2.1 has a syntactic counterpart: a value is epistemic in the sense of Definition 2.1
if it can be formalized by sentences of LE , since such sentences do not refer to the producer and
their truth value is therefore unchanged when the producer is changed. The classification of this
section places V1 and V3 in E; accordingly, any formalization of V1 and V3 considered below is
a set of LE -sentences.
The separation implies no ranking. Institutional values may be as important as epistemic
ones, and the Declaration may legitimately defend both. The question of the next section is
whether the Declaration’s recommendations treat the institutional values as following from the
epistemic ones.

3

The Missing Bridge

3.1

The inference

Consider the following statements, each of which the Declaration either asserts or presupposes.
(P1) The validity of a mathematical result is assessed by the correctness of its proof.
(P2) Mathematical proofs should be independently verifiable.
(C) Mathematical results must be attributed to human authors.
(P1) and (P2) are one formalization of V1 and V3 in LE , with a predicate Valid for validity.
The result below does not depend on this choice of formalization (Corollary 3.3). (C) is the
content of the Declaration’s recommendation to individual mathematicians headed “Aﬀirm the
humanity of authorship”:
4

“Credit and responsibility continue to belong to humans within the mathematical
community and should not be given to automated systems.” [1]
Proposition 3.1 (The missing bridge). (C) is not a logical consequence of (P1) and (P2).
Moreover, (C) is independent of {(P1), (P2)}: both {(P1), (P2), (C)} and {(P1), (P2), ¬(C)} are
satisfiable.
Proof. Consider a single result r whose proof is correct and independently verifiable, and whose
producer is not human. In this situation (P1) and (P2) are satisfied, since r is valid, its validity
is assessed by the correctness of its proof, and its proof is verifiable; and (C) is not satisfied, since
the producer of r is not human. Hence (P1) and (P2) do not entail (C). If instead the producer
of r is human, all three statements are satisfied. The formal version of this argument, with an
explicit model, is given in Appendix A.1; exhaustive enumeration over all interpretations on a
domain of two results and two producers is given in Appendix B.1.
Lemma 3.2 (Invariance lemma). Let Γ be a set of LE -sentences containing a unary predicate
Valid.
(a) If Γ∪{∃r Valid(r)} is satisfiable, then, for each f ∈ {gen, sub, ver, cor}, Γ ̸|= ∀r (Valid(r) →
Human(f (r))).
(b) If Γ ∪ {∃r Valid(r)} is satisfiable, then Γ ̸|= ∀r (Valid(r) → Reviewed(r)).
(c) If Γ ∪ {∃r ¬Valid(r)} is satisfiable, then Γ ̸|= ∀r (Path(r) → Valid(r)), where Path(r) :=
Named(r) ∧ Reviewed(r) (Definition 6.1).
Proof. Let ME be an LE -structure satisfying Γ and the relevant existential sentence. Expand
ME to an L-structure M by adding interpretations for the new symbols; since the sentences of
Γ contain none of these symbols, M |= Γ (the truth of a sentence in an expansion is determined
by its reduct to the language of the sentence). (a) Let AM = {h, m}, HumanM = {h} and
f M (r) = m for all r and all f ∈ {gen, sub, ver, cor}. For r0 with M |= Valid(r0 ), M ̸|=
Human(f (r0 )). (b) Let ReviewedM = ∅. For r0 with M |= Valid(r0 ), M ̸|= Reviewed(r0 ). (c)
Let r1 satisfy M |= ¬Valid(r1 ) and let NamedM = ReviewedM = {r1 }. Then M |= Path(r1 )
and M ̸|= Valid(r1 ).
Corollary 3.3 (Independence from the formalization). Let ΓE be any formalization of V1 and
V3 in LE that admits at least one valid result. Then ΓE ̸|= (C).
Proof. Lemma 3.2(a) with f = gen. Proposition 3.1 is the instance ΓE = {(P1), (P2)}.
The condition that ΓE admit a valid result excludes only formalizations under which no
mathematical result could be valid; such a formalization would not express V1, which speaks
of proofs that confer certainty on their conclusions. Corollary 3.3 closes the step from the
Declaration’s epistemic values to the non-entailment: whatever sentences are taken to express
V1 and V3, provided they are epistemic in the sense of Section 2, they do not entail human
authorship.
In symbols:
epistemic validity ̸⇒ human authorship.
To derive (C) from (P1) and (P2), an additional premise is required, one that connects the
validity of a result with the identity of its producer. That premise is the bridge. The Declaration
does not state it.

3.2

The bridge is used

It might be objected that the Declaration never claims that human authorship is a condition of
mathematical truth, and that Proposition 3.1 therefore refutes a claim nobody made.
The objection is correct about what the Declaration claims, and this paper does not attribute that claim to it. The point concerns the structure of the document. By the grounding
sentence of Section 1, the Declaration bases its recommendations on the characteristic values
of mathematical research, presented together, as one set. The set contains both the epistemic
values of E and the institutional values of I. The recommendation to aﬀirm the humanity of
authorship is one of the recommendations so grounded. Since both kinds of values are offered
as the joint ground of the same recommendations, the logical relations between them are open
to examination; and Proposition 3.1 shows that the epistemic values, taken by themselves, do
not ground the recommendation that authorship be human.

3.3

What is not claimed

It does not follow that human authorship should be abandoned. A community may adopt the
attribution of results to human authors as an institutional norm, for reasons of its own. What
follows is narrower: if it does so, the norm is an institutional choice concerning the organization
of mathematical practice, and not a consequence of the conditions under which mathematical
results acquire their validity.

4

Attribution Is Not Verification

The Declaration does not exclude automated tools from mathematical research. Its first recommendation to individual mathematicians reads:
“Transparently disclose the use of automated tools, including large language models,
machine learning systems, proof assistants, and other mathematical software.” [1]
The list places large language models in one class with proof assistants and other mathematical
software. The Declaration thereby treats these tools as members of a single continuum, subject
to one obligation of disclosure.
For results obtained with automated tools, the Declaration asks organizations to demand
standards that address the associated risks:
“These might include requiring human descriptions of central arguments obtained
by automated tools, insisting on formal verification when appropriate, cross-checking
theoretical and computational results, or external pre-submission review.” [1]
Each item in this list is a means of verification. None of them is a means of excluding a result
because of the kind of producer that generated it.
The history of computer-assisted proof shows the same pattern. In 1976 Appel and Haken
proved the four color theorem; the proof, published in 1977, relied on extensive computer verification and was the first major theorem to be proved in this way [4, 5]. Tymoczko argued that
the acceptance of such a proof changed the concept of mathematical proof, since its computational part could not be surveyed by a human mathematician [6]. The doubt was answered not
by excluding the computer but by a more rigorous use of it: Gonthier produced a formal proof
of the theorem in which the correctness of the computational part is itself formally verified [7].
Distrust of a tool was resolved by a means of verifying the tool.
Two functions are thus to be kept apart. Verification establishes whether a result is valid;
it operates on the result and is author-invariant. Attribution establishes to whom a result is
credited; it operates on the relation between the result and its producer and is not authorinvariant. The presence of an automated tool in the production of a result is a fact about the
6

production, and it is a question for attribution and disclosure; whether the result is valid is a
question for verification. The Declaration’s own standards for automated results are standards
of the second kind. This raises a question that the Declaration does not answer:
If verification can establish mathematical validity independently of the identity of the
producer, what additional epistemic role is served by requiring human authorship?

5

Responsibility Is Not Authorship

5.1

The Declaration on responsibility

The Declaration connects authorship with responsibility. Its recommendation to individual
mathematicians headed “Retain the responsibility for correctness” reads:
“When automated techniques are employed in published mathematical research, the
responsibility for the correctness and adequacy of the arguments and results, as well
as for the completeness and accuracy of citations to relevant prior work, remains
exclusively with the human authors.” [1]
The requirement that a responsible subject exist is reasonable, and this paper retains it (Section 7). The question is what it entails.

5.2

Decomposing responsibility

Responsibility for a published result comprises distinct roles:
• the generator, who produces the content of the result;
• the submitter, who presents the result for publication and answers for it;
• the verifier, who undertakes to establish its correctness;
• the corrector, who undertakes to correct it if it proves wrong.
The requirement that responsibility rest with human authors is a requirement on the submitter,
the verifier and the corrector. It does not by itself say anything about the generator.
Proposition 5.1 (Responsibility without human generation). Let (Resp) be the statement that,
for every result, the submitter, the verifier and the corrector are human, and let (G) be the
statement that, for every result, the generator is human. Then (G) is not a logical consequence
of (Resp).
Proof. Consider a single result r whose content was generated, in whole or in part, by an
automated tool, and whose submitter, verifier and corrector are a human author. (Resp) is
satisfied and (G) is not. The formal model is given in Appendix A.2, and exhaustive enumeration
in Appendix B.2.
The configuration used in the proof is not hypothetical. It is the configuration in which this
paper was produced: automated tools were employed as external reasoning engines, their use
is disclosed, and all responsibility for the content rests with the author (see the Acknowledgements). The Declaration’s requirement is satisfied in full, while the generator is not exclusively
human. The separation of responsibility from generation is therefore not merely possible; it is
in operation.

5.3

The reasons offered

In a commentary on the Declaration, Gowers, who declined to sign it, asks of a future in which
problems are solved by AI with formalized and verified solutions: “Is that a future we should
resist, and if so, why?” [8]. The question can be answered only by reasons, and the reasons that
may be offered for human authorship can each be examined against the decomposition above.
• Accountability. Accountability requires that someone answer for the result. This is a
requirement on the submitter, the verifier and the corrector, and it is satisfied by (Resp).
• Attribution of sources. The Declaration asks for “proactive effort to find and credit the
sources that made a new result possible” [1]. This is an obligation concerning the relation
of a result to prior work; it can be discharged by the submitter, whoever generated the
content.
• Understanding. If understanding is taken to be understanding of the result by those who
work with it, it concerns the community in which the result is received, and belongs to
V5. It is a reason for the institution, not a condition of the validity of the result.
• Community. The preservation of a community of mathematicians is a reason concerning
the organization of practice. It belongs to I.
Each reason is a reason for a norm in I, or is already satisfied by (Resp). None of them supplies
the missing bridge of Section 3.
The legitimate concerns of the Declaration — provenance, responsibility, verification — are
all retained under this decomposition. What is isolated is the single further requirement that
the generator be human, which can now be examined on its own.

6

The Legitimacy Path

The Declaration also describes the route by which mathematical results are to be made public.
Among its recommendations to mathematical organizations is the following:
“Demand that mathematical results continue to be published in peer-reviewed venues
such as journals, proceedings, and books.” [1]
Combined with V2, the attribution of results to specific authors, the Declaration describes a
path of legitimacy: a result reaches the public as a result attributed to named authors and
published in a peer-reviewed venue.
This section makes no claim about how reviewers behave and uses no sociological data. It
asks only how the Declaration’s own text relates the path to validity.
Definition 6.1 (Path and validity). For a result r, let Named(r) mean that r is attributed to
specific named authors, Reviewed(r) that r is published in a peer-reviewed venue, and Valid(r)
that r is valid in the sense of (P1) and (P2). Define
Path(r) := Named(r) ∧ Reviewed(r).
Proposition 6.2 (The path and validity).
(i) Path is not a necessary condition of Valid: ∀r (Valid(r) → Path(r)) is not a logical truth.
(ii) Path is not a suﬀicient condition of Valid: ∀r (Path(r) → Valid(r)) is not a logical truth.
(iii) The conditional
∃r (Valid(r) ∧ ¬Reviewed(r)) → ¬ ∀r (Valid(r) → Reviewed(r))
is a logical truth.
8

Proof. (i) A valid result that has not been published in a peer-reviewed venue satisfies Valid(r)∧
¬Path(r). (ii) A result attributed to named authors and published in a peer-reviewed venue
whose proof contains an error satisfies Path(r) ∧ ¬Valid(r). (iii) The consequent is equivalent to ∃r (Valid(r) ∧ ¬Reviewed(r)), which is the antecedent. The formal model is given in
Appendix A.3, and exhaustive enumeration in Appendix B.3.
Proposition 6.2(iii) is the logical form of the conditional (R) of Section 1.5.
The correspondence with the Declaration is as follows. The recommendation to demand
publication in peer-reviewed venues is one of the recommendations grounded, by the grounding
sentence of Section 1, in the characteristic values taken together. For this recommendation to
be grounded in the epistemic values alone, those values would have to connect validity with
the path, in one direction or the other. By Lemma 3.2(b) and (c), no formalization of V1 and
V3 in LE entails either ∀r (Valid(r) → Reviewed(r)) or ∀r (Path(r) → Valid(r)), provided it
admits both a valid and a non-valid result. The recommendation therefore draws its ground
from the institutional values. This is not a claim that the Declaration asserts either conditional;
it identifies which part of the Declaration’s own ground can support the recommendation.
The Declaration contains no provision for evaluation independent of the identity of the author: it does not mention anonymous or blind review [1]. The path it describes is therefore a path
on which the named author is visible at every stage. This is a description of the Declaration’s
text, not a claim about the behavior of any reviewer. Its significance is structural. Proposition 3.1 showed that validity does not bridge to human authorship; Proposition 6.2 shows that
the institutional path the Declaration describes does not bridge to validity either. The missing
bridge of Section 3 applies not only to the conditions under which mathematical knowledge is
valid, but also to the institutional route by which the Declaration expects valid results to be
made public.

The Declaration’s Reasonable Protections

The Declaration contains protections that this paper retains without qualification [1]:
• the disclosure of automated tools, including large language models, proof assistants and
other mathematical software;
• the retention of responsibility for the correctness of arguments and results and for the
completeness and accuracy of citations;
• verification: human descriptions of central arguments, formal verification when appropriate, cross-checking of theoretical and computational results;
• vigilance against arguments generated by automated tools that are plausible but unreliable
and hard to distinguish from valid proofs;
• the corresponding obligation on reviewers: “When acting as a reviewer, abide by publisher
guidelines. If the use of artificial intelligence is allowed, be transparent about how you
used it, and take responsibility for any significant recommendations you make.”;
• attention to the relation between commercial developers of artificial intelligence and research, including the risk that research questions are prioritized for their amenability to
AI methods;
• investment in public computational infrastructure;
• and, in V3 itself, the principle that no proprietary knowledge or equipment should be
required to understand a mathematical argument.
9

Each of these protections is compatible with the central thesis of this paper, and most of
them belong to the epistemic domain: disclosure, verification and vigilance serve the establishment of validity; the principle of V3 excludes the dependence of understanding on privileged
access. Responsibility, as Section 5 showed, is satisfied by the roles of submitter, verifier and
corrector. These protections are stronger when their epistemic and institutional components
are separated. A requirement of verification that is presented as a requirement of verification
cannot be dismissed as the defence of a profession; a requirement of human authorship that is
presented as a condition of validity can.

8

What the Dissection Reveals

The dissection separates two layers.
Layer I — Mathematics as knowledge. Proof, validity, verification, transparency, reproducibility. These are properties of results, and they are author-invariant.
Layer II — Mathematics as a human institution. Authorship, credit, responsibility,
employment, funding, community, autonomy. These are properties of the practice within which
results are produced, evaluated and made public.
Proposition 8.1 (Central thesis). Layer II may be legitimately protected without presenting
every feature of Layer II as an epistemic condition of Layer I. The Declaration may legitimately
defend both epistemic values concerning mathematical knowledge and institutional values concerning mathematical practice, but it does not thereby establish that the latter are conditions of
the former.
Proof. Call an institutional feature F an epistemic condition if every formalization ΓE of V1 and
V3 in LE entails ∀r (Valid(r) → F (r)). The institutional features examined in this paper are
human generation (Section 3), human submission, verification and correction (Section 5), and
the path of legitimacy (Section 6). By Lemma 3.2(a), with f = gen and with f ∈ {sub, ver, cor},
neither human generation nor human submission, verification or correction is an epistemic condition (Corollary 3.3 for the first). By Lemma 3.2(b) and Proposition 6.2(i), the path is not an
epistemic condition; by Lemma 3.2(c) and Proposition 6.2(ii), the epistemic values do not entail
that the path suﬀices for validity. Hence none of the institutional features examined here follows
from the epistemic values as a condition of validity. Moreover, by Proposition 5.1, retaining the
institutional requirement of human responsibility does not reintroduce human generation. By
Section 7, the reasonable protections of the Declaration are compatible with this conclusion.
The distinction applies also to the words of the Declaration’s supporters, which are to be
distinguished from its text. The Declaration’s website presents the words of Ulrike Tillmann,
Vice President of the IMU: “Mathematics is, and should always remain, a profoundly human
endeavour” [1]. As a statement about mathematics as a practice, the sentence belongs to Layer
II, where it can be defended as an institutional and cultural value. To make it a condition of
Layer I would require an argument that the Declaration does not supply. The same website
presents the words of Peter Scholze, according to which the goal of mathematical research is
human understanding of mathematics, so that mathematics can only thrive in a community
of human mathematicians [1]. The sentence has the form of an inference, and it shows where
a bridge can be built: by a premise that defines the goal of mathematical research as human
understanding. Such a premise is a statement about the aim of the practice. It belongs to Layer
II. When the bridge is supplied, it is supplied from Layer II; it does not run from the validity of
results.

Conclusion

The Leiden Declaration deserves neither dismissal nor uncritical acceptance. It deserves dissection. Its importance lies in the fact that it makes explicit what the mathematical community
says it wishes to preserve; and because it does so, the relations among the things it wishes to
preserve can be examined.
The examination shows that the Declaration joins two kinds of values in one ground. The
first kind concerns the validity of mathematical results, and is author-invariant. The second
concerns the organization of mathematical practice, and is not. The Declaration may defend
both. It does not show that the second is a condition of the first. This distinction matters
precisely because artificial intelligence forces mathematics to confront the difference between
the validity of a result and the identity of its producer.
This paper was written under the epistemic values of the Declaration. Whether it is valid
is to be decided by its reasoning, which is open to verification, and not by the identity of its
author. That is a consequence of the first value the Declaration names.
Before asking whether artificial intelligence can be a mathematician, one must explain what
being a mathematician contributes to the validity of mathematical knowledge.

Verifiable on R; a unary predicate Human on A; a function gen : R → A assigning to each result
its producer.
Statements.
(P1)

∀r (Valid(r) ↔ CorrectProof(r)),

(P2)

∀r (Valid(r) → Verifiable(r)),

(C)

∀r (Valid(r) → Human(gen(r))).

Model. R = {r}, A = {h, m}, Human = {h}, gen(r) = m, Valid = CorrectProof =
Verifiable = {r}.
Claim.

The model satisfies (P1) and (P2) and does not satisfy (C).

Proof. Valid(r) and CorrectProof(r) are both true, so (P1) holds; Valid(r) and Verifiable(r)
are both true, so (P2) holds; Valid(r) is true and Human(gen(r)) = Human(m) is false, so (C)
fails. Replacing gen(r) = m by gen(r) = h yields a model of (P1), (P2) and (C); hence (C) is
independent of {(P1), (P2)}.
Author-invariance. The statements (P1) and (P2) contain neither Human nor gen. Two
structures that differ only in the interpretation of gen therefore assign the same truth values to
(P1) and (P2), whereas they may assign different truth values to (C). This is the formal content
of the classification of Section 2: the epistemic premises are author-invariant (Definition 2.1),
and the attribution condition is not.

Model C: the legitimacy path (Section 6)

Signature. A sort of results R; unary predicates Named, Reviewed, Valid on R; Path(r) :=
Named(r) ∧ Reviewed(r).
Models.
(C1) R = {r}, Named = ∅, Reviewed = ∅, Valid = {r}.
(C2) R = {r}, Named = Reviewed = {r}, Valid = ∅.
Claim. (C1) falsifies ∀r (Valid(r) → Path(r)); (C2) falsifies ∀r (Path(r) → Valid(r)); the
conditional of Proposition 6.2(iii) holds in every structure.
Proof. In (C1), Valid(r) is true and Path(r) is false. In (C2), Path(r) is true and Valid(r) is
false. For (iii): ¬∀r (Valid(r) → Reviewed(r)) is equivalent to ∃r ¬(Valid(r) → Reviewed(r)),
that is, to ∃r (Valid(r) ∧ ¬Reviewed(r)); the conditional has the form ψ → ψ.
