Abstract
To be conscious is to be an experiencing subject. This can be defined not in terms
of computational functions or particular biological substrates, but rather in
terms of relations: between subject and world, between parts of the subject, and
through time. These kinds of relations – comprising a conscious mode of being –
may well be implementable in artificial systems.
(A commentary on: Seth AK. Conscious artificial intelligence and biological
naturalism. Behavioral and Brain Sciences. Published online 2025:1-42.
doi:10.1017/S0140525X25000032)

Main text
The target article by Seth argues against certain forms of computational
functionalism, and makes a strong case that current approaches to artificial
intelligence will not be able to create conscious systems. Seth advocates
biological naturalism instead, emphasising the importance of embedded,
embodied agency and arguing that consciousness may arise from our nature as
living beings with physical bodies. In this we are in agreement. Yet, a critical
piece of Seth’s argument is missing, without which his conclusions are seriously
weakened: the nature and role of the conscious subject.
Seth’s arguments depend on the self—he says conscious experience occurs
for “self-sustaining organisms” that maintain a “dynamic self/other boundary”—

but what he means by “self” is unclear. It does not appear to be a mere organism,
since he rejects biological universalism. It appears conceptually distinct from a
mere body, since he says we experience the self “through, and because of” the
body. The closest description seems to be “living, flesh and blood ‘beast
machines.’” A problem with this description is it treats the self as object, rather
than subject, but the critical form of self with respect to consciousness is surely
the subject (Prinz, 2011).
A subject is a living being with interests (e.g., Kawade, 2009; Jennings, 2020;
Mitchell, 2023). A subject in this sense cannot be simply tacked on to Seth’s
current view. Motivations and interests are not predictions, and subjects are not
Markov blankets; we don’t currently have a way to map one on to the other. Seth
says that “real artificial consciousness might endow a system with its own
interests”, but it seems to us that subjects with interests are conceptually (and
evolutionarily) prior to consciousness. Similarly, he says “conscious contents are
given by the brain’s best guesses,” but does not say to whom they are given.
Without an account of the self as subject it is unclear what features of our
biological system are essential for consciousness, and whether those features
require organic material.
Subjects like us are “enminded,” and not just embodied. Our conscious
experiences are grounded in our own ongoing mentality – in what it’s like to be
us, psychologically. This encompasses all of our memories, attitudes,
dispositions, habits, policies, projects, and interests that make us who we are.
When we experience something consciously, we are experiencing being ourselves
having that experience (Zahavi, 2008). While we can treat ourselves as objects by
taking an objective stance, subjectivity is fundamentally about an irreducible
first-person stance, and this is what is central to consciousness (Ganeri, 2012).
In our view, phenomenal subjectivity rests not on specific computational
functions or particular biological processes or substrates, but rather on relations.
These include relations between (i) subject and world, (ii) levels of information
processing within the subject, and (iii) “temporal parts” of the subject (past and
future selves).
Unlike most current AI systems, a conscious agent must be able to both perceive
the world around it and act on it. Without this interactive relation, all that exists
is passive and abstract information processing (Dewey, 1896). By contrast, in
living organisms, perception is highly selective and entails actively making sense
of what is out in the world, in a way that allows the organism to figure out what
it should do about it (Mitchell, 2023). Indeed, there is good evidence that
conscious perception is necessarily structured through the activity of attention
(e.g. Jennings, 2015).

These capacities are underpinned by neural and cognitive architectures that can
also be defined in terms of internal functional relations. With respect to
consciousness, these involve a functional control hierarchy, with higher levels
operating over representations of (beliefs about) objects in the world, internal
states, motivations, moods, goals, and other elements of cognition. These kinds of
relations can be described (and formalised, e.g., in the tradition of cybernetics) in
abstract terms, without relying on either specific computational
implementations or bespoke biological substrates.
The question then is what kinds of relations will go beyond cybernetic functions
and give rise to or entail subjective experience? The answer, of course, is not
known, but we can still ask what form we expect it to take. Some candidate
elements of subjectivity include the organism being able to distinguish itself
from the world (Jennings, 2020), being able to track its own internal states
(Nikolova et al., 2022), having valenced internal signals that guide action
(Godfrey-Smith, 2020; Cleeremans & Tallon-Baudry, 2022), being able to track
the consequences of its actions (Jékely et al., 2021), making predictions about
itself and the world (as Seth notes), and having a “phenomenal self-model”
(Metzinger, 2004). Conscious subjects should have internal representations that
stand in exploitable relation to things in the world (Shea, 2018), but also some
sort of reflexivity that imbues these representations with “mineness”.
Often overlooked in these discussions, however, is that living organisms have
temporal extension. We live in and through time, and cannot be reduced, as
selves, to a single temporal moment (Husserl 1964; Mitchell, 2023). All living
organisms are beings that (literally) incorporate traces of their past experience
to better propagate themselves into the future (Potter & Mitchell, 2025). In more
complex organisms selfhood is defined by continuity through time of both the
physical and the psychological—of us, as persons. We bring our personal history
to bear in our interactions with the world. We are actively seeking, filtering,
predicting, and interpreting sensory information in inherently subjective, goaloriented ways.
Thus, rather than drawing comparisons and contrasts between biological and
artificial systems, we can see them both as particular instantiations of a more
abstract set of relational principles. We can zoom out and view consciousness as
a “mode of being” (Ginsburg & Jablonka, 2020); specifically, of being a subject
and a self. There seems little reason in principle to think that the relations
underpinning subjectivity and selfhood could not be operationalized and
implemented in many different ways in many different substrates, including
artificial entities. Given the right mode of being in the world, it might very well
be like something to be such a system.
