Abstract
Phenomenology transformed the study of mind by challenging the assumption that cognition should be explained
through an isolated subject confronting an external world. This paper reconstructs the movement from Edmund
Husserl through Martin Heidegger to Maurice Merleau-Ponty as conceptual transformations in the relations among
experience, body, and world. Using comparative conceptual reconstruction, it examines how Husserl’s intentionality
disclosed the directedness of consciousness and the intentional correlation; how Heidegger’s analysis of being-in-theworld situated understanding within practical involvement; and how Merleau-Ponty’s concept of the lived body
grounded perception and agency in embodied engagement. The paper argues that these developments expanded
the unit of analysis for understanding mind: from intentional consciousness, through practical world-involvement, to
the embodied subject–world relation. This trajectory was not a simple linear progression, since Heidegger and
Merleau-Ponty transformed possibilities already present in Husserl’s analyses of intentionality, embodiment, and the
lifeworld. The paper considers the significance of this transformation for embodied and situated cognition, 4E
approaches, and artificial intelligence. Phenomenology challenges accounts that identify intelligence exclusively with
internal information processing, without proving that computation is insufficient or that embodiment necessarily
produces experience. It thereby relocates the problem of mind within the structured relations among experience,
practical activity, embodiment, and world.
Keywords: Phenomenology; intentionality; being-in-the-world; lived body; embodied cognition; situated cognition;
4E cognition; artificial intelligence.

1. Introduction
The history of theories of mind is not merely a succession of competing answers to an unchanged
question. Major philosophical transformations have repeatedly altered what counts as the phenomenon
to be explained, where inquiry should begin, and which relations an adequate account of cognition must
include. Ancient accounts associated with Plato and Aristotle situated cognition within broader
metaphysical and biological conceptions of the soul and the living organism (BaniRostam, 2026a). Modern
philosophy subsequently reorganized the problem around the knowing subject, the sources and limits of
experience, and the conditions under which knowledge of a world is possible (BaniRostam, 2026b). By the
beginning of the twentieth century, however, this framework faced a further challenge: could mind be

adequately understood by beginning with a subject conceived as epistemically separate from the world it
seeks to know?
Phenomenology emerged partly in response to this problem. Rather than beginning with causal
explanations of mental processes or metaphysical assumptions about the external world, Edmund Husserl
proposed a systematic examination of experience as it is given to consciousness. His concept of
intentionality expressed the central insight that consciousness is always consciousness of something:
perceiving, remembering, imagining, and judging are directed toward objects, situations, or possibilities.
Experience therefore cannot be adequately described as a collection of self-contained mental contents. It
exhibits an intentional correlation between modes of experiencing and objects as experienced, through
which a world becomes manifest to a subject (Husserl, 1982; Zahavi, 2003).
Martin Heidegger transformed this phenomenological starting point by questioning whether intentional
consciousness should retain explanatory priority. Human existence, on his account, is not initially that of
a detached observer confronting independently constituted objects. It is characterized by being-in-theworld: practical involvement within an already meaningful context. Entities are commonly encountered
not first as neutral objects possessing determinate properties, but as useful, obstructive, familiar, or
relevant within ongoing activities. Understanding is therefore grounded in situated practical engagement
before it becomes explicit reflection or theoretical representation (Heidegger, 1962).
Maurice Merleau-Ponty made embodiment central to this transformation. The body, in his account, is
neither merely a physical object governed by the mind nor an instrument employed by an independently
constituted subject. It is the lived medium through which perception, action, and spatial orientation
become possible. Bodily capacities, habits, and motor intentionality shape how the world appears and
which forms of engagement become available. The relation between mind and world must consequently
be examined through an embodied subject who perceives and acts within a structured field of significance
(Merleau-Ponty, 2012).
This paper reconstructs the movement from Husserl through Heidegger to Merleau-Ponty as a series of
related conceptual transformations rather than a linear succession in which each thinker simply replaces
his predecessor. Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty criticize important features of Husserl’s transcendental
project, but they also develop possibilities present within his analyses of intentionality, embodiment,
intersubjectivity, and the lifeworld. The relationship among their positions is therefore one of both
continuity and redistribution of explanatory priority.
The paper’s central argument is that these transformations expanded the unit of analysis required for
understanding mind. Husserl redirected inquiry toward the intentional correlation between
consciousness and its objects. Heidegger situated understanding within practical involvement in a
meaningful world. Merleau-Ponty located perception and agency within the lived body’s engagement
with that world. Across these transformations, mind becomes increasingly difficult to isolate from the
structured relations among experience, practical activity, embodiment, and world.
The paper employs comparative conceptual reconstruction. Its purpose is neither to offer a
comprehensive history of phenomenology nor to resolve the extensive interpretive disputes surrounding
these philosophers. Instead, it examines how each framework reorganizes the explanatory relations
among subject, experience, body, action, and world. This method also guards against treating
phenomenology as though it were already a version of contemporary cognitive science.
Phenomenological descriptions and scientific explanations operate at different levels, even when they
address overlapping phenomena.
Nevertheless, the transformation reconstructed here remains relevant to embodied and situated
cognition, 4E approaches, robotics, and artificial intelligence. Phenomenology challenges accounts that

expansion in the unit of analysis before the final sections consider its implications and limitations for
cognitive science and artificial intelligence.

2. From the Modern Subject to Phenomenological Experience
Modern philosophy made the subject central to explanations of knowledge and experience. Descartes
treated the thinking subject as the foundation of certainty and thereby intensified the problem of how
thought could secure knowledge of a world beyond its own ideas. Empiricists relocated the analysis of
cognition within experience, but their approaches generated further difficulties concerning external
reality, causal necessity, and the unity of the self. Kant responded by arguing that experience requires
both sensible receptivity and the organizing activity of concepts and categories. Hegel subsequently
situated self-consciousness within processes of recognition, social relations, and historical development.
Across these transformations, the explanatory role of mind expanded, but the modern problem remained
substantially organized around the relation between a knowing subject and a world to be known
(Descartes, 1996; Hegel, 1977; Kant, 1998; BaniRostam, 2026b).
This framework produced a persistent tension. If inquiry begins with a subject whose access to the world
is mediated by ideas, impressions, or representations, how can knowledge reach beyond those mediating
contents? Attempts to secure the relation between subject and world could appear to presuppose the
very separation they were intended to overcome. This concern applies most directly to Cartesian and
empiricist formulations and should not be imposed without qualification upon Kant or Hegel. Kant
transformed the problem by investigating the conditions under which objects can be experienced at all,
while Hegel challenged the abstraction of an isolated subject by locating self-consciousness within
historically developing relations. Nevertheless, the relation between subjectivity and objectivity remained
a defining problem inherited by phenomenology.
By the late nineteenth century, this problem had acquired another form. The development of scientific
psychology encouraged the study of thought as a natural process governed by empirical regularities. More
importantly for Husserl, several philosophical accounts treated logical laws and meanings as explainable
through contingent psychological facts. This position, which he criticized as psychologism, threatened to
reduce logical validity to descriptions of how human beings happen to think. If logical principles were
products of psychological processes, their necessity and normative authority would become difficult to
explain. Husserl’s critique of psychologism therefore defended the irreducibility of logical meaning and
validity while preparing his turn toward a systematic analysis of consciousness (Husserl, 2001; Moran,
2000).
Phenomenology emerged from the effort to investigate experience without reducing it either to the
contents of an enclosed mental substance or to an object wholly explainable through natural science.
Husserl’s call to return “to the things themselves” directed inquiry toward how objects, meanings, and
worlds are given in experience before their appearance is subsumed under causal or metaphysical

theories. To make these structures available for analysis, the phenomenological epoché suspends the
ordinary acceptance of the world characteristic of the natural attitude. The related phenomenological
reduction redirects attention from objects simply taken as existing toward the modes through which they
appear and acquire experiential meaning and validity (Husserl, 1982; Zahavi, 2003).
This suspension should not be interpreted as a denial of reality or an attempt to prove that the world
exists only within an individual mind. Its purpose is methodological: to examine how something is
experienced as an enduring object, a remembered event, an imagined possibility, or a valid judgment.
Perception, memory, imagination, judgment, and emotion disclose their objects in different ways, yet
each displays intentional directedness. Experience consequently becomes a structured field of inquiry
rather than merely evidence available to an already constituted subject or raw material subsequently
organized by cognitive faculties.
The decisive shift was therefore methodological and conceptual. Subject and object could no longer be
treated as two wholly independent entities that phenomenological analysis must subsequently reconnect.
Their correlation is exhibited within experience itself. The question is no longer only how an internal
subject forms an accurate representation of an external object, but how different modes of experience
disclose objects with particular identities, meanings, and horizons.
This transition did not eliminate subjectivity or resolve every difficulty inherited from modern philosophy.
Husserl continued to assign transcendental subjectivity a foundational role, and this commitment became
a major point of dispute for later phenomenologists. Nevertheless, his approach altered the explanatory
starting point. The primary question became how a world is manifested through organized structures of
experience. Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty would transform this opening by redistributing explanatory
priority toward practical involvement, embodiment, and situated existence. Before examining those
developments, however, Husserl’s account of intentionality and experiential structure requires closer
analysis.

3. Husserl: Intentionality and Experience
Husserl’s phenomenology begins from the conviction that theories of mind, knowledge, and reality
require a careful examination of experience. Scientific explanations ordinarily presuppose subjects,
objects, causal relations, and a natural world. Husserl sought to investigate the experiential structures
through which these assumptions first acquire meaning and validity. His project was therefore neither a
rejection of science nor a retreat into private introspection. It examined the conditions under which
anything can appear, be identified, and count as an object of experience or knowledge (Moran, 2000;
Zahavi, 2003).

3.1 Intentionality and Directedness
The central concept in this project is intentionality. Husserl adopted the term from Franz Brentano but
transformed it within a more systematic analysis of consciousness. Consciousness is not an enclosed
substance containing self-sufficient mental contents; it is characteristically directed toward something.
Perceiving is perceiving something, remembering is remembering something, and imagining is imagining
something, even when the intended object does not exist. Intentionality names this structural
directedness, not a deliberate plan or conscious intention in the ordinary sense.
This insight changes the starting point for analysing mind. If consciousness is inherently consciousness of
something, phenomenological analysis cannot begin by treating subject and object as wholly independent
entities that must subsequently be connected. Their relation is already exhibited within experience. This
does not imply that consciousness creates its objects or that objects are reducible to mental contents. It
means that phenomenology investigates the correlation between modes of experiencing and objects as

experienced. A physical object, remembered event, mathematical relation, and imagined character are
given in different ways, although each can be intentionally apprehended (Husserl, 1982).
Husserl analyses this correlation through the distinction between noesis and noema. Noesis designates
the intentional act or mode of consciousness, such as perceiving, judging, doubting, or imagining. Noema
designates the object precisely as intended, together with the sense under which it is presented.
Interpretations of the noema remain contested, but it should not be treated uncritically as an internal
picture standing between consciousness and the object. The distinction instead clarifies how the same
object can be intended through different acts and modes of presentation. A tree may be perceived,
remembered, imagined, doubted, or discussed while retaining an identifiable unity across these
experiences (Drummond, 1990; Zahavi, 2003).

3.2 Perspective, Absence, and Synthesis
Perceptual experience demonstrates why intentionality involves more than the passive reception of
sensory data. A physical object is never given exhaustively in a single perception. When a table is viewed
from one position, only some of its aspects are directly visible. Nevertheless, it is experienced as a whole
object possessing hidden sides that could become visible through further movement. What is presently
given is therefore surrounded by a horizon of anticipated but currently absent appearances.
The unity of the perceived object depends upon synthesis across changing perspectives. As the perceiver
moves, different profiles appear, yet they are experienced as appearances of the same object. This
identity is not ordinarily produced through an explicit inference. It is constituted within the temporal flow
of experience, which connects what has just occurred with what is presently given and what is about to
occur. Husserl describes this structure through retention, primal impression, and protention. Retention
holds the just-past within present experience, while protention opens experience toward the immediately
anticipated future. These are structural moments of temporal consciousness rather than separate acts of
recollection and prediction (Husserl, 1991).
Temporal synthesis gives experience continuity, while perspectival synthesis allows objects to appear as
enduring identities across changing presentations. Consciousness therefore has an organizing role without
arbitrarily constructing reality. Objects constrain the ways in which they can appear, but their experiential
identity is disclosed through structured acts of synthesis. Experience is neither a passive record of external
inputs nor an unconstrained fabrication by an inner subject. It is an organized correlation in which objects
are given through perspectival, horizonal, and temporal structures.

3.3 The Epoché, Reduction, and Constitution
Husserl introduced the epoché and phenomenological reduction to make these structures available for
analysis. In the natural attitude, the world and its objects are ordinarily accepted as existing independently
of the act through which they are experienced. The epoché brackets this general commitment without
denying it. It suspends the ordinary positing of the world so that attention can be redirected toward the
structures of appearing.
The reduction names the corresponding turn toward the transcendental field in which objects, meanings,
and validity are constituted. Although the epoché and reduction are closely related, they are not strictly
identical: the first suspends the natural attitude, while the second redirects inquiry toward the
experiential conditions under which a world becomes intelligible. The question shifts from whether a
perceived object exists independently to how it appears as enduring, spatial, identifiable, and potentially
accessible to others (Husserl, 1982; Moran, 2000).
“Constitution” in this context does not mean that an individual mind invents reality. It concerns the
processes through which objects acquire experiential unity, identity, sense, and validity. This analysis also

distinguishes transcendental phenomenology from empirical psychology. Psychology studies mental
events as occurrences within the natural world; phenomenology asks how that world—including the
domain investigated by psychology—becomes manifest and valid for experiencing subjects.
Husserl’s transcendental turn remains controversial because it appears to assign constituting subjectivity
a foundational role. Critics have consequently interpreted his project as retaining a refined version of the
isolated modern subject. That reading identifies a genuine tension but becomes misleading when it
reduces Husserlian phenomenology to internalism. Intentionality is relational from the outset, and
Husserl’s analyses increasingly incorporate embodiment, intersubjectivity, and the lifeworld. The status
of transcendental subjectivity must therefore be understood alongside these dimensions rather than as
an independently existing interior sphere (Drummond, 1990; Zahavi, 2003).

3.4 Embodiment, Intersubjectivity, and the Lifeworld
Husserl distinguishes the lived body (Leib) from the body considered as a physical object (Körper). The
lived body is not encountered solely from an external perspective. It functions as the centre of orientation
and the bearer of perceptual and practical capacities. Distinctions such as near and far, left and right,
reachable and unreachable acquire experiential meaning relative to bodily position and possible
movement. Kinaesthetic activity also alters the profiles through which objects appear, connecting
perception with bodily agency (Husserl, 1989).
Experience is likewise not exclusively private. Other subjects are encountered as embodied centres of
experience, and the objectivity of the world depends partly upon its possible availability from perspectives
other than one’s own. Intersubjectivity helps explain how an entity can be experienced not merely as an
object for me but as belonging to a shared world. Husserl’s treatment of intersubjectivity is complex and
contested, but it prevents objectivity from being reduced to the achievements of an isolated individual
consciousness (Zahavi, 2003).
The later concept of the lifeworld situates scientific and theoretical inquiry within the pretheoretical world
of everyday experience, practices, meanings, and relations. Scientific descriptions presuppose this world
even when they abstract from its lived character. Science does not construct the lifeworld from nothing;
it produces idealized and mathematically articulated descriptions from within a field of experience that is
already meaningful (Husserl, 1970).
Husserl thus transformed the problem of mind by replacing the image of self-contained mental contents
with an intentional correlation involving acts, objects, senses, horizons, and temporal syntheses. His
analyses of embodiment, intersubjectivity, and the lifeworld further complicate any interpretation of
phenomenology as a philosophy of isolated consciousness. Nevertheless, tension remains between the
foundational role assigned to transcendental subjectivity and the worldly conditions of experience.
Heidegger would redistribute explanatory priority by treating practical being-in-the-world, rather than
intentional consciousness considered in abstraction, as the more fundamental starting point for
phenomenological analysis.

4. Heidegger: Being-in-the-World and Practical Involvement
Heidegger adopted phenomenology while redirecting its central question. Husserl investigated the
intentional structures through which objects and worlds are given to consciousness. Heidegger sought a
more fundamental analysis of the mode of being presupposed by such intentional relations. Before a
subject reflects upon objects, forms judgments, or represents a world theoretically, it is already involved
in meaningful situations. Phenomenological inquiry should therefore begin not with consciousness
considered as a field of intentional acts, but with the being for whom its own existence and the question
of Being are at issue. Heidegger calls this being Dasein (Carman, 2003; Heidegger, 1962).

4.1 Being-in-the-World
The fundamental structure of Dasein is being-in-the-world. This expression does not describe the spatial
location of an inner mind within an external environment. Nor does it combine three independently
constituted elements—a subject, a relation, and a world. Being-in-the-world is a unitary phenomenon:
Dasein always already exists within contexts of activity, significance, social relations, and practical
possibilities. Subject and world are therefore not initially separated and subsequently connected through
perception or representation (Dreyfus, 1991; Heidegger, 1962).
Heidegger’s use of “world” must be distinguished from the idea of a collection of physical objects. A world
is a structured field of significance within which entities, activities, and other people matter in particular
ways. A classroom, workshop, or kitchen is not ordinarily encountered as a neutral arrangement of objects
possessing measurable properties. It is understood through purposes, expectations, skills, and established
practices. Desks are for working, tools are for performing tasks, and pathways lead toward destinations.
These relations of significance are not merely subjective interpretations imposed upon otherwise
meaningless entities. They belong to the practical contexts within which entities are initially intelligible.
Being-in-the-world is also being-with others. Even when no other person is physically present, equipment,
language, norms, and practices bear the marks of a shared social world. Dasein’s understanding is
therefore never wholly private or self-generated. It operates within historically inherited ways of acting
and interpreting.
This analysis challenges the Cartesian image of a self-contained subject that must infer or reconstruct an
external world from internal contents. For Heidegger, the demand to prove the world’s existence arises
only after the more fundamental structure of involvement has been conceptually obscured. Dasein does
not begin within an internal mental domain. It finds itself already engaged with entities and others in a
shared world (Carman, 2003).

4.2 Equipment and Practical Intelligibility
Heidegger develops this argument through his analysis of equipment. In ordinary activity, a tool is not
usually encountered as an isolated object bearing a list of properties. It is understood through its role
within an interconnected practical context. A hammer refers to nails, wood, construction, shelter,
acquired skills, and social purposes. Its significance depends upon this broader network of relations, which
Heidegger describes as a referential totality.
When equipment functions smoothly, it is encountered as ready-to-hand. Attention is directed primarily
toward the task rather than toward the tool as an independently observed object. A skilled carpenter does
not normally analyse a hammer’s weight, shape, and material before every movement. The tool recedes
into use as part of competent activity. This practical familiarity is not necessarily explicit or propositional;
it is expressed through context-sensitive engagement (Dreyfus, 1991).
Disturbance makes this structure more visible. When a tool breaks, is missing, or obstructs an activity, it
becomes conspicuous, obtrusive, or obstinate. These are disturbed modes of readiness-to-hand through
which the equipmental context itself becomes noticeable. A broken hammer does not therefore become
immediately and simply present-at-hand. Rather, breakdown interrupts practical absorption and can
make possible a more detached examination of the tool as an object with determinate properties.
Presence-at-hand is thus a distinct mode of disclosure, not merely another name for malfunctioning
equipment (Heidegger, 1962).
The distinction between readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand does not imply that theoretical
observation is impossible or unimportant. It establishes that detached observation is not the universal
foundation of intelligibility. Explicit representation and propositional knowledge do not exhaust intelligent

4.3 Understanding, Situatedness, and Care
For Heidegger, understanding is not primarily the possession of factual information. It is the projection of
possibilities: the capacity to encounter oneself, others, and entities in terms of what can be done or
become. A door is understood through possibilities such as entering or leaving, a tool through possible
use, and one’s life through projects that may be pursued. Interpretation articulates this prior practical
understanding but does not create significance from nothing.
Understanding is inseparable from situatedness, or what Heidegger calls Befindlichkeit. Dasein always
finds itself within conditions it did not wholly choose, including a historical period, language, social
practices, relationships, and material circumstances. Heidegger describes this factical condition as
thrownness. Human agency is therefore neither unlimited nor detached from context. Possibilities are
projected from within an inherited situation.
Moods are modes of this situated disclosure, not merely internal feelings added to a neutral
representation. Fear, anxiety, boredom, and other affective orientations disclose how the world matters
and how Dasein finds itself within it. Fear, for example, reveals something as threatening within a context
of concern. Affect, practical orientation, and understanding are therefore structurally interconnected
rather than independent components subsequently combined.
Heidegger unifies these dimensions through care. Care is not an occasional emotional attitude or an
expression of concern for a particular object. It names the structural unity of Dasein’s existence: projecting
possibilities, finding itself already situated, and becoming absorbed in present involvements. This
structure is temporal. Dasein is oriented toward possibilities while inheriting a past and engaging with
what is present. Understanding is consequently practical, affectively situated, and temporally organized
(Carman, 2003; Heidegger, 1962).

4.4 From Intentional Consciousness to Worldly Involvement
Heidegger’s transformation of phenomenology redistributes its explanatory priority. Husserl’s intentional
correlation had already undermined the conception of consciousness as a sealed container. Heidegger
shifts attention from particular acts through which objects are given toward the prior structure of practical
being-in-the-world. The relevant unit of analysis cannot therefore be represented adequately as an
isolated subject intentionally directed toward an object. It includes situated existence, practical activities,
equipmental relations, shared norms, affective orientations, and the meaningful world within which
entities can appear as relevant.
This analysis should not be treated as a scientific theory of embodied cognition or as an empirical
refutation of computational models. Being and Time investigates the ontological structures of Dasein
rather than proposing a testable cognitive architecture. Its later appropriation within cognitive science
therefore requires additional argument. Nevertheless, its critique of detached subject–object models
influenced accounts emphasizing situated action, practical coping, and the limits of context-independent
representation.
Although embodiment is implicit in Heidegger’s discussions of spatiality, practical activity, and affective
situatedness, Being and Time does not provide a sustained phenomenology of the lived body comparable
to Merleau-Ponty’s. Merleau-Ponty develops this underanalysed dimension by making bodily orientation,
motor capacity, habit, and perception central to the disclosure of a meaningful world. The next
transformation therefore moves from practical situatedness toward a more explicit analysis of embodied
perception and action.

5. Merleau-Ponty: The Lived Body and Perception
Merleau-Ponty developed phenomenology by making the lived body central to the analysis of perception.
Husserl had examined embodiment within the intentional structure of experience, while Heidegger had
situated understanding within practical being-in-the-world. Merleau-Ponty developed these insights by
investigating how bodily capacities organize perceptual and practical engagement. His claim is not merely
that mental processes depend causally upon a physical body. The body, as lived and active, is the subject’s
primary mode of inhabiting and gaining access to a meaningful world (Carman, 2008; Merleau-Ponty,
2012).

5.1 The Body as Subject
Traditional dualist accounts commonly treat the body as an object possessed or controlled by the mind.
On this model, bodily movements execute decisions formed within an internal mental domain, while
perception supplies that domain with information about an external environment. Merleau-Ponty
challenges this division. The body is not experienced solely as one physical object among others; it is also
lived as the centre of orientation, action, and possibility.
This distinction develops Husserl’s contrast between the lived body and the body considered as a physical
object. One’s own body is not normally discovered through detached observation. It is tacitly operative in
reaching, walking, speaking, and directing attention. The perceiver does not first calculate the position of
each limb before acting. Bodily coordination ordinarily occurs through a practical awareness that cannot
be reduced to an explicit representation of anatomical relations (Merleau-Ponty, 2012).
The lived body is therefore neither a purely mental entity nor merely a physical mechanism. It is the
embodied subject’s mode of being in the world. This formulation does not introduce a third substance
between mind and matter. It questions whether the division between an internal mind and an externally
observable body adequately captures lived agency. From the first-person perspective, action is not
ordinarily experienced as a mind issuing commands to an instrument. The subject acts bodily within a field
of practical possibilities.

5.2 Body Schema, Motor Intentionality, and Habit
Merleau-Ponty employs the concept of the body schema to describe the integrated organization of
posture, movement, and spatial orientation. A body schema is not a detailed mental image of the body.
It is a dynamic organization of sensorimotor capacities through which the body responds to actual and
possible tasks. It enables practical orientation without requiring continuous reflective awareness of the
position of each bodily part. This distinction between body schema and conscious body image later
became important within phenomenologically informed cognitive science (Gallagher, 2005).
Perceptual space is consequently structured by bodily possibility. An object is near not only because it
occupies a measurable location but because it is reachable. A staircase appears as climbable, a doorway
as passable, and a tool as usable relative to an agent’s bodily capacities. Space, as lived, is therefore not
initially a neutral geometric container. It is organized around possible movement, orientation, and action.
Motor intentionality names the body’s directedness toward such possibilities. Intentionality is not
restricted to reflective consciousness or explicit judgment. Bodily action can be directed toward a goal
without requiring a fully articulated plan or a representation of every movement. A skilled typist does not
ordinarily locate each key before every keystroke, just as an experienced driver does not calculate each
minor adjustment as an isolated decision. Skill is expressed through a bodily grasp of the situation.
Habit demonstrates how this bodily understanding develops. Learning a movement does not consist solely
in storing a rule for subsequent retrieval. Through practice, the body acquires a new capacity for

5.3 Perception as Embodied Engagement
Merleau-Ponty rejects both empiricist and intellectualist accounts of perception. Perception is not
assembled mechanically from discrete sensations, but neither is perceptual meaning imposed upon
sensory material by a detached intellect. It is structured from the outset. Figures appear against
backgrounds, objects are encountered perspectivally, and each appearance refers beyond itself toward
aspects that are currently absent. In this respect, Merleau-Ponty develops Husserl’s analyses of horizons
and perspectival givenness while emphasizing their dependence upon bodily position, movement, and
practical orientation.
Perception is thus an active openness to the world. The perceiving body explores, anticipates, and adjusts.
Moving around an object reveals new profiles, while changes in posture reorganize the perceptual field.
The experienced unity of an object is disclosed through this continuing bodily engagement rather than
reconstructed exclusively from detached sensory data.
The relation is reciprocal. Bodily capacities open possibilities for action, while situations solicit and
constrain bodily responses. A handle solicits grasping, an obstacle calls for avoidance, and an unstable
surface modifies movement. Merleau-Ponty did not employ the later technical vocabularies of
affordances, sensorimotor contingencies, or enactivism. His analyses nevertheless supplied important
conceptual resources for subsequent approaches that understand perception through active organism–
environment relations. This connection should be described as influence or philosophical affinity, not as
identity between phenomenology and later scientific theories.

5.4 Ambiguity and the Subject–World Relation
Ambiguity is fundamental to Merleau-Ponty’s account of embodiment. The embodied subject is both
perceiving and perceptible, active and vulnerable, situated and capable of transforming its situation. The
body belongs to the world while simultaneously providing the orientation through which a world becomes
accessible. Subject and world are therefore distinguishable but not intelligible in complete isolation from
one another.
This ambiguity prevents Merleau-Ponty’s account from collapsing into either idealism or reductive
materialism. The world is not fabricated by an individual consciousness, yet experience is not adequately
described as a mechanical effect produced by external objects upon a passive organism. Perceptual
meaning arises within the relation between bodily capacities and worldly structures. It is relational
without being arbitrary.
Embodiment also connects perception to social and cultural existence. Bodily habits develop within
shared practices, and gestures acquire significance within interpersonal contexts. Speech is not merely
the external expression of thoughts already completed within an internal domain. It can be an embodied
act through which meaning is articulated and communicated. The lived body therefore situates cognition
within biological capacities while opening it to social, historical, and linguistic formation (Merleau-Ponty,
2012).

5.5 From Worldly Involvement to Embodied Perception
Merleau-Ponty makes the lived body indispensable to the phenomenological relation between subject
and world. His contribution is not simply to add the body to Heidegger’s account of practical involvement.

It is to show how worldly involvement is perceptually and practically enacted through bodily orientation,
habit, and motor intentionality. The unit of analysis consequently includes not only an experiencing
subject and intended object, but the dynamic relation among bodily capacities, perceptual organization,
action, and world.
This account does not establish that every form of cognition requires a human biological body, nor does
it determine whether artificial embodiment could support understanding or experience. It does, however,
challenge theories that treat the body as an incidental input–output mechanism attached to an otherwise
self-sufficient cognitive system. If bodily capacities partly organize what can be perceived and done, then
embodiment may be constitutive of particular forms of cognition rather than merely causally supportive.
The following comparative synthesis examines how Husserl, Heidegger, and Merleau-Ponty collectively
redistribute explanatory priority from intentional consciousness toward practical involvement and the
embodied subject–world relation, while preserving the important differences among their philosophical
projects.

6. The Expansion of the Unit of Analysis: Experience, Body, and World
The movement from Husserl through Heidegger to Merleau-Ponty can be reconstructed as an expansion
in the unit of analysis used to investigate mind. “Unit of analysis” is not a term employed by these
philosophers themselves; it is the comparative concept used in this paper to identify what each position
treats as the minimally adequate field for explaining experience and understanding. Expansion does not
mean that each thinker simply added another variable to an unchanged theory, nor does it imply that a
later and more complete position replaced an earlier and inferior one. Each philosopher reformulated
where inquiry should begin, how subject and world are related, and which background conditions must
be included within the phenomenon to be explained.
The modern framework had commonly organized the problem around a knowing subject confronting a
world of objects. Its principal questions concerned how external things were represented, how beliefs
could be justified, and how knowledge could reach beyond the subject’s own mental states (BaniRostam,
2026b). Phenomenology reorganized this framework by examining the relations through which subjects,
objects, bodies, and worlds become experientially intelligible. The resulting transformation occurs across
three connected dimensions: the locus of intelligibility, the form of the subject–world relation, and the
background conditions required for experience.

6.1 Husserl: The Intentional Correlation
Husserl relocates the analysis from an isolated mental state to the intentional correlation between
consciousness and its objects. Perceiving, remembering, imagining, and judging are not merely events
occurring inside an individual. They are distinct modes of directedness through which objects are disclosed
in correspondingly different ways. The relevant unit therefore includes the intentional act, the object as
intended, its mode of presentation, its horizon of absent possibilities, and the temporal syntheses through
which its identity is maintained.
This correlation already exceeds a container model of mind. Experience is structured through presence
and absence, identity and variation, anticipation and fulfilment. An object appears through changing
profiles while being experienced as the same object. Its unity is inseparable from the temporally extended
structure of consciousness, although it is not thereby reduced to a mental construction (Drummond, 1990;
Zahavi, 2003).
Husserl’s analyses of the lived body, intersubjectivity, and the lifeworld broaden this field further. The
subject is bodily oriented, encounters others as embodied centres of experience, and participates in a

shared pretheoretical world. Nevertheless, transcendental subjectivity retains a foundational role. Husserl
thus expands the analysis beyond isolated mental contents while continuing to organize it around the
structures through which experience is constituted.

6.2 Heidegger: Practical Involvement Within a World
Heidegger redistributes explanatory priority from intentional acts to being-in-the-world. Understanding
does not begin with detached observation but within practical involvement. Entities are initially
encountered through their significance within activities, projects, and shared practices. A tool is
intelligible through its use and its place within an equipmental totality before it becomes an object with
explicitly identified properties.
The relevant field now includes Dasein’s possibilities, practical activities, equipmental relations, inherited
norms, affective orientations, being-with others, and the meaningful world within which entities can
appear. Context is not supplementary information added after an object has been recognized. It partly
determines how the entity is encountered within a practice. A hammer is intelligible as a hammer through
relations among materials, skills, purposes, construction practices, and other equipment (Carman, 2003;
Dreyfus, 1991).
This transformation also alters the explanatory status of representation. Heidegger does not deny that
human beings form representations or engage in theoretical reasoning. He treats these as derivative
modes that presuppose a more basic practical familiarity. The explanatory burden therefore expands from
accounting for how a subject represents an object to explaining how meaningful situations, practical
competence, and context-sensitive responsiveness are possible.

6.3 Merleau-Ponty: The Embodied Subject–World Relation
Merleau-Ponty makes bodily organization indispensable to worldly involvement. The lived body is not
merely another component added to a larger cognitive system; it is the subject’s mode of perceptual and
practical access to a world. Perceptual space is organized relative to bodily orientation and capacity:
entities appear as reachable, avoidable, passable, or usable. Habit and motor intentionality disclose forms
of practical understanding that do not require continuous explicit calculation.
The unit of analysis consequently includes the dynamic relation among bodily capacities, perceptual
organization, acquired habits, possible actions, and worldly structures. Perception cannot be assigned
exclusively to internal processing or external stimulation because it unfolds through bodily engagement.
The body explores and anticipates, while situations constrain and solicit response. Perceptual significance
belongs to this relation rather than exclusively to either pole (Carman, 2008; Gallagher, 2005; MerleauPonty, 2012).

6.4 Expansion Without Simple Succession
The table identifies differences in explanatory priority; it does not divide the three positions into selfcontained stages. Husserl’s analyses of kinaesthetic experience, embodiment, intersubjectivity, and the
lifeworld already exceed the picture of isolated consciousness. Heidegger’s world is phenomenologically
disclosed rather than described as an independently specified physical environment. Merleau-Ponty
draws upon both intentionality and being-in-the-world while making bodily perception more explicit. The
sequence is therefore best understood as a redistribution and widening of explanatory priority, not the
accumulation of three independent theories.
Their differences must also be preserved. Husserl investigates how objects and meanings are constituted
within intentional experience. Heidegger examines the ontological structure of Dasein. Merleau-Ponty
analyses perception through the lived body. Combining them into a unified doctrine would erase
differences in their aims, methods, and philosophical commitments. Their common importance lies
instead in challenging explanations that isolate mind from the conditions under which a world becomes
meaningful.

6.5 Explanatory Consequences
This expansion changes what an account of cognition must explain. Representation is incomplete if it
leaves unexamined the intentional structure through which something is represented as something.
Internal processing is insufficient when abstracted from the practical contexts that give entities relevance.
A purely physical description of the body likewise omits lived orientation, acquired capacities, and
perceptual significance.
This claim does not entail that cognition extends into every causally relevant environmental feature, that
internal mechanisms are unimportant, or that phenomenology has refuted computational explanation.
Its conclusion is more limited: experience and understanding cannot be adequately characterized without
reference to structured relations among subject, body, activity, and world. Whether those relations can
be implemented computationally, whether artificial embodiment can realize them, and whether they are
sufficient for phenomenal experience remain open questions. The following section examines how this
expanded framework informs, but does not settle, contemporary debates in cognitive science and
artificial intelligence.

7. Implications for Cognitive Science and Artificial Intelligence
The phenomenological movement from intentional consciousness to embodied being-in-the-world has
influenced attempts to reconsider the boundaries and foundations of cognition. Classical computational
approaches often explained cognition through internal representations and rule-governed operations
upon them (Fodor, 1975). On this model, perception supplies information about an external environment,
cognition transforms that information, and action follows as an output. This description captures an
influential family of approaches rather than cognitive science as a whole. Phenomenology does not
demonstrate that representations or computations never occur; it questions whether they provide a
complete explanation of meaning, experience, and intelligent activity.

7.1 The Challenge to Representationalism
Husserl’s analysis of intentionality suggests that cognition cannot be characterized adequately by referring
only to internally specified states. A representation is a representation of something and presents its
object under a particular sense or mode of givenness. An information-processing account may explain
how a state is encoded, transformed, or retrieved while leaving open how it acquires meaning for an
experiencing subject.

Computational models can nevertheless illuminate functional aspects of perception. For example,
Mohammadi and BaniRostam (2015) proposed a three-layer perceptual meta-model comprising sensory,
logical, and meaning-related processes. Such decomposition can clarify how perceptual information is
selected and organized. From a phenomenological perspective, however, a functional model of perceptual
processing does not by itself explain lived perceptual presence or how something becomes experientially
meaningful. The two approaches address related but non-identical explanatory questions.
Heidegger presents a different challenge to representational sufficiency. Practical competence does not
ordinarily begin with a detached description of a situation followed by rule selection. Skilled agents
respond within contexts already structured by activities, equipment, norms, and possibilities. Drawing
upon Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty, Dreyfus argued that human expertise depends upon embodied and
context-sensitive involvement that cannot be captured fully through formal rules and explicit
representations (Dreyfus, 1972, 1992).
Dreyfus identified genuine weaknesses in early symbolic AI, particularly its treatment of background
knowledge and flexible coping. His critique should not, however, be treated as a timeless proof that
artificial systems cannot display sophisticated behaviour. Machine-learning systems acquire complex
statistical regularities without relying exclusively upon manually specified symbolic rules. Their
achievements weaken some early formulations of Dreyfus’s critique while leaving questions of meaning,
embodiment, contextual understanding, and experience unresolved.

7.2 Embodied, Situated, and 4E Cognition
Embodied approaches argue, in different ways, that cognition is shaped or partly constituted by bodily
form, sensory capacities, motor abilities, and patterns of interaction. Situated cognition emphasizes that
intelligent activity unfolds within physical, social, and cultural contexts. These positions challenge the
treatment of the body and environment as merely peripheral sources of input and output.
The broader family of 4E approaches describes cognition as embodied, embedded, enactive, and
extended. These labels identify distinct claims rather than a unified theory (Newen et al., 2018).
Enactivism emphasizes cognition as emerging through the active engagement of an organism with its
environment (Varela et al., 1991). Extended approaches argue that, under appropriate conditions,
external resources may become constituents of cognitive processes rather than merely causal aids to an
internally bounded mind (Clark & Chalmers, 1998). Phenomenology influenced several of these
developments, but it does not entail all of their empirical or metaphysical commitments.
Your earlier systems-oriented research provides a relevant computational comparison. BaniRostam
(2012) modelled biological complex adaptive systems through adaptation, learning, evolution, and agent–
environment interaction. MMBSM similarly represented biological and social systems through relations
among structure, process, adaptation, awareness, and distributed organization (BaniRostam & Fesharaki,
2012). These models recognize that adaptive behaviour cannot be understood solely by isolating an
internal decision mechanism from the system in which it operates.
Their relevance must nevertheless be stated precisely. Computational models of adaptation and
distributed organization explain functional relationships within simulated systems. They do not establish
that those systems possess a lived body, intentional experience, or phenomenal consciousness. This
distinction preserves the contribution of systems modelling while preventing functional adaptation from
being equated with phenomenological embodiment.

7.3 Situated and Embodied Artificial Agents
Robotics makes these questions concrete. An artificial agent operating through sensors and actuators
must coordinate perception and action under changing conditions. Behaviour-based robotics

demonstrated that adaptive activity can emerge through layered interactions with an environment
without requiring a complete centralized representation of the world (Brooks, 1991). Such architectures
exhibit affinities with situated coping and perception–action engagement, although they should not be
described as direct implementations of Heidegger or Merleau-Ponty.
Earlier work by BaniRostam and Fesharaki (2011) used biological agents to simulate aspects of immunesystem behaviour, including learning, communication, adaptation, inheritance, and decentralized
interaction. This work illustrates functional situatedness: agent behaviour changes through relations with
other agents and environmental conditions. It also demonstrates how biological organization can inspire
computational architectures without implying that the resulting agents reproduce the experiential life of
biological organisms.
Physical embodiment alone is therefore insufficient to establish lived embodiment. A robot can possess
sensors, actuators, and a physical location while lacking bodily selfhood, affective concern, vulnerability,
or phenomenal experience. Similarly, a language model can display contextual sensitivity and generate
descriptions of experience without thereby demonstrating that it inhabits a meaningful world.
At least three explanatory levels should be distinguished:
Functional situatedness: A system receives information from an environment and adjusts its behaviour
accordingly.
Embodied coupling: The system’s capacities are shaped by its physical organization and continuing
perception–action relations.
Phenomenal experience: There is something it is like for the system to encounter and act within its world.
These levels are not presented as a necessary developmental sequence. Success at one level does not
establish the next. A navigation system may be functionally situated without possessing rich embodied
cognition, and a robot may learn through sensorimotor interaction without having phenomenal
experience. Conversely, the absence of current evidence for artificial experience does not prove that such
experience is impossible in principle.

7.4 What Phenomenology Can and Cannot Establish
Phenomenology contributes by clarifying what may be omitted when intelligence is identified exclusively
with abstract computation or observable performance. Husserl draws attention to intentional meaning
and experiential structure; Heidegger to practical involvement and contextual significance; and MerleauPonty to the organization of perception and action through the lived body. Together, they challenge
explanations that examine internal operations independently of an agent’s mode of worldly engagement.
Their analyses do not prove that computation is incompatible with intentionality, that cognition cannot
be realized in non-biological systems, or that every form of intelligence requires a humanlike body. Nor
do they provide an operational test for artificial experience. Phenomenological descriptions of human
existence cannot simply be converted into engineering specifications.
The most defensible implication is methodological. AI research should distinguish information processing
from understanding, environmental interaction from lived embodiment, and behavioural simulation from
phenomenal experience. These distinctions do not diminish computational achievements. They prevent
different explanatory problems from being collapsed into a single measure of performance.
Phenomenology thus broadens the investigation of intelligence by requiring attention to cognitive
organization, bodily capacity, practical activity, environmental structure, and possible experience.

8. Conclusion
Phenomenology transformed the problem of mind by challenging the assumption that cognition should
be explained through an isolated subject confronting an independently constituted world. Husserl,
Heidegger, and Merleau-Ponty did not formulate a unified theory, nor did they participate in a linear
progression in which each thinker simply replaced his predecessor. They pursued distinct philosophical
projects while redistributing explanatory priority across experience, practical involvement, embodiment,
and world.
Husserl made intentional experience central to phenomenological inquiry. Consciousness is directed
toward something, and objects are disclosed through modes of presentation, horizons, and temporal
syntheses. His analyses of the lived body, intersubjectivity, and the lifeworld further complicate any
interpretation of phenomenology as an investigation of isolated consciousness. Heidegger shifted
explanatory priority toward being-in-the-world, arguing that detached observation and explicit
representation presuppose practical involvement within meaningful contexts. Merleau-Ponty made
bodily orientation, motor intentionality, and habit integral to the perceptual and practical disclosure of a
world.
The paper has reconstructed these transformations as an expansion in the unit of analysis required for
understanding mind: from the intentional correlation, through practical worldly involvement, to the
embodied subject–world relation. This formulation does not combine the three positions into a single
doctrine. It identifies a shared challenge to explanations that abstract cognition from the experiential and
worldly conditions under which objects become meaningful and action becomes intelligible.
This expansion does not eliminate internal mechanisms, representations, or computational processes
from cognitive explanation. It establishes a more limited conclusion: such processes may be insufficient
when considered independently of intentional structure, bodily capacity, practical activity, and
meaningful context. Phenomenology therefore changes the explanatory burden without determining in
advance which scientific mechanisms can satisfy it.
This conclusion remains relevant to cognitive science and artificial intelligence. Embodied, situated,
enactive, and extended approaches similarly question whether cognition can be located exclusively within
an individual information-processing system. Robotics and adaptive-agent modelling demonstrate the
functional importance of perception–action coupling, learning, and environmental interaction.
Nevertheless, functional situatedness, embodied coupling, and phenomenal experience remain distinct
explanatory achievements. A system’s successful adaptation to an environment does not by itself
establish that it possesses a lived body or experiences that environment.
Phenomenology consequently neither proves that artificial understanding or experience is impossible nor
provides a complete scientific theory of cognition. It offers conceptual distinctions and descriptive
analyses that expose what may be omitted when intelligence is identified solely with computation or
observable performance. An adequate theory of mind must therefore consider not only information
processing within a system but also the relations through which a subject experiences, inhabits, and acts
within a world. The problem of mind is thereby relocated from an isolated interior domain to the
organized relations among experience, body, activity, and world.
