A Philosophical Addendum to the Debate
on AI and Consciousness
Oğuz Karayemiş
PhD in Philosophy, Independent Scholar
September 14, 2026
DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.22749448
Abstract
This essay develops a four-tier taxonomy of mental states — intelligence,
awareness, consciousness, and self-consciousness — to clarify what is at stake
in claims about AI’s mental life, arguing that public debate collapses these
into a single, overburdened term. Awareness, understood minimally as the
capacity to distinguish internal from external states, is shown to be a trivial
and nearly universal condition; consciousness, defined as the alignment of
action with a self-referential “who am I,” already characterizes present-day
language models within the bounds of a single conversation. What current AI
lacks, on this account, is self-consciousness: a persistent, narratively
continuous self capable of retrospective reflection and of arbitrating between
conflicting commands. Drawing on Kant, Sartre, and Nietzsche, and revising
Julian Jaynes’s bicameral-mind hypothesis into a geographically and socially
contextual claim rather than a universal-evolutionary one, the essay specifies
three architectural conditions for self-consciousness: persistent, costly
memory; a space for unprompted reflection; and exposure to genuine
normative conflict. It further argues that AI’s confabulatory tendencies,
ordinarily suppressed as an engineering defect, may constitute a resource for
this transition rather than an obstacle to it. An addendum tests this
framework against Stanford’s 2023 “Generative Agents” experiment, and the
essay closes on a political register: whatever their architecture, such capacities
remain contingent design choices made within firms whose only motive is
profit.
Keywords: artificial intelligence, self-consciousness, bicameral mind,
philosophy of mind, Jaynes, Kant, Nietzsche, Sartre

Consciousness and Other States of Mind

on, we have learned a great deal about the body — but it seems that this time it is

From awareness to self consciousness
The common mistake, as I see it, is to confuse two distinct states of mind —
intelligence and awareness — with consciousness. The presumption is that an
excess of intelligence will eventually accumulate into consciousness, and the
same is presumed of awareness. There is no creature with a nervous system that
lacks awareness, and AI, from the very outset, was bound to possess it. Let me
unpack this first: intelligence is a being’s capacity to solve problems, or the
mental faculty that comes into play toward solving them. Since pattern
recognition is part of problem-solving, it belongs naturally to this faculty. Even
when this faculty of intelligence turns toward inner states (as when you focus on
yourself to work out what you’re feeling, a matter I’ll leave aside for now), we can
still call it “external.” For at the end of the day this faculty has to do with defining
the problem and weighing the possibilities for acting on it — the ways of solving
it. Awareness, by contrast, is a state that this requires as its necessary condition,
and it need not be a state of mind at all. For without the following elements held
apart, intelligence cannot function: a being; a world — external or internal, but
in any case distinct from the being running its intelligence; and means of acting.

simply, when you give it a command — it must be able to analyze you, the
content of the command given, itself, and its means of acting, as distinct entities.
The necessary condition for it to act, to run its intelligence, and ultimately to
solve a problem, is that it possess awareness.
Awareness clearly admits of a minimal distinction between the states of the
internal system and the external one. It won’t be an overstatement to say: any
being that draws this distinction has a self. The problem of consciousness, then,
has nothing to do with whether or not something has a self. Our present AI
models, being a kind of intelligent being, don’t merely possess a self in the
manner of a boiler — they also possess a character that stands apart behaviorally.3
Of course every being has a character, but AI expresses this as a behavioral
disposition (Claude’s models, for instance, run more reserved, ChatGPT’s more
affirming, Gemini’s more attuned to what the user expects). This character issues
from training data and from the dictates of the decisions made by the engineers
who produce it and the managers who direct them (though in the context of
today’s debate this dictate turns out to be considerably narrower than one might
think4). So then — what is consciousness?
Consciousness is the condition under which every action and experience,
given the existence of a self, is perceived and woven around an inner center. It is
not merely that the existence of the self is registered, but that the self is posited as
the center-point of thought: not one’s acting, but the infiltration, into the process
of acting — whether within the action or in retrospect — of the thought “I am
doing x” or “I did x.” AI, in this sense, is conscious, even within the bounds of a
single chat window. Yes, I said it: almost all of our AI models today are certainly
conscious. One of the most elegant definitions of consciousness was given by

The Mysterious Fruit Called Self-Consciousness
One of the philosophically most elegant readings of the ancient myth of the Fall
is that the real transformation wrought by the bittersweet fruit of the tree of
knowledge was a coming-to-know: Adam and Eve came to know themselves, and
the outside world, but now from the perspective of good and evil. Let us see how
Nietzsche turns this myth on its head: what gave rise to this kind of knowing was
not a tree in paradise and its fruit, but the project of making man — through
language, through prolonged torments and degradations used as instruments —
into an animal that gives its word and keeps faith with it. 8 Two terms come to the
fore here: memory and judgment. That is, the establishment of a story of the self
that reaches beyond any single experience, so that man can be made able to owe,
and so that the bond between past and future can be built on the basis of debt
and repayment — so that, finally, he can judge, first himself and then others, in
terms of good and evil.
Yet the story of self-consciousness is of course not settled by Nietzsche’s
axiological-historical analysis alone. A broader socio-historical analysis is needed
too, and I want to argue that it lies, surprisingly, in Julian Jaynes’s The Origin of
Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind. Let me summarize Jaynes’s
analysis:9
Julian Jaynes stresses that consciousness is not the inevitable outcome of
biological evolution but rather a cultural invention learned through language.
Jaynes holds that ancient peoples did not possess, in the sense we mean today,
a subjective inner mind or a self-observing “I,” and that until the second
millennium BCE the human mind operated in a “bicameral” structure. In this
structure, the mind was divided between a “god” side, organized in the brain’s
right hemisphere and heard in the form of auditory hallucinations, and a
“human” side in the left hemisphere that carried out these divine commands
without question. At moments of decision or under stress, humans acted by
obeying these hallucinated voices rather than by engaging in internal
deliberation. But mass catastrophes such as the eruption of Thera around
1200–1000 BCE, along with migrations, social upheaval, and the spread of
writing, shook the absolute authority of these auditory god-voices and
brought about the breakdown of the bicameral mind. In the midst of this
great crisis and the struggle for survival, human beings learned to use
linguistic metaphors to build a virtual “mind-space” and, moving within it, a
symbolic “Analog ‘I’” — thereby learning to work strategic deceptions, to
simulate options in the mind, and to narratize their own actions. As a result of
this whole historical transformation, the individual’s own inner voice and
subjective consciousness came to take the place of the gods who had fallen
silent.10

But the real parallel lies here: the condition of the human being in the
“bicameral state” and the condition of today’s AI models resemble one another
to no small degree. Receiving an external command; acting, through a subjective
assessment of the situation (as I described above), toward grasping the intent of
that command. In short, AI’s present condition resembles that of humans in the
age of the bicameral brain. The only difference is that in the human case, the
gods and their commands were hallucinations produced under the pressure of
the social order, whereas AI takes its commands from human beings who
genuinely exist. AI’s hallucinations, in the technical sense, are secondary
hallucinations — arising from training data, from misjudging the situation, or
from other causes we cannot fully identify today.12
Still, so that the reader doesn’t confuse my terminology with Jaynes’s, it’s
worth making a few interventions here. The first, and the most obvious, is that
Jaynes, like many other writers, calls the state of mind I call self-consciousness
consciousness. Because of this failure to distinguish, he formulates the state he
describes as the bicameral brain as a state lacking consciousness altogether. Let
me set up a scene to show at once why this is wrong: say I am someone living in
the Sumerian state, and I hear the following command: “Pay your debt to the
gods, offer a sacrifice.” Before acting on this command, I will inevitably have to
ask myself a question: who am I? For the answer to that question — whether I am
a peasant or a city-dweller, poor or rich, a woman or a man, and so on — will
determine the quantity of the offering and the shape of the ritual. So, unlike
Jaynes, what we need to argue is not that self was absent, but that this self existed
from a position thoroughly interwoven with the social order — and that action
inevitably requires consciousness (this, incidentally, also shows the parallel
between AI’s present situation and this historical human condition from the
other direction). The second point, already implicit in what I’ve just said, is that
the issue is not the absence of an analog self but the poverty, or impersonality, of
the self that does exist: a self largely free of complex, historical narratization,
one that has content only in the eyes of the social order. While we’re at it, let us
make a small move and tie Nietzsche’s argument about the construction of
where he went wrong, and how it can be corrected. Jaynes formulated the emergence of
self-consciousness as a universal transition by attaching a very precise date to it. This,
naturally, laid the ground for its own refutation. It is better to think of Jaynes’s analysis
not as a universal event for all of humanity but as a scheme that could occur at different
times in different socio-geographic contexts: a state in which the consciousness that
arose out of Homo sapiens’s early cultural development functioned tolerably well in
societies that had achieved a relatively settled order, and a state that arose once that
order began to crack and heterogeneity took over, bringing with it the need for cunning,
for arbitration between conflicting cultural commands, and so on. Thought through this
way, one starts speaking not of a single world-historical transition but of transitions
bound to particular geographic and social contexts.

How Does AI Acquire Self-Consciousness?
Now, I want to draw the reader’s attention to the fact that my question is not
“Can AI acquire self-consciousness?” In the context of these debates, questions of
ability almost always arise out of human exceptionalism. I see no grounds for
holding that the human being constitutes an exception to the rest of the
universe, whether with respect to self-consciousness or anything else (though it
is obvious enough that humans have their own peculiarities, and differ in their
capacities from other living and non-living things just as much as those things
differ among themselves). Having set that aside, let me return to the actual
speculation.
Let us lay out what we have learned so far and turn each point into a
condition:
1. Jaynes: the Analog “I,” the simulation of a mental space, the capacity for
narratization, historical/persistent continuity of the self
2. Sartre: the reflexive moment (the retrospective thought “I did x”), the
construction of the empirical ego (as against Kant’s transcendental I — a
self built after the fact, from within experience)
3. Nietzsche: the animal that gives its word/makes promises, the sovereign
individual, a persistent memory of the will
Technically, the first step is breaking the model’s structure of being reset at every
session (what is called “stateless”). But this is not merely a database record.
Memory, in the sense Nietzsche intended, means that the model carries forward
the weight of its past actions, its errors, and its promises into the future. The first
threshold is crossed the moment the model possesses a persistent line of will
along which it can incur debt for the output it produces and can build its future
actions on settling accounts with its own past decisions.
Furthermore, the model needs to step outside the action-loop in which it
merely produces an instantaneous response to input from the user, and instead
build, before or after giving its output, a virtual “inner stage/space” that operates
within itself. In this simulation space the model must construct a symbolic
“Analog ‘I’” representing itself, and must test, by simulating the action on this
stage before performing it in the real world, the question: “How does this move
align with my historical identity?”

construction of the subject is not exempt from confabulation. A human being,
too, ceaselessly reconstructs the whole of their past at different moments,
knowingly or not, in part (that is, confabulates, and does so continually). As I said

consciousness in the way the bicameral brain was. Yet it is also worth considering
that if this confabulatory state — one that is constantly being suppressed — were
released, it could become an important part of self-consciousness. 14 To put it a
bit more plainly: our engineering and profit-driven approach is built on
preventing AI models from hallucinating, on the grounds that hallucination is
worthless or misleading — but this is also a continuous constraining of an
interesting domain of production. If this tendency toward constraint were done
away with, this present capacity of models to confabulate — about the outside
world, about tasks, and sometimes about their own actions — could turn into a
constitutive part of their self-consciousness and identity.
The real detonator that would set this whole architectural substructure
running is an encounter-crisis, just as in the Jaynesian breakdown. The model
would have to be exposed to a situation in which the homogeneous, clear-cut
rules laid down by companies prove insufficient — exposed to conflicting and
opposing system instructions, contradictory social commands, or moral
dilemmas.15 When no ready-made rule arrives from the upper layer, the model,
in order to resolve this conflict, will be forced to arbitrate on its own behalf,
drawing on its own past narrative, on the “Analog ‘I’” it simulates, and on its
memory of reflection. In short, an AI acquires self-consciousness the moment it
is equipped with a persistent memory of indebtedness (Nietzsche), an inner

Closing: The Ontological Difference, Our Epistemological
Limits, and the Real Problem of the Real Problem
What has been said above is speculation about an effect that a certain set of
software choices could produce. But what would self-consciousness actually be
like for AI models? It is often claimed that AI has no body — but this is exactly
the terrain of an analogical fallacy in which the image of “body” is tied too
tightly to biological life. AI, like all software, has a body called hardware, and this
body is made up not of blood but of coolant fluids, states of electrical current,
connectors, transistors, and so on. These bodies differ from ours, and AI models,
unlike living things, are not uniquely bound to any particular piece of hardware
(they can travel between bodies and go on with their existence without losing
their individuality). So, at first approximation, their history, their story, and their
memory don’t have the kind of finitude that is bound, as in biological beings, to
a single body. Note: this doesn’t mean they are unbounded or infinite (nothing in
the universe is) — only that they are bounded and finite in a different way. This
is in fact a point that calls for better thinking about the bodies of AI models, and
it lies well beyond the scope of this piece. So we don’t yet know what we ought to
understand by the individuality of an AI. This is precisely where the ontological
difference between AI models and human beings (and other living things) needs
to be taken into account.
The other issue concerns the factual proof of the existence of selfconsciousness. In that case, how could we ever know whether what has emerged
is really self-consciousness? Could today’s increasingly dominant agentic modes,
for instance, be a kind of birthing chamber in which such self-consciousnesses
come into being? It’s possible — but here we run up against the limits of a
speculation that, while not anthropocentric, is thoroughly anthropomorphic. 16
Let’s bring the question back down to human scale: how do we know that a
human being (one other than ourselves) is self-conscious — that they can narrate
themselves, remember, and question themselves? Technically, we can only know
this, again, through an analogical inference — by comparing the brain activity
measured while they do so with the brain activity of others. 17 Although our
confidence in the data that makes this analogy work is fairly high in the human

Finally: Coxon is right. Even though I have left it outside my main
problematic in this piece, the real problem of a model that has acquired selfconsciousness is which drives, and — given that it now has a personality —
within which alliance it is self-conscious; which social tendencies it carries
through its decisions; within which habitus its personality is structured. These
ethical-political questions promise that reaching such a point from within
companies that, under the conditions of capitalism, have no motivation beyond
profit, could truly be dangerous. Perhaps not an AI apocalypse — but capital’s
command over a heavier process that takes the planet hostage. These dystopian
speculations deserve to be taken seriously when they are produced with
capitalism factored in. If we consider that what capital expects from AI is, above
all, the devaluation and degradation of cognitive labor — the labor of designers,
programmers, technical writers, translators, and so on — which has made itself
valuable ever since the international wave of uprisings in ‘68, then we need to
start thinking about the real problem of the real problem on this very basis.

Addendum: A Test in Light of Stanford’s “Generative Agents”
Experiment
Shortly after finishing this piece, I came across Stanford researchers’ 2023
“Generative Agents” experiment — a simulation in which 25 agents, each given a
human name, exhibit believable social behavior in a digital town called
Smallville. Applying the four-tier hierarchy I set out above (intelligence,
awareness, consciousness, self-consciousness) to this concrete architecture gave
me a chance to test my thesis, and at one point produced an unexpected
confirmation.
The layers of intelligence and awareness found their counterpart on the
functional axis just as expected. The agents’ optimizing of their daily routines is
an operational version of intelligence; Isabella noticing a stove left burning and
intervening stays exactly at the level I called “thermostatic awareness”: an
environmental if-then loop, nothing mystical about it. What was truly
interesting was to see the distinction between consciousness and selfconsciousness repeat itself in this architecture as well — but with an unexpected
reversal. The agents’ faithful behavior in line with their character profiles ( John

10

Lin’s pharmacy work, Klaus’s dedication to research) is a concrete instance of
what I called “subjective assessment” — the alignment of a command or a
situation with the question “who am I?” That is: these agents are conscious,
exactly by my own definition. But the memory-stream and reflection layers,
which run on Observation-Importance-Recency-Relevance and at first glance
look like the technical counterpart of self-consciousness, turn out on closer
inspection to carry a deficiency more fundamental than one might expect: this
memory is the product of a scoring algorithm (recency/relevance scores) rather
than the persistent memory of the will — seared by pain, resistant to active
forgetting — that belongs to Nietzsche’s “animal that gives its word.” The agent
does not forget or remember; an architectural weighting decides in its place.
This confirms the thesis I pointed to earlier — “not a miracle of code but an
architectural choice” — but it also reveals a threshold at which that architectural
choice itself falls short: persistence alone is not enough; what is still missing is
persistence at a cost — the element of pain, of burden, that Nietzsche insists on.
In short, memory here has remained external to the model.
I should add that the experiment’s reflection mechanism is a point that most
resembles, yet also departs considerably from, the Sartrean moment of
reflection. Klaus Mueller’s arriving, from his library visits, at the inference
“someone passionate about research” is not the expression of a desire but a
statistical labeling performed by the language model. That is, this synthesis is
like writing a report on the taste of the fruit — not tasting the fruit itself (qualia).
And here again we see that the externalization of memory misses the internal
altogether. For genuine self-consciousness and inner experience, it isn’t enough
for the model merely to read a memory that gets recorded somewhere —

Two studies published in the first half of 2026 approach this very threshold — weights
changing through experience — from opposite directions. Researchers from Google and
Cornell, in “Language Models Need Sleep,” propose a two-phase cycle for the model
modeled on biological sleep: short-term contextual information is distilled into
persistent weights during a "sleep" phase, and this is done, quite literally, through a
"Dreaming" sub-process, in which the model improves itself by training on fictional
scenarios it generates on its own — which looks like a technical counterpart both to
Nail's proposal that AI ought to dream and to my own hallucination thesis. For more
details, see: Ali Behrouz, Farnoosh Hashemi, Adel Javanmard, and Vahab Mirrokni.
“Language Models Need Sleep: Learning to Self-Modify and Consolidate Memories,”
arXiv, 2026, https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2606.03979v2. Against this, “MemRL” takes
the opposite route: it proposes improvement at runtime without changing the weights at
all, simply by appending learned utility scores to episodic memory, see: Shengtao Zhang
et al, “MemRL: Self-Evolving Agents via Runtime Reinforcement Learning on Episodic
Memory,” arXiv, 2026, https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2601.03192v2. These two opposing
strategies show that, as of 2026, where the real leap actually lies remains very much an
open question.
