Organismic Agency as the Principle of Individual Selection
Hugh Desmond1

Abstract
This article introduces organismic agency as the principle of individual selection. Individual selection involves the organism itself selecting a behavior or development according to individual preference metrics. It is the explanatory counterpart
to natural selection, and both compete to explain phenomena of goal-directedness. This conceptualization allows agency’s
underlying causal mechanism and explanatory function to be clarified. It also points to an operationalization strategy
where agency is inferred when evidence suggests that natural selection cannot explain observations of organisms preferentially engaging in particular patterns of development or behavior.
Keywords Agency · Goal-directedness · Mechanism · Natural selection · Organism

Introduction
In recent years, a novel research program has aimed at
theorizing organisms as agents. While there are many
approaches within this program, a common denominator is
the view that organisms are not simply designed machines
or “a bundle of adaptations” (Huxley 1942, p. 420) shaped
by external forces such as natural selection, but direct
themselves towards certain end states. Various modeling
approaches analyze this in terms of the underlying organizational structures and processes (Moreno and Mossio 2015;
Virenque and Mossio 2024) or in terms of how organisms
interact with the environment (Walsh 2015; Fulda 2017).
Empirically, the research program has also sought to use the
agential theoretical perspective to generate specific predictive claims (Sultan et al. 2022).
The program is not without its critics, however, who
contest whether the concept of agency succeeds in generating novel successful explanations beyond what established
modes of explanation already do (DiFrisco and Gawne
2024). In essence, the critique is that agency is explanatorily
superfluous: any organismal phenomenon that is potentially
explainable as the outcome of the organism’s agency can

This dispute is, arguably, the latest installment of ongoing fundamental disagreement—going back a century and a
half, and more (Huneman 2026)—about what makes organisms distinct, if anything, from other complex systems. One
issue at stake, in particular, is the long-standing question
whether teleological explanations of organismal behavior
and development can be fully recast in terms of mechanistic
modes of explanation—and, in general, in terms of (natural)
selectionist explanations. This was a dividing issue between
Darwin and Wallace (Wallace 1889), through the question
whether some birds really selected their mates because of
perceptions of “beauty”. Darwin believed they did; Wallace recast such instances of sexual selection in selectionist
terms (see discussion in Prum 2017).
Wallace’s stance has shaped some of the major research
programs of 20th-century evolutionary biology, including
the work on mate choice and sexual selection (following
Grafen 1990a; b; see also Zahavi 1975) and on altruism and
kin selection (following Hamilton 1963). These research
programs both seek to explain apparently agential phenomena—organisms that seem to care about beauty in sexual
mates, or organisms that seem to care about the good of
non-offspring—as traits shaped by natural selection. The

Along these lines of thinking, attributing agency to organisms might merely seem to provide a convenient shorthand
to refer to an underlying process of natural selection—or
simply an anthropomorphic projection.
By contrast, within the agency literature, this adaptationist approach to organisms and their behavior and development is often rejected outright. The reasoning is that natural
selection is a derivative phenomenon, depending wholly on
how organisms interact with their environment. The organism, with its goal-directed ecological interactions with the
environment—seeking nutrition, shelter, avoiding toxins or
predators, and so on—is the primitive theoretical concept,
inextricably bound with its perceived environment (Walsh
2015; Fábregas-Tejeda 2026) in creating its own conditions
of self-maintenance (Montévil and Mossio 2015; Moreno
and Mossio 2015). This leads to a very different conclusion:
that agency reflects the fundamental nature of organisms.
The purpose of this article is to chart a path forward by
developing the agency concept as substantially as possible
within a methodologically adaptationist framework. The
working assumption is that the principle of natural selection
provides the default explanation of organismal behavior and
development.1 However, under some conditions, natural
selection ought to be rejected as an explanation and under
yet further conditions, agency ought to be accepted. Adaptationism is taken as the starting point in order to explore its
limits, and to identify the empirical conditions under which
agency can be considered explanatorily indispensable.

the conditions under which it is defensible to conclude that
organisms are agents.
This briefly summarizes the approach taken. As to the
actual content of the proposed idea—concerning what
organismic agency is—the proposal is to take the concept
of choice or decision-making as the prism through which to
view the self-productive processes of organisms. Agency is
primarily a process of deliberation or decision-making carried out at the level of the individual: agency is “the principle of individual selection”. In the way natural selection
involves a selection of certain individuals over other individuals, agency involves the individual organism selecting
one behavior over another, or one developmental pathway
over another. And like natural selection, individual selection aims to explain just how organisms seem to manifest
behaviors and traits that “fit” their natural environment in
very precise ways that are “beneficial” or “profitable” to the
organism (Darwin’s original phrasing: e.g., p. 64 and p. 128
in Darwin [1859]2008), and that seem unintelligible if simply explained as chance occurrences.
This conception of agency as individual selection constitutes a departure from perhaps the most common understanding of agency in the agency literature, namely as the
capacity for goal-directedness. This capacity, on the account
presented here, is derivative, not foundational: if an organism exhibits goal-directed behavior or development, this is
to be considered “agential” only if it is the outcome of a process of individual selection. This, together with the working
assumption of the explanatory priority of natural selection
over agency, can be seen as a modeling choice.
It is thus important to emphasize that the scope of the
argument in this article is constructive and pragmatic.
Agency is redefined as individual selection, and this allows
an adaptationist framework to be extended. This concept of
what agency is, and how it functions in explanations, constitutes a distinct theoretical framework that may be useful for
those who may wish to attribute a genuine sense of agency
to organisms, while remaining within a broadly adaptationist
framework. However, as noted later, the conclusions of this
framework overlap significantly with those of the ecological

A Stylized Illustration of Individual Selection
Imagine a series of the following observations. A gazelle
grazes while a lion approaches. The lion comes within a
certain distance; the gazelle looks up in the direction of the
lion. However, it becomes apparent the lion is not approaching the gazelle but some other point, and eventually the
gazelle continues feeding.
There are several apparently functional behaviors here—
e.g., monitoring a predator, feeding. If we were to categorize these observations, it would be hard to avoid some
invocation of psychological agency: the gazelle “hesitated”,
“assessed” the situation, and was “reassured” about the danger. However, why would this not be an anthropomorphic
projection? What exactly is it about gazelle behavior that
suggests that the concept of “agency” plays an explanatory
role?
Many aspects of the behavior would seem at least in
part explainable through the principle of natural selection. As an offshoot of the genus Gazella, the gazelle has
inherited a number of distinct cognitive modules from its
mammalian and vertebrate ancestors, including interoceptive hunger signaling (Siemian et al. 2021; Stevenson et
al. 2023), foraging decision-making mechanisms (Mobbs
et al. 2018), and a fight-or-flight response (Sansom et al.
2009; Herberholz and Marquart 2012). These modules have
been shaped by natural selection to produce certain types
of behavioral output (e.g., run away) given certain types of

sensory input (e.g., growing size of certain types of shape
that tend to correspond to predators). This apparent “hesitation” and “assessment” is not necessarily any genuine decision-making but can simply be accounted for as the balance
between these modules: all were somewhat activated, but
none sufficiently so to determine the overall behavior of the
organism. Then, an environmental trigger tipped the organism towards one type of behavior rather than the other (i.e.,
continue grazing).
Thus, in principle, some combination of selectionist and
mechanistic explanation could be sufficient to account for
the observations, making the “agency” concept superfluous.
The gazelle is undergoing lines of causal influence but not
necessarily doing anything. It seems to be acting independently, but in reality it is carrying out “if–then” rules shaped
by natural selection and triggered by environmental stimuli.
The question that this article wishes to emphasize is that
this is only one explanatory possibility, and whether it constitutes a full explanation depends on the actual observations. For instance, say that we observe that the gazelle flees
when the lion approaches to within distance D1: why D1
instead of a further distance D2 > D1? One could hypothesize
that the “nervous” type of gazelle, which fled once predators
approached to D2, was selected against, because it spent too
many energetic resources on false dangers and missed out
on feeding opportunities. However, this explanatory strategy entails that we must hypothesize yet another heritable
adaptation—the “nervous type”. Whether such a hypothesis
is applicable will depend on the evidence: is there evidence
that D1 is a cutoff for all gazelles in the population? Does D1
remain a cutoff when environmental context is varied, for
instance if there are natural obstacles (river, rocks) between
lion and gazelle, or if the lion is caged (in an experimental
setting)? If we observe the gazelle deciding differently when
protected by a river or by metal bars, or if it exhibits other
evidence that it is making a decision tailored to the relevant
factors in its specific, token environment, then we might
consider that the gazelle is carrying out its own decision.
In general, at least some decision-making can be modeled as heuristic decision-making (Gigerenzer 2000), and
in such cases, one need not invoke agency. The individual
is not making their “own” decision but rather following
preset rules. Because such decision-making responds in
a domain-specific and automatic way to certain types of
environmental triggers, it is a prime candidate for explaining how decision-making can be shaped by causes external to the organism, such as selection or imprinting. The
model of heuristic decision-making predicts that the organism exhibits rule-bound behavior, or what Hofstadter once
called “sphexishness” (Hofstadter 1982, p. 22): maladaptive
behavior when the organism is placed in a subtly different
environment with the same triggers as the old environment.

Different heuristics or “personalities”, whether shaped by
natural selection or by irreversible imprinting, can be distributed in a single population (Bergmüller and Taborsky
2010), with, for instance, some more risk-averse and others
more adventurous. Such personalities would be “sphexish”
as well, where the risk-averse type may continue to be riskaverse even in environments where this is maladaptive.
If the model of heuristic decision-making fails —for
instance, the organism fails to exhibit the expected sphexish
behavior—what then is the alternative? One can search for
another rule governing the behavior or consider the possibility that the behavior is random. However, at some point
we could consider the alternative that the gazelle itself is
conducting its own decision-making, with its own rules and
preferences. Natural selection may be part of the explanation why the gazelle has some cognitive capacity in the first
place, and may explain why there are some decision rules
that act as heuristics, but if there is evidence that a token
gazelle reacted appropriately in a specific way to the specificities of its unique token environment, it may be insufficient to merely appeal to a heritable, fitness-enhancing trait,
such as the fight-or-flight response or even to a heritable
personality. In such cases, we may opt to characterize the
situation as “the gazelle decided the lion was not a danger”—instead of, for instance, “the gazelle’s fight-or-flight
response was deactivated”.
This line of reasoning does not simply recapitulate the
challenges long faced by cognitive ethology, namely the
question of the conditions under which psychological agency
can be invoked (Griffin 1978; Allen and Bekoff 1997). In
the case of the gazelle, the failure of the model of heuristic decision-making implies that the gazelle is not simply a
vehicle for pre-shaped cognitive modules, and rather, that
its brain is processing information from its specific token
environment. The relevant contrast is not between mechanism and the mental, but between rule-bound and contextsensitive behavior. Hence, this line of inquiry is applicable
across organisms, including those without nervous systems.
Natural selection may shape the organism’s heuristic decision-making, but what if the organism encounters an atypical environment where two or more heuristics seem equally
applicable? Then either we must invoke a meta-heuristic
that was externally caused (through selection, inheritance,
or imprinting), hypothesize that the organism is simply acting randomly, or else admit the possibility that the organism
itself is conducting its own decision-making that is finely
attuned to its specific, token environment. There is a selection process, as a choice between responses, but at the level
of the individual, not the population.
To emphasize from the outset, the capacity for this
individual selection might itself be a trait subject to natural selection. If we assume that natural environments are

13

too complex and heterogeneous to be navigated by population-level heuristics alone, individual selection could be
hypothesized to be the adaptive “delegation” of fine-tuned
decision-making to the organismal level. Instead of Mayr’s
distinction between “proximate” and “ultimate” causation,
a distinction that better captures what is at stake is that
between “centralized” causation (type-level, heuristic decision-making) and “decentralized” causation (token-level,
context-sensitive decision-making). The former produces
a rule; the latter produces a tailored deliberation. Even if
there was a “centralized” decision for individuals to develop
a “decentralized” decision-making capacity, that does not
mean that each individual decision can be explained by reference to the original centralized decision. Similarly, agency
can evolve through natural selection without agential explanations being reducible to natural selection explanations.

Agency as a Structuring Cause
The preceding section aimed to give a global sense of the
principle of individual selection, and how one might use
it in investigating organismal phenomena. The rest of the
article will work towards building up these intuitions more
formally. The first step in this section is to clarify the meaning of the general term “agency” for the present context.
Though motivations for the stipulative choices are briefly
given, the goal of this section is not to make definitive
claims about the nature of agency (as a general concept), but
to prevent some avoidable confusions further down the line.
In an original sense of the term, an agent is an entity
which acts, and is to be contrasted with a “patient” which
undergoes the causal influence of external causes (Schlosser
2015). It is also possible to understand agency as primarily
a mental capacity, such as the capacity for intentional representation, and to enquire to what extent that can be generalized to nonhumans—however, many of the same conceptual
challenges arise (see discussion in Aaby 2024). This definition of agency is broad enough to capture the varying uses
across domains and disciplines, from metaphysics (Oderberg 2024) and physics (Barzegar et al. 2025), to jurisprudence (via culpability: Dubber 2015), ethics, and politics
(through the concepts of freedom and autonomy).
Agency as self-causation. In the context of biology, the
contrast between agency and patiency is often denoted by
the term “activity” (versus passivity) or by various phrases
involving “self-*” (self-organization, self-regulation, selfdirection, self-maintenance, and so on). In the following, I
will use the phrase self-causation as the more general category: a system is an agent insofar as it is the cause of its
own change of state.

Organismic Agency as the Principle of Individual Selection

Defining agency in this way serves to avoid some confusion: agency is not a synonym for causation as such, nor
is it a synonym for individuality. However, it does so by
introducing a seeming contradiction in terms: since a cause
is distinct from its effect, no entity or process can be both
cause and effect. Therefore, self-causation in itself cannot
be interpreted in a literal sense of picking out a part of physical reality that forms a closed causal loop. This does not
mean that it is nonsensical. Through idealization, scientific
models “lie” about reality by default (Cartwright 1983)—
indeed, idealization is their modus operandi for capturing
the structural and explanatory properties of a target phenomenon. The challenge, then, is to explicate why it can
be explanatory to model a system as both cause and effect.
Defining agency as self-causation is a mere starting point
that is in need of further elaboration.
Agency as a structuring cause. Agency does not refer to
a physical entity that can be seen, touched, or even localized
in space. It is also not a physical cause in the sense that it
does not transmit a distinctive physically localizable “mark”
such as energy or matter (Salmon 1984). Rather, the relevant
notion of causation is difference-making causation (cf. Hall
2004): attributing agency (self-causation) to an organism
means that the organism (as a whole) makes a causal difference for observed patterns of behavior and development.
In particular, some organism-level property is a structuring cause for the observed behavior or development. Structuring causes, as the term suggests, are causes that shape
the “structure” of events, and are contrasted with triggering
causes, which set a sequence of events in motion, but do not
account for why a certain sequence rather than another was
set in motion.
The classic illustration (Dretske 1988, 2004) of a structuring vs. triggering cause is the general dying in a car
explosion because the terrorist planted a bomb that could be
triggered by turning the ignition key. Would we judge that
the general was “the” cause of his own death? He perhaps
contributed to his death—had he not stepped into the car, he
would not have died—but he was hardly “the” cause in the
stronger sense of someone who is to be held responsible for
the outcome. Rather, it is the terrorist who should be held
causally responsible. While the terrorist did not determine
the outcome, he did shape the structural conditions that
strongly affected the probability of the outcome of dying.
Applied to agency, there are two lessons to be drawn,
one negative and one positive. The negative lesson is that
attributing agency to an organism need not imply that one
is attributing some type of voluntaristic, uncaused free will.
Attributing agency to the gazelle does not turn on finding
evidence that its behavior is uncaused by any outside environmental input. This would set the agency concept up to

be unintelligible. It would also lead to confusion between
agential behavior and random behavior.
The positive lesson is that we can investigate agency
attributions in the full assumption that any gazelle behavior
was triggered by sensory inputs, setting in motion a physiological-neurological cascade playing out in a causally
closed and deterministic universe. The question is whether
there is evidence that the shape of this cascade, or the relative frequency of one cascade compared to another cascade,
is structured by the whole organism. Agency needs to pick
out some organism-level property that plays this structuring-causal role.

Agency as Explanatory Hypothesis
Agency is commonly defined as the capacity for goaldirectedness. In the concept of individual selection, by contrast, goal-directedness is not the definiens of agency but
rather the explanandum. As explored later, the definiens is
selection or decision-making, involving the actualization
of preferences. However, because such selection cannot be
observed but only inferred, agency is primarily approached
as an explanatory hypothesis (or rather, a class of explanatory hypotheses) that is to be tested against the best available data.
One implication of this choice is that the hypothesis of
agency ought not be tested against any observed organismal
behavior or development. Much behavior or development
must be allowed to be simply “random”, however intellectually unsatisfying this may be. The fact that this particular
oak tree I encounter on my morning walk has a large root
growing at 21 degrees from north is not a fact that is caused
by any biologically interesting property in either the organism or environment. It likely is the product of a confluence
of unrelated circumstances that is unique to that tree.2 These
are not the type of phenomena that an agency concept should
seek to explain. However, what is “goal-directedness”?

However, this minimal definition of goal-directedness
quickly leads to quandaries when authors start making a
distinction between genuine and merely apparent goaldirectedness. For instance, Nagel once argued that phenomena such as the marble rolling to the center of a bowl, or the
knee stretching upon being tapped, ought not to be judged as
goal-directed, because they do not have the requisite property of “persistence” (Nagel 1977). By this he meant that
if the system encountered an obstacle, it is “not equipped
with any mechanism for making appropriate compensatory
responses to those disturbances” (Nagel 1977, p. 270). This
gave rise to a large discussion about what “genuine goaldirectedness” is, if anything (reviewed in Okasha 2023).
In this article, this distinction between genuine and
merely apparent goal-directedness will be bypassed (indeed,
one could argue it simply recapitulates the problem of
agency). Instead, we will operate with a working definition
of goal-directedness where a “goal” is a behavioral or developmental state (or pattern of behavior/development) that the
organism approaches much more frequently than other possible states within its lifetime, across variable background
conditions, without this resulting from some mathematical,
physical, or chemical constraint. In this sense, a goal can be
said to be a “preferred” or “privileged” state.
As an instance of a constraint, consider how fish exhibit
“rheotaxis”: they align with the direction of the stream,
facing upstream, and swim against the flow. However, this
can be predicted by abstracting away from all properties of
the fish and representing it as a vortex dipole (Porfiri et al.
2022). Moreover, even dead fish exhibit rheotaxis: a counterintuitive phenomenon that is caused by specific properties
of vortex dynamics (Beal et al. 2006). In other words, physical principles seem to do a lot of the work in explaining why
fish preferentially exhibit rheotaxis. Similarly, if one were
to observe that a population of moths packed into a corner
gradually spreads across a room, this could be judged to be
a physical phenomenon (diffusion) or even a mathematical
phenomenon (convergence to the highest probability state
through the law of large numbers). Such phenomena are,
very minimally, excluded from the definition of apparent
goal-directedness above.

The working definition of goal-directedness is intended
to exclude phenomena that can be explained through physics, chemistry, or mathematics: if some form of apparent
goal-directedness can be explained through a physical law,
there is no need to invoke agency. Simultaneously, it is
intended to be inclusive towards phenomena where natural
selection and/or mechanistic explanations may be viewed
as doing most of the work. A plant stem bending to the light
is goal-directed, even if this seems to be well explainable in
physical terms, through blue light activating receptor phototropins and triggering redistribution of the growth hormone
auxin. This is not an explanation that simply results from
physical constraints, for one can ask the question why plant
stems possess such phototropins to begin with—a line of
questioning that might lead to considering an adaptationist hypothesis. Similarly, some nonfunctional behaviors,
where some function is activated outside its conditions of
selection, may be considered goal-directed on the definition
above. If a rat becomes addicted to morphine, this involves
a nonfunctional activation of its dopamine system, but asking why it has a dopamine system to begin with may lead
one to consider an adaptationist hypothesis.
For now, it is fair to say that in many epistemic contexts—without epistemic access to what the underlying biochemical cascades are—many organismal phenomena are
apparently goal-directed. Of course, such a judgment may
be undermined by further considerations, but this is precisely
the question: what explains apparent goal-directedness?

Competing Explanations
How is apparent goal-directedness to be explained? The
principle of natural selection offers one possibility. And in
this article’s proposal, agency offers an alternative, competing “ultimate explanation” (in E. Mayr’s sense) for observations of goal-directedness.
Let me take an example from the human context that
offers a relatively extreme illustration of this competition.
In evolutionary psychology, adaptationist explanations are
often sought for patterns of human behavior. If males with
trait T tend to mate with females with trait T’, the default
explanatory strategy pursued in evolutionary psychology is
that this mate choice strategy benefits the fitness of T-type
male humans (Buss 2019 provides many examples). However, is such an explanation by natural selection always
a good explanation? Not necessarily, if we presume that
humans are agents that can act upon reasons in a way that is
not entirely controlled by fitness-enhancing cognitive modules selected in the Pleistocene. Say that John, a particular
T-type male, happens to prefer Mary, a female lacking T’,
because they share the same taste in humor or art or some
idiosyncratic trait. An adaptationist explanation may seem

Organismic Agency as the Principle of Individual Selection

far-fetched and implausible here. In many respects, even if
there are population-level patterns, the romantic choices of
many token individuals simply cannot be fully explained
through brute adaptationist logic, and we must appeal to the
agency of individuals (Dupré and O’Neill 1998).
This may seem to carve out an explanatory pluralism:
population-level traits are explainable by natural selection,
whereas individual-level properties are explainable through
agency. And this is true to a significant extent. However,
individual-level properties can also be explained through
natural selection, and in this sense they can compete.
Returning to the example, an individual may subjectively experience their decision as “their own”, for instance,
because they have good reasons for it. However, this may
be a subjective overestimation of their agency. Their decision may in fact simply and unambiguously be channeling
gender-typical mate preferences. When there is evidence of
individual preferences entirely conforming to expected mate
preferences, an evolutionary psychologist may question
whether that individual’s agency is playing any causal role.
It is not making a difference to their behavior in the way that
the individual believes it does. Dawkins’ perspective seems
appropriate (Dawkins 1982): the individual plays the role of
a vehicle for expressing a genetic trait—an unconscious and
inherited cognitive bias—shaped over many generations. In
this perspective, the subjective belief in one’s own agency
appears as a mere post-hoc construction—a perspective
long applied to human moral beliefs (Haidt 2001).
Note the scope of the argument here: the point is not
whether evolutionary psychology’s strategy of explaining
human behavior is true or false. The lesson is that evolutionary psychology’s adaptationist logic deflates agency. If
an adaptationist explanation is seen to be adequate for a specific explanandum, then agency does not play a causal role.
It is in this sense that the explanatory competition between
natural selection and agency is to be understood. When
an explanation by natural selection successfully accounts
for the observed phenomenon which was apparently goaldirected (e.g., a revealed preference), the initial impression
of goal-directedness is undermined. It is not really “the
organism itself” that is causally responsible for the apparently goal-directed behavior/development, but rather a part
of the organism (the trait). What initially may have appeared
as an agential behavior turns out to be the expression of an
adaptive function, which is just shorthand for what that part
of the organism was selected for (Neander 1991).
The competition between natural selection and agency
can be put formally as follows, even if it anticipates some
elements that are introduced later in the article. Both target instances of apparent goal-directedness, which involve
a particular outcome (organismal state or pattern of states)
being probabilistically privileged in the organism’s life

history. This asymmetry between possible outcomes calls
for an explanation. Natural selection provides a possible
explanation where the ranking between outcomes is defined
through fitness: the privileged state is privileged because
of fitness maximization. Agency is an alternative principle,
grounding explanations where the ranking between outcomes is defined through other, individual-level metrics.
What best explains the organismal preference—fitness, or
some other metric such as beauty, play, or welfare? Whether
the privileging is best categorized as involving fitness differences or differences in individual-level metrics depends on
contextual empirical details. In this article’s approach, such
questions cannot be decided a priori: agency is an explanatory hypothesis that must be accepted or rejected in competition with natural selection.
This means that, on the framework offered here, one cannot directly ask whether an organism is an agent. Rather, the
question to ask is whether a particular behavior (or pattern
of behaviors) is best explained as expressing the organism’s
agency, or not. Some organisms may display more agential behaviors than others. For some organisms, one could
doubt whether they display any agential behavior. But all
organisms, humans included, exhibit many behaviors that
are non-agential, such as falling in gravitational fields.3
Further explaining and specifying the nature of this
explanatory competition is, in a sense, what will occupy the
rest of the article. In the rest of the subsection, I would like
to consider a series of objections that will help situate this
approach4.
First, these claims about explanatory competition seem
to take sides on the long-standing philosophical debate on
whether natural selection can explain the traits of token
organisms. On the one side is the “negative view” of natural
selection (Sober 1984) that holds that only trait distribution
in populations can be explained; on the other is the view that
natural selection does have some explanatory relevance for
the trait composition of a token individual. Without reviving this debate, it can be helpful to position this article.
The approach here assumes the negative view as a starting
point (the primary explanatory target of evolution by natural
selection is indeed to explain changing trait distributions in
populations) but allows for the possibility that a token trait
(of a token organism) may be unambiguously subsumed
under a specific trait type. So, the implied position on natural selection does not take sides in this debate in the philosophical community. In principle, when the token trait is
identical to the type trait, then natural selection can explain
the traits of token organisms. As illustrated in the example

against. If goal-directedness is viewed as the explanandum
of agency, not its definiens, as done in the approach here,
then feedback mechanisms and natural selection/agency are
complementary explanations, and the genuine competition
lies between natural selection and agency.

Causal Structure of Individual Selection
To sum up so far: through a stylized illustration, the idea
was introduced that agency involves an organism selecting a
preferred behavior or development. Then we analyzed what
type of causal and explanatory principle individual selection might be: a process where an organism-level property
causes apparently preferred organismal states or patterns,
and that can narrowly compete with natural selection.
The challenge now is to clarify (1) what such an “organism-level property” could mean, (2) how precisely this is a
(structuring) cause of behavior, and (3) how precisely this
may be explanatorily indispensable. This section tackles the
first two questions in general terms; the next section does so
with more empirical detail by identifying concrete “organism-level properties”. Section “Operationalizing Individual
Selection” and section “Case: Bacterial Chemotaxis” consider the question of explanatory indispensability by operationalizing individual selection.

In the present context, agency is to be causally analyzed
through a different concept in nonlinear dynamics: symmetry breaking. In a sense, this involves an expansion of
the classic cybernetic approach to allow for multiple stable
equilibria, with the system in principle being attracted to
any one of them, depending on the context. The analogy
is not a person picking up a pencil but deciding whether to
pick up a pencil or pick up a marker. Both will require servomechanisms to realize, but before the choice is made, the

are not symmetrical, a “choice” by the person is not necessary: it is obvious to the person what they should pick up.
The person then acts to break the symmetry between the
options.
In slightly formal terms, “symmetries” refer to two different types of outcome that are equally probable given the
initial state of the system plus the laws that describe the
system’s evolution (Brading et al. 2021). In other words,
outcomes are symmetrical when no outcome is privileged.
Symmetry breaking then refers to a process by which one
outcome comes to be realized instead of another, despite the
initial state and laws being indifferent to the two outcomes.
Phase transitions constitute the classic example of symmetry breaking (Fig. 1). For instance, in the phase transition
from paramagnetism to ferromagnetism, the magnetic spins
are no longer randomly distributed but have definite “preferential” orientation within domains. This is a (local) breaking of the symmetry in orientations, and without an external
cause having “predecided” this process. By contrast, if the

phase transition occurs within an external magnetic field,
then all spins will align with the external magnetic field.
Even in physics, symmetry breaking is more of a general
explanatory template rather than a rigidly defined theory
(Borrelli 2021). It can be used in a wide range of epistemic
contexts, from early-universe cosmology to traffic flows.
One way to think about it is that physicists use symmetry
breaking as a reasoning template for explaining the appearance of novelty, whether novel forces (e.g., the appearance
of electromagnetic forces from the electroweak force) or
novel properties (e.g., laminar flow, ferromagnetism). It is
in this more general sense that it can also be a useful concept
in a biological context (Longo and Montévil 2019).
To apply this to our stylized example: “symmetry” arises
when the gazelle faces nontypical conditions relative to its
history of natural selection. All inherited cognitive traits are
equally activated by the conditions, such that they compete
against each other to determine behavior. In the specific
environment the gazelle faces, fleeing or continuing to feed
seem equally preferable “from the perspective of natural
selection”. Multiple possible behaviors seem equally adaptive. When the gazelle selects one course of behavior over
another, this state of symmetry is broken.
Symmetry can only be defined relative to a dynamics,
so let us emphasize how symmetries arise with regard to
natural selection and what their biological reality is. Natural
selection shapes the evolution of organisms through traits
that are adaptive to typical circumstances: the spatiotemporal distribution of various types of nutrition, predators,
mates, etc. When faced with “typical” circumstances, the
various types of organisms may be assigned a well-defined
fitness value: how well or poorly an organism is expected
to fare. However, in evolutionarily novel scenarios this is
no longer the case. “Novel” scenarios can be defined as
environments where there is a symmetry with respect to
the dynamics of natural selection—in other words, there
are multiple behavioral or developmental responses to the
environment that have equal fitness. Equal fitness means an
equal expected reproductive output, where the expectation
function is defined by the historical selective environments.

Symmetry breaking is a helpful concept to capture
how agency could be causally constituted. In the case of
the gazelle, the symmetry induces a competition among
selected traits in determining the behavior of the whole
organism (fleeing or feeding). In the case of bacterial chemotaxis (discussed later), the symmetry may induce a competition between different gene-regulatory mechanisms that
produce different concentrations of proteins that pull the
organism in different directions, producing “pausing behavior”. The symmetry is broken by an additional environmental input, which then sets in motion a physiological cascade
that results in the behavioral/developmental output. This is a
causal-mechanistic translation of the agential process.
Note that symmetry breaking is necessary but not sufficient for individual selection. For instance, symmetry
breaking in a magnet does not entail that the magnet is an
agent choosing a certain orientation. Or to take an even
more basic example: a rock balancing on a precipice but
eventually tipping over towards one side rather than another
is also undergoing symmetry breaking, but is not an agent.
The rock might even be said to have two “goals” (falling
to the left and falling to the right) that are in competition,
and once the wind picks up, a “decision” is made. However,
the rock is not an agent because external causes (the wind,
the gravitational field, etc.) entirely account for the rock’s
behavior. There is no distinction between type and token:
the relevant behavior of this token rock is identical to any
other token rock. There is no need to invoke “rock agency”.
What “individual-level” property then allows for agency
to be attributed? In general, an (emergent) explanatory
notion being translatable to causal-mechanistic terms
does not mean that that notion is reducible to those terms
(O’Connor 2021); however, there still needs to be an identifiable higher-level property that accounts for why agency
cannot be reduced to causal-mechanistic terms.

Preference Metrics as Structuring Causes
A preference metric is the principle, relative to particular
dynamics, by which one outcome is probabilistically privileged over another outcome. Fitness can be interpreted as
the preference metric inherent in natural selection. Individual selection presupposes other, individual-level metrics.
To this end, consider the distinction between sorting and
selection (Vrba and Gould 1986). Differences in reproductive rate are necessary but not sufficient for a process of population change to be deemed selection. If these differences
in reproductive rate do not correspond to fitness differences,
this is not selection, but just “sorting”. In sorting, no trait is
privileged over another by the dynamics themselves.
Can fitness be a universal preference metric, applicable
to all behavior? The “phenotypic gambit” in behavioral

13

ecology involves the assumption that organismal preferences
correspond to fitness-maximizing strategies that moreover
have a simple genetic basis. Or in Grafen’s original terms,
it is the assumption that “[t]he success of a [behavioral]
strategy is the number of offspring left by an animal adopting it, or alternatively its inclusive fitness” (Grafen 1984,
p. 63). For our purposes, the difference between individual
fitness and inclusive fitness does not matter; what matters is
that when behavioral ecologists speak of animal “decisionmaking” (Westneat and Fox 2010, p. 131) or “preferences”
(Martin and Bateson 1993, pp. 69–73) this is intended as a
shorthand for a more complicated process by which some
fitness-enhancing cognitive module is being activated by
environmental signals in the way it was selected to do.
As discussed previously, there is at least one species
where the phenotypic gambit—channeled through evolutionary psychology—is strongly resisted. And that is
because it is assumed that fitness is not the only preference metric or “value-standard” (McShea 2023) that characterizes human behavior. Insofar as agency (as individual
selection) is attributable to nonhuman organisms (and this
empirical question is set aside for the moment), the organisms must be characterized by preference metrics that are
distinct from fitness.
To reformulate this idea more succinctly but abstractly:
preference metrics structure the probability distribution
of the possible outcomes within an organism’s life-history. This formulation helps clarify the deeper connection
between individual selection and natural selection. Fitness
is a preference metric that groups life-history outcomes
by reproductive number and assigns a probability to each
equivalence class (this is how it is conceptualized in Pence
and Ramsey 2013). Individual preference metrics group lifehistory outcomes according to different equivalence classes
(according to welfare, safety from predators, nourishment,
self-preservation, or play), which may only partially correlate with fitness-based equivalence classes. The phenotypic
gambit is the working assumption that fitness operates as
an individual preference metric as well—the failure of the
phenotypic gambit is a necessary condition for attributing
independent individual preference metrics.
Individual preference metrics explain why individual
selection cannot be reduced to mere symmetry breaking. An
individual preference metric can be thought of as a structuring cause that does not exert any physical causal influence,
but that does make one behavioral/developmental pathway
more probable than another. A peahen whose cognition
gives weight to aesthetic features—symmetry, contrast, tail
elaboration, display vigor—will exhibit a different pattern
of mate-acceptance behavior than a peahen whose decision
mechanisms track only indicators of underlying fitness,
such as costly handicaps. The aesthetic preference metric

Organismic Agency as the Principle of Individual Selection

explains this difference in behavior and can be understood
as a causal feature insofar as one adopts a difference-making concept of causality (Hall 2004). Or alternatively, a
plant with a preference for physiological integrity will be
triggered into different developmental responses (immune
activation, growth arrest) when stressed by the introduction of soil bacteria than a plant with only a preference for
maximizing fitness. In this way, preference metrics can be
thought of as organizational properties of the organism, predisposing the organism to respond in certain ways rather
than others to novel environments.7

Candidates for Individual Preference Metrics
To make the preceding discussion of individual preference
metrics more concrete, in this section, I briefly touch on a
number of candidates for individual preference metrics.
Play. Playful behavior is sometimes defined negatively,
as behavior without immediate utility or fitness benefits
(Bateson 2014; Tahar 2023). In other words, it is difficult to
account for observed play as fitness-maximizing behavior.
However, what preference metric is then implicitly at stake?
Different authors seem to invoke different candidates, from
a “pleasurable emotional experience” (Held and Špinka
2011) to “fun” (Bateson 2014). Bateson is quite explicit in
this regard: “[the playful behavior’s] performance serves as
a goal in itself. Play is ‘fun’” (Bateson 2014, p. 100).
Play can be considered as an illustration of an individual preference metric insofar as the organisms themselves
decide to exercise these capacities in very particular ways
because they gain “enjoyment” from this exercise. Note that
this does not imply that fitness has no explanatory role to
play. Individual and natural selection compete, but only for
narrowly defined explananda. An observed phenomenon of
play will typically be complex and admit of multiple complementary explanations. For instance, there is evidence
that increased play is correlated with greater behavioral
and immunological resilience (in studies of bear cubs: see
Bateson 2014 for references). Thus, an explanation by natural selection could suffice to account for why cubs at least
sometimes engage in play. However, it does not necessarily
account for the precise manners in which cubs play nor for
observations that cubs engage in play more frequently than
strictly necessary to increase resilience. Sporadic and stereotyped forms of play could be just as adaptive. From the
perspective of natural selection, stereotyped play is symmetrical with the rich, inventive forms of play observed. Hence,

Ecological goals. Both play and beauty would seem to
only be attributable to organisms with certain cognitive

playful behavior concern mammals and birds. Even though
such attributions are controversial enough, it would seem

more broadly applicable across species. These concern what
could be called “ecological goals”: predator avoidance,
nourishment, avoidance of extreme temperatures, and so on.
This class of preference metrics more closely coincides with
fitness, so a little care is needed to disentangle the sense in
which they can be considered agential.

extent with what is metaphorically “preferred” by natural
selection. However, the overlap may not be complete. (If it
were, this would be grounds for eliminating the individual
preference metric per Ockham’s razor.) Individual selection
thus can manifest itself as the organism being “choosier”
or “more sensitive” than would be expected from natural
selection alone. An organism may appear to prefer a particular type of shelter over another or pick up on novel sources
of food that did not occur in historical selective environments. Natural selection may select traits (such as seeking shelter and foraging behavior) in evolutionarily typical
environments, but there may be an additional process of
individual selection if the individual also acts in its token
environments to pursue the ecological goals that constitute
the fitness components. Thus, the same preference metric
can guide both the design of a heuristic decision-making

Operationalizing Individual Selection
The operationalization of agency is a core challenge for
defenders of the concept: what types of evidence allow for
agency to be inferred, and conversely, what types of phenomena does agency distinctively explain? For completeness’ sake, it should be acknowledged that attributing even
minimal goal-directedness requires some inference (e.g.,
ruling out physical explanations). And any assessment of
goal-directedness is fallible: if it turns out afterward that the
trait (e.g., rheotaxis) that was thought to be goal-directed
(whether the result of agency or natural selection) can be
fully explained through physical, chemical, or even mathematical principles, then the prior assessment of goal-directedness would need to be revisited. However, in such a case,
not only is agency superfluous, but also natural selection—
a critique of adaptationist hypotheses that goes back to
(Gould and Lewontin 1979). For that reason, we can put
such cases aside.
Given that assumption, making the inference to individual selection presupposes there must be grounds to rule
out natural selection. This is this article’s working assumption of methodological adaptationism: if natural selection
is perfectly adequate for the target explanandum, and there
is no explanatory need to invoke individual selection, natural selection takes priority. Natural selection can be granted
the status of null hypothesis concerning the types of goaldirectedness where agency might be explanatorily relevant.
This status of methodological privilege has implicitly been
rejected by many in the agency literature—and see also the
discussion of alternative null hypotheses of sexual selection
by Prum (2010)—but this article engages in a different intellectual exercise. Manifestations of goal-directedness can in
principle be reclassified as adaptedness, as long as a plausible causal reasoning can be set up where the goal contributes to fitness (directly or indirectly).
Within the framework of individual selection, the conditions for explanatory failure of natural selection can be
interpreted as evidential conditions for inferring individual
selection. The operationalization of individual selection
will be based on the operationalization of natural selection
(Endler 1986), and three evidential conditions in particular
will be considered:

Organismic Agency as the Principle of Individual Selection

1. Evidence that the observed behaviors/development are
fitness-enhancing but cannot be attributed to an underlying heritable trait as the conditions for past selection are
absent;
2. Evidence of a preference for behaviors/development
that lower fitness but that enhance some other individual
preference metric;
3. Evidence of a preference for behaviors/development that
are fitness neutral but enhance some other individual
preference metric.

Adaptation in Novel Environments
The relevant concept of novelty is not so much phenotypic
novelty (Wagner and Lynch 2010) as environmental novelty defined relative to the selective environment (Brandon
1990). The selective environment is an abstract representation of the real physical environment, where environmental
circumstances are classified according to the fitness differences they produce in a heterogeneous population (for
more detail on novelty and the selective environment, see
Desmond 2022). Conversely, a “novel environment” is one
that is not subsumable as a well-defined “state” of the selective environment. It produces a selection pressure that is illdefined relative to the selective environment (as theoretical
abstraction).
Environmental novelty allows the first evidential condition to be formulated: if an organism produces an adaptive
behavior when facing environmental circumstances that
did not plausibly recur in the past sufficiently as to produce
stable fitness differences, then this is grounds for rejecting
the hypothesis of natural selection. The adaptive behavior is (per definition of adaptive) fitness-enhancing, but if
there are good evidential grounds for judging the environmental circumstances to be novel, this provides grounds for
concluding that there was no past selection that shaped a
heritable trait. Thus, while natural selection could in principle explain the adaptive behavior, there may be evidence
that natural selection does not in fact explain the adaptive
behavior.
This test is not entirely separate from the long-standing
theme that one of the hallmarks of agency is the production
of phenotypic novelty, creativity, or inventiveness (Kauffman 2000; Newman 2023; Tahar 2023)—a theme that goes
back at least to Bergson (1911). However, the difference
is that novelty is defined relative to the environment: the
precise pattern of physical change of the organism may
not be novel, but it may count as “novel” if it is produced
adaptively in a novel environment. The bacteria swimming
towards or away from a chemical concentration are selecting a behavior from a very limited repertoire, but if they
do so adaptively in an environment that can be considered

novel, this is grounds for rejecting natural selection and
inferring individual selection.
The difficult empirical challenge in this test is to define
what precisely counts as “novel”. In general, any environment will bear some resemblance to past environments.
Errors can arise when judging a given environment as
novel, when in reality crucial cues may be shared with past
environments.
How then can novelty be established? Novelty is itself
a hypothesis that must be inferred, and it is not a simple
observational quantity. The operationalization of environmental novelty is, in general, a separate project that is of
increasing research interest (Staples et al. 2025). A full
discussion is therefore outside the scope of this article.
However, one pragmatic strategy could be to base tests of
novelty on tests for “environmental constancy” in operationalizations of natural selection. For instance, in longitudinal studies (method V in Endler 1986), the main type of
evidence for inferring natural selection is long-term stability
or long-term directional trends: these indicate some stable
feature in the environment that the population is adapting
to. To rule out the possibility of directional trends resulting
from chance, Endler recommends combining such tests with
further tests against the observed ecology of a population:
cohort analysis and life-history analysis. These studies can
identify recurrent environmental circumstances that covary
with fitness differences, in turn allowing for an inference of
natural selection. In this way a concept of “effective environmental constancy” is introduced: environments are not literally constant, as they are always changing and everywhere
heterogeneous, but features can be sufficiently constant for
natural selection to occur. Conversely, “novelty” would be
a lack of environmental constancy: a break in a long-term
directional trend, or a lack of recurrent environmental features that covary with fitness of individuals in a population.

Fitness-Lowering Behaviors
Fitness-lowering behaviors might be considered the strongest form of evidence, but one might expect any genuinely
fitness-lowering behavior to be very rare in nonhuman contexts. Because agency itself is constituted by properties of
organismal organization, which themselves may have some
genetic basis, such a capacity for agency would not be transmitted if it consistently produced fitness-lowering behavior.
In this way, one can expect individual selection to—by and
large—operate in the “gaps” left by natural selection, and
not to seek to causally counteract natural selection. Individual and natural selection are in explanatory competition, but
not necessarily in causal competition.
There are also additional hurdles for inferring individual selection from apparently fitness-lowering behavior.

To begin with, any impression that a behavior is fitnesslowering may simply be mistaken. The apparent preference
for fitness-lowering behaviors can result from evolutionary mismatch and ecological traps (Schlaepfer et al. 2002)
or from other mechanisms such as parasite manipulation
(Hughes and Libersat 2019)—in other words, cases where
the organism is not expressing its agency but is manipulated
by exogenous factors.
Moreover, when a behavior is accepted as genuinely fitness-lowering, the correct response may be to modify the
fitness metric rather than jettison it. This was the strategy
for explaining altruistic behaviors that were apparently fitness-lowering. The metric inclusive fitness was introduced
to augment the adaptationist framework (Hamilton 1963,
1964). Similarly, phenomena such as monogamous marriage—regarded as a puzzle because they reduce the reproductive success of high-status males—are accounted for
through the metric of group competitive success (Henrich
et al. 2012). In both cases, the fitness-lowering behavior is
accounted for by modifying the fitness metric.
Given these caveats, one ought not rule out a priori the
possibility of fitness-lowering organismal behavior stably
persisting across generations. Ornithologists such as Richard Prum argue that costly sexual displays in runaway Fisherian selection are evidence of aesthetic awareness in some
birds (Prum 2012)—yet others have demanded “abundant
proof” before considering such hypotheses (Grafen 1990b).9
This article does not take a stand on this particular evidential
question, but identifies the type of evidence as decisive for
inferring agency: if evidence of a fitness-lowering behavior
(that yet enhances some individual metric, such as beauty)
were to present itself, this would pose a genuine challenge
to adaptationist frameworks.

Fitness-Neutral Behaviors
The third criterion concerns a different type of breakdown
in the fitness metric. If an organism seems to exhibit goaldirected behavior, but the behavior does not seem to additionally contribute to fitness compared to other possible
behaviors, this may be interpreted as evidence for an individual preference metric. Within a strict comparison of individual selection versus natural selection, this relative failure
of natural selection can be taken as evidence for inferring
individual selection, according to some agential metric
(enjoyment, beauty, etc.)
One example comes from mate choice, where there seems
to be evidence that some birds prefer certain display traits
over others despite the competing traits conferring equal

fitness on the holder (this is discussed at length in (Desmond 2024). In particular, there seems to be evidence that
peahens prefer certain peacocks over others without any

Certain plumage configurations are preferred over alternatives that—to human observers—are equally long, complex,
colorful, and so on. As reported in (Desmond 2024), there
is a debate in this subfield on whether peahens are picking
up on some other, currently unobserved signals that correlate with fitness components such as immunological resilience. However, there is no conclusive evidence of this. The
alternative hypothesis is the hypothesis of individual selection: peahens, as Darwin himself claimed, have a “sense for
beauty” (Darwin 1871b, p. 73) and select peacocks in light
of that preference metric. Note that this does not imply that
natural selection has no role to play. Natural selection may
explain why peacocks with a train below a cutoff length or
with a number of ocelli below some other cutoff may be universally rejected: peacocks below the cutoff may have clear
disadvantages on underlying fitness components. However,
selective dynamics may not fully predict why an individual
peahen makes the decisions it does. An individual peahen
may have a stable preference for a type of train that does not
indicate higher fitness than some other non-preferred types
of train.
If the example of mate choice concerns the tension
between fitness and beauty, other examples can illustrate
the tension between fitness and welfare. Consider a stylized
test case where a guppy is observed responding to the introduction of a predator by opting for a behavior of self-preservation that suppresses reproductive opportunities. This
may not be a fitness-lowering behavior, but it may suggest
a preference for a behavior that also prioritizes its own welfare. Or it may consistently evince preference for a certain
type of shelter over another, even if both are equivalent for
predator protection. If there is evidence of the guppy consistently opting for one course of action rather than another
even if both are equivalent in fitness in the environments
typically encountered in the guppy’s lineage, this is evidence for a preference that is independent of fitness. The
stylized example with which we began—the gazelle evaluating whether to continue grazing or flee—was an example
of this category.
A hurdle for this evidential test consists in the introduction of the concept of animal personalities. Animal personalities are consistent individual differences in aggressiveness,
risk-taking, stress responsiveness, and so on (Dingemanse
and Réale 2005), and, as such, point to stable, fitness-neutral
decision rules in individuals, since no single personality is
selectively optimal (Nettle 2006). A natural selection model
of personality posits that personality could be a group-level
adaptation to reduce in-group conflict, in which individuals

Organismic Agency as the Principle of Individual Selection

converge on specific personalities through developmental
adaptation to their social niche (Bergmüller and Taborsky
2010). According to such a model, the consistent behaviors
of any one animal personality do not necessarily indicate
specific preference metrics of the individual organism, but
simply the organism’s developmental history, which caused
rigid decision rules and behavioral habits to have been
developed.
This leads to a conclusion that is equally applicable to
the other evidential tests: evidence of fitness neutrality is, in
itself, insufficient for inferring individual selection. These
conditions need to be coupled with a positive source of evidence for an individual preference metric, such as welfare,
or pleasure from play, or an aesthetic standard. This is what
may be lacking in phenomena of animal personality: what
preference metric do they express? An aggressive guppy
may be exhibiting aggressive behavior because of imprinting in early life-history, or because it happened to inherit an
aggressive personality. It may not be exhibiting the behavior
to further its “welfare” or some individual-level metric.
This brings us to the more conceptual dimension of the
operationalization challenge: how to operationalize the
preference metrics? One challenge to overcome is the risk
of introducing anthropomorphic constructs. For instance,
this risk is present in attributing a sense of beauty to birds
(Desmond 2024): why call whatever the peahens are preferring “beauty”? This suggests it has something in common
with human concepts of “beauty”—but is that implication
justified? (And even restricted to the human context, the
question of what beauty is is subject to perennial debate.)
To counter this risk of anthropomorphism, any attribution of
an individual preference metric would need to rule it out to
some reasonable standard, by considering alternative metrics that carry less anthropomorphic risk—such as welfare,
or autopoietic stability.
Another challenge is the risk of overattribution. This risk
arises especially with metrics such as welfare, since much of
organismal behavior could potentially be attributed as contributing to welfare. Hence, any assignment of an individual
preference metric would also need to clarify which types
of behavior do not contribute to that metric but are perhaps
artifacts of external processes (such as developmental history). This conceptual refinement is separate from the actual
process of inference and will not be further considered here.

test it, in this subsection, we will consider the evidence for
whether one of the most basic forms of locomotion, chemotaxis in E. coli, can be considered agential.
Despite E. coli being perhaps one of the most studied bacteria, the conclusion will be that it is ambiguous. A straightforward selectionist-mechanistic explanation is inaccurate.
Bacterial chemotaxis is certainly not like a mechanism that
takes certain sensory cues as inputs, produces motor outputs, and is adaptive in well-defined types of environments.
However, whether it involves a type of decision-making that
allows for the inference of agency is not easy to ascertain.
Some background on the underlying science is necessary here. First, contrary to common representation, chemotaxis does not simply entail “swimming to the source of
nutrients”. Bacteria are sometimes attracted to compounds
that are not particularly nutritious, and not always strongly
attracted to highly nutritious ones (Keegstra et al. 2022). The
relation between the strength by which bacteria are attracted
to a compound, and the positive effect that compound has on
the bacteria’s growth rate, is—in general—very noisy (see
Keegstra et al. 2022, Box 1). Hence, even if one can assume
that a particular type of chemotaxis evolved through natural
selection, one cannot simply assume that the evolutionary
function of chemotaxis lies in maximizing exposure to compounds that benefit the growth rate. In fact, Keegstra et al.
review how chemotaxis serves other ecological functions,
including expansion into novel environments (Keegstra et
al. 2022).
Further, there is a discrete set of environmental cues
that bacteria are sensitive to in “deciding” how to navigate
the environment through chemotaxis. Distilling three studies (Yamamoto et al. 1990; Taylor et al. 1999; Ortega et
al. 2017), Table 1 gives a non-exhaustive summary of the
“preference structure” of E. coli with some more empirical
precision. The table can be read as a mapping from sensory
input (amino acids, sugars, etc.) to behavioral output (attraction/repulsion). The mapping is highly modular, in the sense
that each sensory input is processed by its own dedicated

H. Desmond

mechanism (i.e., a receptor). Of these mechanisms, Tar and
Tsr are the most abundant in the E. coli membrane and are
sensitive to two of the most important sensory inputs—
aspartate and serine levels. The other chemoreceptors (Tap,
Trg) are much less abundant in the periplasm but can modulate responses in collaborative networks of chemoreceptors.
In natural environments, E. coli bacteria will be presented
with multiple inputs simultaneously. It responds to a soup of
types of amino acids, sugars, minerals, and so on. Thus, in
any given natural environment, it will need to make a “decision” about what importance to give to all these different
inputs. Our question is whether we can remove the inverted
commas around the word “decision”: are bacteria engaging in genuine decision-making, or are they controlled by
exogenous causes? Are they agents, or automata, following
decision rules? The following is a non-exhaustive overview
of evidence of bacterial decision-making.
A recent study (Park and Aminzare 2022) considered a
bacterial environment consisting of just two components—
serine and aspartate—and proposed that bacteria operate
with a simple decision rule: if Tar/Tsr is below some threshold value, the bacteria move up serine gradients, and otherwise they move up aspartate gradients. This, the authors
report, agrees well with experimental observations where E.
coli are exposed to only two stimuli (serine and aspartate).
However, for our purposes this does not count as decisive
evidence, as the question arises whether they have described
a decision rule or rather a causal process whereby more
intense activation of the aspartate-sensitive receptor leads to
a response to aspartate gradients, and the same with serine
(see also Kalinin et al. 2010). What appears to be decisionmaking thus may simply be a causal “tug-of-war” with a
multidimensional environment tugging on the organism in
the directions of different substance gradients. Sometimes
the tugging does not yield a clear resolution—in which case
the bacterium pauses and tumbles in place—but even if it
does produce a resolution, the organism itself may simply
be understood as undergoing competing environmental
triggers.
Some further conceptual refinement of what “novelty”
means can help interpret the empirical literature. The environment of the bacteria is defined by a number of discrete
variables, tracking types of amino acids, minerals, sugars,
and so on. A response to novelty cannot mean that the bacterium responds to some new substance—as it has no receptor to even track the substance. Novelty can mean, at most,
an environment consisting of different patterns or different
combinations of sensory inputs than was typical during the
selective history of its lineage. Thus, counter-indicating evidence would be evidence where a bacterium responds adaptively to a novel environment with decision rules tailored to

13

its novel token environment and that further an individual
preference metric, perhaps autopoietic stability.
In a classic study, E. coli was shown to use temporal
comparisons of gradients to “decide” whether to swim up
them or change direction. The comparison involves a fixed
decision rule that weights the past second positively and the
three seconds before that negatively (Segall et al. 1986).
This is an adaptive strategy in response to spatial gradients by sensing temporal gradients. However, this decision
rule does not necessarily involve any learning specific to
its token environment. Moreover, the study does not reveal
what the origin of this decision rule could be, and it offers no
evidence that would rule out the hypothesis that the decision
rule was selected for by natural selection.
Another study demonstrated the role of environmental
noise in modifying chemotactic behavior (Vladimirov and
Sourjik 2009). However, here, too, it is doubtful whether
the study constitutes evidence of bacteria learning about
their specific token environment, or whether bacteria are
simply proceeding with learning rules integrated with decision-making rules that would be adaptive in any typical
environment (as in Segall et al. 1986). Similarly, there is
evidence for the predictive ability of bacteria (Tagkopoulos
et al. 2008).10 There are other sources of evidence that could
be included, such as evidence that prior swarming behavior
affects cellular iron levels, which act as a memory affecting future swarming behavior (Bhattacharyya et al. 2023).
Again, in such studies, there is no evidence that undermines
the natural selection hypothesis.
In general, decision rules have been demonstrated to
guide chemotactic behavior, from threshold triggers to bethedging (Salek et al. 2019). More recent studies suggest
evidence of more complex decision-making. For instance,
chemotactic decision-making depends on quorum sensing,
which picks up on the signals of population density (Laganenka et al. 2025). Population density affects the ratios of
Tar/Tsr receptors, which in turn affects decision rules (Kalinin et al. 2010). However, what matters is whether there is
evidence of token individuals tailoring such decision-making to their specific environmental circumstances. Is there
reason to believe the organism itself is carrying out the decision-making, or is it a vehicle for behavioral rules shaped by
exogenous causes?
In this regard, an ongoing and intriguing area of inquiry
concerns the interaction between chemotaxis and the formation of biomolecular condensates, which can respond
to environmental stress and reorganize the structure of
signaling networks within bacteria (Sasazawa et al. 2024).
Condensate formation alters cellular responses to temperature fluctuations, nutrient starvation, and the detection of

The main lesson in this discussion of the evidence on
chemotactic decision-making is not to extract a definitive
assessment of bacterial agency, but rather to illustrate how
the conditions for inferring agency may be applied in concrete contexts.
Note that, in contrast to other analyses of chemotaxis
(Barandiaran et al. 2009; Barandiaran and Egbert 2014;
Moreno and Mossio 2015, pp. 99–101; Fulda 2017; Potter
and Mitchell 2022), the complexity of the internal cytoplasmic structure is of secondary importance in the analytic
approach proposed here. It is true that a host of proteins
(CheA, CheW, CheY, CheR, CheZ) modulate how precisely
the flagellar motor responds to the receptor inputs, and that
this modulation occurs in a nonlinear way. However, in the
model presented here, nonlinearity is a necessary condition
for agency attribution (via symmetry-breaking) but insufficient. The cytoplasmic lattice built by Che proteins could,
in principle, also be interpreted as instantiating what some
have called the “computational cell”, where computation
is assumed to proceed by fixed rules (Bray 2009). In the
model of individual selection, establishing the underlying
organizational structure is of secondary importance; what
matters primarily are the observed patterns of behavior, and
whether this provides evidence for organismic preferences
and decision-making that is sensitive to the organism’s specific context.
These conditions are relatively exacting, and a lot of the
current evidence of “learning”, “memory”, or “prediction”
I would judge to fall below the bar required for attributing agency. However, given that conducting an exhaustive
review of the evidence is beyond the scope of a generalist article such as this one, and especially since emerging
evidence regarding biomolecular condensates seems to
demonstrate one causal mechanism through which agential
decision-making could be instantiated (though how it precisely influences decision-making in the wild seems to be
as of yet unclear), I would not want to make a strong judgment that chemotaxis is not an agential behavior. It certainly
is much more complicated—integrating many independent
signals from the environment, as well as intra-organismic
sources of signaling—than its basic textbook representation
suggests, and even if chemotaxis is one of the simplest and
best-studied organismic processes, much is still unknown
and it is still an area of intense ongoing research activity.

Closing Discussion
In a narrow sense, the proposal of this article can be seen as
highlighting the notion of “choice” (through selection) as
the key concept towards understanding agency. This overlaps with other accounts of agency (Fulda 2023), and the
way choice is operationalized through adaptiveness in novel
environments shows further overlaps with “inventiveness”
(Tahar 2023) and through the concept of individual good
shows overlaps with “intentionality” (Aaby 2024). Hence
the highlighting of choice is not intended as a particularly
original or distinctive contribution. In fact, one could venture that any psychological metaphor (learning, memory,
representation, etc.) can be used to characterize agency: for
any psychological property X (choice, inventiveness, intentionality, control, learning, utility maximization, and so on),
the question is “under what conditions is it justified to attribute X to the organism”, and “can we consider apparent X
to be genuine X”. Regardless of one’s leading psychological
metaphor, the fundamental questions remain the same.
The main contribution of this article is situated at a more
abstract level and lies in elucidating a more overtly positivist approach to agency that does not venture far from
methodologically adaptationist approaches to behavior and
development. Agency is primarily posited as an explanatory
hypothesis used to account for observed goal-directedness
in behavior and development. Only once agency is deemed
the best explanation of the observations can one consider
drawing conclusions about the underlying metaphysics of
organisms (Lipton 2004).
Agency and natural selection are conceptual duals and
compete as explanatory principles for specific explananda
and behaviors. Natural selection describes a selective process “carried out by” the environment, whereas agency
describes a selective process carried out by the organism.
A biologist need not choose camps here: while they may
compete for narrowly defined explananda, both principles
may be invoked to fully explain observed behaviors or
developmental trajectories. Agency as individual selection
is—potentially—an additional tool in the biologist’s conceptual toolbox: one that may be especially useful to, for
instance, explain adaptive behavior in novel environments
or to explain revealed preferences for nonadaptive behavior
(beauty, play, welfare).
