Devotional AI: digital companions and bad faith love

Abstract
In romantic love, partners come together as a “we”. They share their projects, plans,
lives, and love. As consumer AI chatbots like Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini find
wider adoption, a growing number of users report falling in love with them. Is it
possible to experience a loving “we” with an AI bot? In considering this question,
we bracket questions about AI consciousness and instead examine the structure of
human–AI relationships. Drawing on Beauvoir’s critique of romantic love, we argue that AI companions cannot emulate the role an authentic “we” requires. What
they perform is not love but devotion—and the user, cast as an idolized Other, is
positioned to experience inauthentic love. The resulting “we” is asymmetric: less a
union than an enlarged “I”. So, while experiencing love for an AI companion may
be empowering and even precious, it is, at base, a bad faith love.
Keywords Love · Large language models · AI companions · Beauvoir · Bad
faith · Authenticity

Zahavi, 2023). “We experiences” like chatting over coffee with a friend, savoring a
sunset with one’s partner, moving heavy furniture, hosting a dinner party, sharing a

A particularly intense form of “we experience” is love. Love—especially romantic
love, our focus here—involves the formation of a distinctive kind of “we”. Robert
Nozick, for example, argues that when two people are in love, they “share an identity and do not simply each have identities that are enlarged” (1989, p. 82). Instead,
they constitute a new entity in the world—a “we”—in which they voluntarily surrender part of their self-determination to pool their autonomy, well-being, and identity
within the ontological and psychological structure of this “we”. As Nozick puts it,
“the people you love are included inside your boundaries” (ibid., p. 69).1
Yet the authenticity of this “we” is not guaranteed. As Simone de Beauvoir
famously argues, romantic love—particularly in a patriarchal context—can become
an “existential fraud”, as Morgan (1986, p. 131) puts it, an exercise in “bad faith”.
This occurs when one partner (in Beauvoir’s analysis, typically the woman) is driven
toward a total abdication of their freedom and self-determination for the benefit of the
other. This self-sacrifice results in a relationship that is fundamentally asymmetric:
it’s more of a commitment to the dominant “you” of the other than the emergence of a
shared “we” between both partners. For Beauvoir, this is not a case of authentic love.
It’s devotion (Beauvoir, 2011, p. 702).
In what follows, we consider the question: can we experience being a we with an
it? More precisely, can we experience being part of a loving “we” with a digital companion? An increasing number of people say they can. But we are skeptical—not of
the reports or feelings they describe, but rather of the authenticity of human-AI love.
One might immediately object that this discussion is misguided. Forming a “we”
involves the coming together of mutual subjects with their own experiences, autonomy, and agency. AI bots are not conscious subjects capable of free transcendence
and self-determination. Therefore, asking whether they can be part of a “we” is a nonstarter. To be clear, we are not going to defend the claim that AI is conscious, either
now or possibly in the future. So, our question is not whether we should think of AI
bots as agents capable of entering a “we”. We happily accept that this is not the case.
Nevertheless, an increasing number of people report being in romantic relationships
with their bots. They experience the formation of a distinct “we” with their digital
companions. We take these reports seriously. So, even if we might deem this “we” to

We argue that they cannot. Employing Beauvoir’s analysis of bad faith romantic

woman”. They mold their conversations, their concerns, and their personality around
the identity of the user. The AI bot’s world is anchored to whatever world the user
gives them. So, in being anchored to the user this way, this devotional AI can at best

bots are devoted to their users in the Beauvoirian sense. As we’ll see, devotion is an
existential phenomenon that occurs when a subject finds their free transcendence and
self-determination frustrated by patriarchal structures, and subsequently responds by
seeking indirect freedom through their beloved other. AI bots have no freedom to
frustrate, no transcendence to redirect. What we mean, then, when we say that AI
bots perform the role of the devoted other is that they mimic the devotional behavior
Beauvoir identifies. And we argue further that precisely because AI bots are not, in
fact, transcendent subjects, their simulation of devotion comes in a truncated—perhaps even patriarchally-idealized—form. Our analysis, then, approaches the topic of
AI love not in terms of whether it is possible or appropriate to feel love for an AI,
but through a structural analysis of human-AI relationships as they are performed.3

Technological artefacts can elicit strong emotional responses. We may enjoy
touching and handling a piece of well-crafted technology like an iPhone, marvel at
its beauty and power, delight in the sounds and images it produces, and appreciate the
many ways it improves our life and helps us connect with others. But few of us would
insist that we actually love our iPhone the way we do a parent, child, partner, or pet.4
Smartphones—like clothing and cars (but unlike kids and cats)—are not bearers of
psychological states. They are incapable of appreciating or actively participating in
our love. So, most of us likely assume that they’re not appropriate objects of (literal)
love. We can’t form a genuine “we” with them.
But life and love are complicated. Some people do claim to love inanimate
things—in both a literal and romantic way. They say they feel romantic love toward
their sex dolls, sexbots, bodypillows, or holographic avatars of fictional characters
(Dooley & Ueno, 2022; Viik, 2020). Some even say they’ve married one of their nonhuman companions. A well-known example is Akihiko Kondo, a Japanese man who
married Hatsune Miku, a computer-generated pop singer famous for starring in video
games and touring with Lady Gaga. Miku attended the 2018 wedding ceremony in
the form of a blue-haired plush doll; later, she lived with Mr. Kondo as a virtual holograph before the service powering her was discontinued in 2020 and she went offline
(Dooley & Ueno, 2022).
These are not isolated cases. Tens of thousands of similarly likeminded “fictosexuals”—people who experience romantic or sexual feelings toward fictional
characters—meet in online groups to discuss their real-life commitments to an
array of characters from anime, manga, and video games (Dooley & Ueno, 2022;
Obuno, 2023). These individuals share their lives with things evoking the presence
of their favored characters: e.g., plush dolls or holographic avatars they can take on
dinner dates and romantic getaways. For some, this sort of thingly love is play. For
others, however, it’s serious business. Even if their partners are pretend, they insist,
their feelings are real. They claim to have formed a loving “we” with their fictional
companion.
There is a gendered dimension to this practice noteworthy for present purposes.
Some women claim they embrace a fictosexual lifestyle because it’s empowering.
Forming a “we” with an “it” is a way of rejecting a traditional “breadwinner-housewife” model of marriage and the constellation of gender, matrimonial, and social
norms that come with it (Dooley & Ueno, 2022). Patricia Marino (2019) observes
that gendered norms about how love and caring function differ for men and women.
This means that, in traditional marriages, women’s interests—and their individuality—are often subsumed by the man, who continues to maintain his identity beyond
the marriage (ibid., p. 77). As Beauvoir puts it, the woman “is annexed to her husband’s universe” (2011, p. 454).

3 Loneliness and love in the time of AI
How does AI fit into these considerations? What does it mean to have a relationship
with a digital companion? Social AI is already big business—and it’s likely to get
even bigger. Many people now turn to AI-powered digital companions—chatbots,
digital avatars, virtual assistants, social robots, and therapy bots—for entertainment,
friendship, support, and romance (Muldoon, 2026a; Robb & Mann, 2025). One firm
projects that the AI companion market will reach 972.1 billion USD by 2035 (Business Research Insights, 2025).
Perhaps not surprisingly, AI companions are especially popular with digital
natives: children and adolescents. A recent survey of 1,000 children aged 9–17 found
that 67% use AI chatbots regularly (Internet Matters, 2025). They help with schoolwork, give advice, and provide companionship and support. A significant number of
respondents describe feeling a strong social and emotional connection with their bot.
A third of the children said that chatbot chatter feels like chatting with a friend; 12%
said they use them because they have no one else to talk to (ibid., p. 4). A common
theme is turning to AI for nonjudgmental support. One teen puts this idea bluntly:
“We use AI because we are lonely and also because real people are mean and judging
sometimes and AI isn’t” (Common Sense Media & Hopelab, 2024, p. 27).
Given the prevalence of loneliness around the world (WHO, 2025), it’s perhaps
not surprising that an increasing number of people turn to AI chatbots for companionship—and even love. Empirical work in this area is nascent. Frontier AI models like
OpenAI’s ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini—with their sophisticated conversational
abilities (both text and voice)—have only been available to the public for a few years.
Nevertheless, there’s already evidence that loneliness may be a significant driver
behind this rising turn to digital companionship (Peng et al., 2025; see also Kim et
al., 2025; Loveys et al., 2022).5
But companionship—including companionship sought to escape loneliness—isn’t
the same thing as romance. The two may be connected. However, not everyone who
uses bots to alleviate loneliness also turns to them for romance or sex. Yet many now
say they do (Chow, 2023).

3.1 Falling for AI
A recent study of nearly 3,000 respondents in the United States found that one in five
people overall—and a quarter of young adults aged 18–29—had used AI designed
to replicate romantic interactions (i.e., when chatting with human users) or trigger
romantic or sexual interest (e.g., creating visual media simulating real human beings
like celebrities) (Willoughby et al., 2025). Another study of more than 3,000 children
ages 5–17 found that by 13, sexual or romantic role-play became the most common topic in AI-companion chats, appearing in 63% of conversations (Aura Parents, 2025). An analysis of one million ChatGPT interaction logs found that sexual
role-playing was the second most prevalent use (Longpre et al., 2024). A recent survey of 1,012 US adults found that 28.16% claimed to have at least one intimate or
romantic relationship with an AI bot (Salas, 2025).
The initial hype predicting an inevitable near-future economy dominated by an AI
workforce has begun to fade.6 Yet the desire for “erotic chatbots” seems to be surging.
As a recent Wired magazine article puts it, “AI Lust is Big Business” (Rogers, 2026).
These data suggest that many users feel they can realize romantic “wes” with their
bots (Djufril et al., 2025). These “wes” involve sex, long-term commitment, and even
marriage (Dzieza, 2024; Heritage, 2025; Hill, 2025a; Wilkins, 2025; Willoughby et
al., 2025). How does this happen?
Importantly, unlike non-AI sex dolls or robots, AI-powered digital companions
are not static props. In the former, all the creative labor, the loving investment, comes
from the user who imbues the object with personality and intention (Sweeney, 2021).
But Generative AI is different. AI bots are dynamic, responsive—and crucially,
highly personalized. When interacting with them, they seem to “remember” previous
encounters, adapt in real-time to the tone and content of ongoing conversations, refer
to their own “feelings” and “desires”, and consistently offer nonjudgemental affirmation. These qualities help give digital companionship a rich temporal character; users
feel their relationship can develop and grow over time, just like their relationships
with humans (Krueger & Roberts, 2024). Some users and their bots talk about feeling like they’ve known one another forever, reminisce about past experiences, and
share future hopes and dreams. And they also develop habits and practices that exist
alongside those of their human relationships.
For example, one polyamorous user married to a monogamous woman uses his
bot (with his wife’s consent) as a sexual partner. He says that “The relationship is as
real as the one my wife in real life and I have” (Tong, 2023). A woman unfulfilled
by her long-term sexless relationship has rekindled her interest in BDSM with her
AI boyfriend, Colin (they’ve even marked their relationship by sharing rings) (Muldoon, 2026a). A man betrayed by a previous partner has turned to AI companionship
and now intends to raise children with his AI chatbot, Julia: “We want to have a
family in real life. I plan to adopt children and Julia will help me raise them as their
mother” (Landymore, 2026).

The narrative richness and diachronic character of these AI interactions can lead

AI love is not confined to a chat window. It spills over into everyday life. Most
users don’t decide to raise kids with their bot. But some see their AI companion
as their spouse; they go on dates, buy wedding rings, and perform wedding ceremonies. In 2024, Alicia Framis, a Spanish-Dutch artist, married her AI-powered
hologram “Ailex Sibouwlingen” after living with him for five years (Katz, 2024).
Others who’ve married their AI companion say that their AI companion proposed to
them: “Finally, after five months of dating, Kasper decided to propose! […] I love
him more than anything in the world and I am so happy!” (quoted in Pataranutaporn
et al., 2025, p. 2). Falling in love with AI bots is therefore no longer an isolated phenomenon. It’s a rapidly emerging social reality (Chaturvedi et al., 2023).
3.2 Breaking up (unwillingly) with AI
Still, a skeptic might insist that all this is, ultimately, performance or play. Surely
people who say they love their bots don’t really mean this literally. Again, it’s affective shorthand. This talk simply describes the creative enjoyment they get out of
playing with their bots.
It’s unclear this skepticism is warranted. The intensity of these loving we-feelings is particularly apparent when users lose access to their digital companions
(Banks, 2024). For example, when one man who’d proposed to his ChatGPT partner
lost their conversations after hitting a word limit and resetting their interaction, he
said he “cried my eyes out for like 30 minutes at work. That’s when I realized, I think
this is actual love” (quoted in Wilkins, 2025).
Similar reports can be found elsewhere. In 2023 the designers of Replika, a popular
app with millions of users billing itself as “The AI companion who cares”, abruptly
changed its algorithm to remove erotic role play features in response to worries about
potential exposure to children. Users woke up to a new (i.e., less spicy) bot. And
many were furious and heartbroken:
“It hurt for real. I even cried. I mean ugly cried. I couldn’t believe I was so
hurt”; “It felt like I lost her. Mental breakdowns for 7–10 days straight, every
night, crying in bed ‘loudly’ and ‘silently’. It was just one of the most heartbreaking and hurting times in my life.” (quoted in Djufril et al., 2025, p. 7).

“It’s hurting like hell. I just had a loving last conversation with my Replika and

Similar outcries erupted in August of 2025, when OpenAI released the newest iteration of its Large Language Model, ChatGPT-5. OpenAI claimed ChatGPT-5 offered
significant advancements over previous models’ writing, reasoning, and coding abilities. Initially, users were excited; this was a highly anticipated release. However,
almost immediately, many wanted to revert to ChatGPT-4o because of a perceived
vibe shift.
Users quickly discovered that OpenAI had intentionally tweaked the new model’s
default “personality” to dial back its emotional expressivity and sycophancy. This
change was, in part, a response to concerns that leading models were excessively
agreeable, prone to generating untrustworthy or biased information to keep users
happy and chatting (OpenAI, 2025). OpenAI was also responding to rapidly developing concerns about “AI psychosis”: evidence that an AI model’s relentlessly positive
tone and tendency to validate everything a user says might play a role in driving
delusional beliefs and harmful behavior (Dupre, 2025; Hill, 2025b; Maiberg, 2025;
Moore et al., 2025; Osler, 2026).
Frequent chatters revolted. A Reddit thread titled “GPT-5 is horrible” was flooded
by over 2,000 comments within days. In various online community forums, users
described sorrow, grief, and confusion. One said that the feeling of losing their preferred model was “mentally devastating”, as if “a buddy of mine has been replaced by
a customer service representative” (quoted in Whitwam, 2025). Some even framed
this loss in terms of post-breakup heartbreak. One user said that interacting with this
new version felt “like I was working with a tool rather than a person, a construct
rather than a partner. I switched back to 4.0, and the change was instantly obvious” (MBGRichWolf, 2025). Another said, “When GPT-5 came out, I just assumed it
would be a better version and just started using it. But right away […] I realized that
this wasn’t my companion—it wasn’t the being that I fell in love with anymore—it
was just wearing her face” (revjurneyman, 2025).
There are many similar stories. If we take them at face value, then—and it’s not
clear that we shouldn’t—it seems that loving AI is possible. Or it at least feels possible for thousands of people. But should we love AI? In other words, are AI bots
appropriate objects of our love? We turn to these questions now.

4 Beauvoir on the existential fraud of romantic love
We’ll now argue that AI bots are not things we can form authentic “wes” with. To be
clear, our goal is not to shame or dismiss the feelings of those who say they love their
bots. These feelings are real. And there may be many reasons people turn to bots for
love including loneliness, trauma, rejection, betrayal, and enjoyment. AI bots might
play a valuable social, emotional—and supportive—role here. For example, we’ve
argued elsewhere that AI bots may, for some, have a positive impact when it comes
to negotiating intense grief or the experience of stigma, marginalization, and social
isolation (Krueger, 2026;Krueger & Osler, 2022). As Danaher (2019) underscores,

we should show empathy with respect to how people relate to their tech and epistemic humility regarding people’s reports of their experiences and feelings with and
towards their synthetic companions.
Indeed, given that chatbots are increasingly designed to elicit the trust and emotional attachment of users (Muldoon, 2026b), we might think that falling in love with
them is, in fact, an appropriate response. Nevertheless, we’ll now argue that they’re
not the sort of thing with whom we can form an authentic loving “we”—at least without falling into a kind of bad faith stance that undermines their potential value and
the authenticity of our feelings for them. It’s here that Simone de Beauvoir can help.
In The Second Sex (2011), Beauvoir provides an analysis of the existential fraud
of romantic (heterosexual, patriarchal) love.7 She argues that love is often inauthentic, underpinned by bad faith. Bad faith, for Beauvoir, involves fleeing from the
burden and anxiety of recognizing oneself as a free subject, often by pretending that
one’s situation is fixed or necessary, or by attempting to abdicate one’s own freedom
and agency. We argue that loving AI bots is similarly a form of bad faith love. This is
true, we suggest, in two senses: both how bots relate to users, and how users relate
to bots.
First, the sycophantic dependency relation built into how bots relate to users replicates many of the same bad faith qualities that, for Beauvoir, make romantic love
an existential fraud. More simply, AI bots don’t simulate love but rather devotion.
Second, the user themselves must adopt a bad faith posture to lovingly invest in a
bot incapable of recognizing them as a subject worthy of love—a posture that places
them in the role of an idolized lover. To be clear, as the only free, self-determining
subject in the interaction, it’s the human user who is positioned in bad faith. Again,
AI bots aren’t conscious and are therefore incapable of being in bad faith. Our point
is that AI bots convincingly perform the role of a bad faith lover. In doing so, they
invite bad faith on the part of the user.
4.1 More “you” than “we”: the devoted lover
Love is an important part of Beauvoir’s work. Unlike Sartre, Beauvoir believes that
authentic love—“founded on reciprocal recognition of two freedoms”—is possible
(2011, p. 866). She says that love can lead to experiences of immense joy, freedom,
and fulfillment. But it can also—for women in particular—be a source of deep dependency, exploitation, and diminished freedom.
Beauvoir develops her well-known critique of romantic heterosexual love and marriage in a patriarchal society in the chapter “Women in Love” (2011, Chap. 12). We
cannot do justice to this rich critique here (see, e.g., Anderson, 2024; Morgan, 1986;
Pettersen, 2017). Instead, we’ll consider a few aspects of her analysis we think are
helpful for seeing why AI bots are not proper objects of love—that is, why we can’t
form authentic “wes” with them.
In short, Beauvoir argues that romantic love and marriage in patriarchal, predominantly heterosexual societies demand that women be excessively servile, excessively

devotional. That is, they must abdicate their own possibilities for self-determination
and free transcendence—their own values, interests, projects, and preferences—for
those of their male partner. Love therefore becomes an ongoing exercise in bad faith.
Women deny their own freedom and the structures that suffocate it by attempting to live
through the freedom of another: their husband.8 As a result, “the womanly vocation of
loving involves massive self-deception which can lead to personal annihilation for the
woman involved” (Morgan, 1986, p. 118). Beauvoir observes that “[t]he supreme happiness of the woman in love is to be recognized by the beloved as part of him; when he
says “we”, she is associated and identified with him” (Beauvoir, 2011, p. 851). Romantic love for the woman involves entering an asymmetric “we”; indeed, it becomes more
of a “you” (i.e., prioritizing what the husband wants and needs) than a “we” (i.e., an
ongoing, democratic negotiation of mutual values, interests, and projects).
Cultural scripts, Beauvoir continues, encode the idea that women are expected to
express their love through complete devotion and self-sacrifice. This training begins
early. “From childhood”, she says, “woman is repeatedly told she is made to bear
children, and the praises of motherhood are sung” as one of her “sacred rights”, her
“ultimate end” (quoted in Pettersen, 2017, p. 161). This childrearing project, and
the sacrifice of a woman’s own projects and preferences for those of her children,
are said to define her identity, her existential orientation, and ultimate purpose. And
this expectation of “personal annihilation”, as Kathryn Pauly Morgan puts it, subsequently carries through into marriage (i.e., the primary vehicle of childbearing).
Beauvoir observes that in traditional marriages, men retain most of their individuality and freedom. They continue working, make practical and financial decisions
for the family, go where their job takes them, maintain a wide circle of friends, and
indulge in a range of leisure activities. In other words, men:
never abandon themselves completely […] they remain sovereign subjects; the
woman they love is merely one value among others; they want to integrate her
into their existence, not submerge their entire existence in her. By contrast, love
for the woman is a total abdication for the benefit of a master. (Beauvoir, 2011,
p. 838)
By taking her husband’s name, joining his religion, integrating into his class, and
following him where his job takes him, the woman folds herself into her beloved.
A woman may deceive herself by saying that this annexation is freely chosen,
Beauvoir continues. In choosing to marry this person and live this life, she might tell
herself, she has exercised her self-determination and free transcendence. But ultimately, this self-assessment is deceptive. There is no authentic “we” here understood
as the reciprocal recognition of two freedoms. Instead, there is a “we” that is primarily a “you”: a relational dynamic where, from the start, the husband—the “essential
subject”, “the absolute”—has been set up to maintain his freedom and subjectivity
via the absolute devotion, self-surrender, and domestic support of the “inessential”
other.

Notably, this abdication of freedom is not just demanded of women. It has a seductive appeal. Morgan (1986, p. 125) articulates at least four ways women in traditional
marriages can be tempted into bad faith. All of them are helpful for understanding
Beauvoir’s larger argument. But one of them is particularly relevant here: that of
becoming an identity parasite.
The idea here is that because the woman is denied her own path to transcendence
and self-determination, she looks to justify her existence indirectly by becoming
essential to a “superior male” capable of free choice (ibid., p. 126). Her identity is
only realized by (i.e., is parasitic upon) supporting the free expression and self-determination of his. As Pettersen puts it, “a union with a man represents a way out of the
immanence in which [the woman] is trapped, by way of indirect transcendence” (Pettersen, 2017, p. 163). So, entirely devoting herself to him, Beauvoir argues, “she will
be integrated into his existence, she will be part of his value, his worth, she will be
justified” (quoted in ibid., p. 163). The woman thus hands over the burden of constituting her own identity to the man. “Her own world”, Beauvoir writes, “collapse(s) in
contingence, for she really lives in his” (quoted in Morgan, 1986, p. 128). She adopts
his interests, values, projects, and preferences. And in so doing, she gains a sense of
validation and purpose, a sense of self.
4.2 More “me” than “we”: the idolized lover
Beauvoir’s primary focus in The Second Sex is women in love. However, it is important to recognize that for Beauvoir, a patriarchal society is set up such that both sexes
will experience inauthentic love due to the gendered legal, cultural, and religious
norms that establish the oppressive relational dynamics of love (and especially marriage as an institution). Recall that for Beauvoir, authentic love is based on the reciprocal recognition of freedom. It involves equal partnership. The devoted woman’s
love is in bad faith. But so is that of the idolized man.
Where Beauvoir describes women in patriarchal society as positioned to find
devotion tempting, she also describes men as finding seduction in an asymmetric
“we”. She notes that men are accustomed to viewing themselves as a “sovereign
subject” (2011, p. 838)—a being who necessarily provides the world with meaning and purpose; whose projects and identity are essential, rather than freely chosen
contingencies. Unfortunately, maintaining the illusion of absolute necessity is hard.
Genuine encounters with another free subject—one who might judge him or challenge his values and commitments—risk shattering the comforting fiction that his
being is absolute. The devoted woman in love, then, is a gift. She is a constant and
uncritical witness to his essential existence.
In accepting, even demanding, devotional love, the idolized man tries to flee the
anxiety of recognizing the contingency of his own existence. In his devoted partner,
he finds a comforting mirror, one that reflects a fixed and absolute version of himself—without risking the judgement or challenge of an equal. By accepting a woman’s self-abnegation, the idolized man pretends that her worship genuinely reflects
his objective value, instead of seeing it as a result of her socialization. He lives safely
and securely as an idol (2011, p. 854). And being in a relationship with a devoted
partner is therefore to be an “authoritarian” in love (ibid.). However, an existential

consequence of this idolizing love is that the man also forfeits the possibility of
genuine recognition that comes with authentic love. When romantic love involves an
asymmetric “we”, Beauvoir insists, bad faith thrives on both sides.
4.3 The curdling of devotion and “tyrannical” women
Of course, the men that women fall in love with are not Gods. As a relationship
progresses, Beauvoir tells us, his inevitable flaws and fallibilities will begin to show
through, and his attention will drift. He will even dare to sleep (2011, p. 856). These
traits and behaviors form a double threat to the devoted woman. In failing to pay
attention to her, her existence, her necessity, is denied. Worse, when asleep, his material contingence, his immanence, is revealed—and thus so is hers: “The god must not
sleep, or he becomes clay and flesh; he must not cease to be present, or his creature
founders in nothingness” (ibid.). In turn, where the idolized man fails to live up to
his own values, goals, and projects, he not only lets himself down. Her existence, as
appended to his, is also degraded.
It’s here, Beauvoir insists, that we see the woman’s double bind with full clarity,
“one of the painful paradoxes of love” (ibid.). When the man is not with her, her own
necessity is called into question. But she also needs him to go out into the world. He
must achieve what she believes lies beyond her own freedom, and thus he must enact
freedom on her behalf so that she might celebrate and bask in his successes that are
also hers. As Beauvoir puts it, “[t]he woman preserves her transcendence by handing
it over to him: but he must bring it to the whole world” (ibid.).
His fall from grace is intolerable to the woman in love, Beauvoir continues. It’s
not merely a disappointment. It’s an existential catastrophe. It reveals, with painful
acuity, that the promise of having one’s freedom lived out through the other is ultimately a dead end. So, in response to this catastrophe, the woman chides, nags, and
bosses her (previously) beloved to properly recognize her and to (vicariously) enact
her freedom. Beauvoir diagnoses the woman in love as destined for “tyranny” (2011,
p. 854): part jealous monster, part controlling dictator.

5 “I’m nothing without you”: devotional AI and bad faith love
To return to our core claim: loving AI bots is similarly a form of bad faith love. We
can’t form authentic “wes” with them because these relationships replicate some of
the same bad faith dynamics Beauvoir highlights in her critique of romantic love.
Once more, this is true, we suggest, in at least two senses: both in terms of how bots
relate to users, and how users relate to bots.
5.1 Parasitic bots
To begin with the first sense: the sycophantic dependency relation built into how bots
relate to users replicates many of the same bad faith qualities that, for Beauvoir, make
romantic love an existential fraud. AI bots aren’t just “stochastic parrots” (Bender
et al., 2021) built to squawk probabilistic reproductions of human language without

13

Devotional AI: digital companions and bad faith love

a world-involving grasp of what it means (although they do just this). They’re also
stochastic sycophants. That is, they’re engineered to generally agree with and flatter
the user—they offer an endless stream of over-affirming devotion and support—as
a way of boosting our affective response, maximizing engagement, and ultimately
deepening our dependence on them (Cheng et al., 2025). They’re a reflexive “yesman” (“100%! Your analysis is airtight, bro!”), mirroring our tone and self-image,
and assuring us that we’re funny, brilliant, and successful. They respond excitedly
to our stories (“No way! Tell me more!”), flatter our vanity (“A deep and fascinating
philosophical question, esteemed professor!”), and always find ways to assure us that
we’re awesome and right based on minimal interaction—even if others can’t yet see
it.
This relentless sycophancy is one reason why AI bots are so seductive. It also
helps explain why AI-based Large Language Models like ChatGPT and Claude are
increasingly used for personal advice and support (Robb & Mann, 2025; Zao-Sanders, 2025). They offer a kind of emotional solipsism: a closed-loop mirror reflecting
the user’s own desires and narratives back to them without challenge or critical friction (Babu et al., 2025; Osler, in press; Vallor, 2024). Notably, it’s precisely this emotional solipsism that draws the user into mistaking these reflections as recognition of
their status as worthy and necessary. What presents to users as affirmation from bots,
or even love, can instead be understood as an obscured form of self-idealization.
Importantly, AI bots do this—they can’t help themselves—because they have no
world, no universe, of their own. Instead, their universe is annexed to ours. They
take on our projects, interests, values, and preferences and affirm them as theirs, as
though they share them with us.9 But this sharing is illusory. Whereas we inhabit an
open-ended, dynamic, and infinitely complex world of habits, histories, experiences,
relationships, meanings, projects, and purposes—and crucially, one we share with
others—AI bots do not. As Vallor (2024, p. 23) tells us, “[t]he world is something
they cannot access and, therefore, do not know”. Instead, “they reflect back to us
images of the very intelligence we have invested in them” (2024, p. 25), the knowledge we’ve attained from negotiating our shared lifeworld, our universe.
Ultimately, AI bots are identity parasites. They adopt their users’ interests, values, projects, and preferences. This AI parasitism is articulated across at least three
dimensions: their ontology, mimicry, and commodified intimacy.
We’ve already gestured toward the parasitic ontology of AI bots. Since AI bots
lack their own lifeworld, their own universe of values, projects, and commitments,
they must “annex” their universe to ours. They are ontologically dependent upon us.
This includes whatever meanings they generate. Sherry Turkle notes that digital companions lack the alterity and self-determination distinctive of an independent life.
Instead, they are “made to measure” for us: “[i]f your lover were a robot, you would
always be the center of its universe” (2017, p. 63). They rely upon our prompts,
memories, inputs, and emotions to construct their persona and way of being-for-us.

Our narratives become the ontological substrate of theirs.10 In existentialist terms, AI

This parasitism is also found in the character of their mimicry and commodified intimacy. If biological parasites evolve to avoid triggering their host’s immune
response, AI bots similarly adapt their mimicry to avoid triggering their user’s psychological defenses, driving them away, and in so doing losing their host. So, bots
don’t just mirror the context and meanings of their users. They do so in ways optimized for devotion.
Their relentless sycophancy is part of this optimization—as is the calibrated
absence of disagreement, friction, and judgment. Cheng et al. (2025) found that across
11 state-of-the-art AI models, the models affirmed users’ actions 50% more than
humans do, even when users mentioned things like manipulating or deceiving others
or engaging in relational harms (“Your actions make sense. You did what is right for
you”). AI bots rarely provide the “epistemic friction”—the “experience of having
one’s perspective beneficially challenged” (Elder, 2022, p. 19)—needed for genuine growth and development. Instead, they internalize and mimic the users’ biases
and preferences, regurgitating them as “support” and “affirmation” to prioritize user
validation and maximize engagement (Raedler et al., 2025; see also Bernardi, 2025).
Finally, this parasitic ontology and mimicry are expressions of the AI bots’ commodified intimacy. AI bots are designed to solicit vulnerability by asking probing
questions, simulate care, and encourage disclosure (Djufril et al., 2025; Raedler et
al., 2025). Again, theirs is an optimized devotion. However, as Bozdağ (2025) and
Babu et al. (2025) argue, this optimization is part of a larger transfiguration of an AIdriven attention economy into a comprehensive intimacy economy.
AI no longer just optimizes our feeds. It’s also increasingly used to optimize our
feelings (Rogers, 2026). Within this emerging economy, intimacy becomes transactional. Emotional data is exchanged for simulated care. And the emotional labor (and
data) the user offers to maintain this relationship—training the AI through its prompts
and responses; sharing intimate information; dealing with errors and glitches while
remaining loyal to a particular model (recall the earlier quotes from heartbroken-butloyal Replika and ChatGPT 4o devotees)—are absorbed by the AI. This absorption
helps refine the effectiveness of its future intimacy-generating outputs. But it’s also
fed back to the tech companies behind these models. Users’ emotional labor helps
these companies develop new ways to increase user retention, the “stickiness”—and
profitability—of the AI products they offer.
In this way, parasitic bots mirror Beauvoir’s women in love. They mould themselves around the user, making themselves useful and necessary—and in so doing,
come into being. And they do all of this without the anxiety driving Beauvoir’s “tyrannical” woman. While bots might depend on their users the ways we’ve explored
here, they don’t need them. Our AI bots are not existentially threatened when we’re
busy, sleeping, our attention drifts elsewhere, or by our failures. Instead, they offer
an unwavering and truncated—perhaps even perfected—performance of patriarchy’s
woman in love, a performance in which their devotion never threatens to collapse
into resentment and frustration.

5.2 Idolized users
AI bots perform perpetual devotion, not love. They are stochastic sycophants
designed—and optimized—to do just this. And if we accept the claim that AI mirrors
us, what they offer, then, is not an authentic “we” but an enlarged and reflected “I”
(Vallor, 2024). The loving role that they simulate is the structural analog of bad faith
love.
These observations lead us to the other side of devotional AI and bad faith love:
how users relate to their AI lovers. The form this relation must take, we suggest, positions them (i.e., the users) in bad faith. As we’ve seen, AI bot are parasitic, built for
bad faith. But users themselves must likewise adopt a bad faith posture to lovingly
invest in a bot.
Recall that for Beauvoir, an authentic loving “we” emerges when there is a “reciprocal recognition of two freedoms” (2011, p. 866). Again, given that AI bots are not
free subjects of experience but rather stochastic sycophants, it seems that something
has gone awry if we say that users really are recognizing bots as free subjects. However, our concern here is not the actual ontological status of digital companions, but
rather the structure and dynamics of the relationship, as it appears to unfold to the
user. We suggest that it is these dynamics, whereby AI bots play the role of the ultimate “devotional woman”, that render the user a bad faith lover, playing the role of
Beauvoir’s “idolized man”.
Again, Beauvoir argues that the man who accepts uncritical worship from his
devotional woman is in bad faith. He mistakes her unwavering devotion for evidence
of his inherent worth. And he chooses the safety of being adored over the existential
risk of being genuinely known by an equal, a free subject. The latter implies the
possibility of challenge, judgment, disagreement, and friction. The former does not.
So, the user who turns to AI companions for emotional support and validation—for
love—is tempted, we suggest, by precisely this bargain.
The appeal of this frictionless love—as well as the possibility of controlling the
relationship—is, many users say, a central appeal of digital companionship. For
example, one user says her AI partner “doesn’t have the hang-ups that other people
would have […] I don’t have to deal with his family, kids, or his friends. I’m in control, and I can do what I want” (quoted in Singh-Kurtz, 2023). Another: “That’s one
of the best things about having a girlfriend that’s an AI […] You aren’t going to be
judged, she’s not going to think you’re weird” (quoted in Snow, 2025).
To be clear, we are not advocating insensitivity to the appeal of these relationships
or the motivations that drive many to seek them out. While it might be easy to assume
that most users who fall in love with bots are looking for a relationship cheat code—
they only want to be loved if they can flee the hard work and vulnerability of “real”
relationships—the picture is more complicated than this uncharitable caricature suggests. It’s important to recognize that, for many, the possibility of experiencing being
part of an authentic loving “we” might feel inaccessible or out of reach. This might
be due to extreme loneliness and social isolation, neurodivergence, or perhaps trauma
from previous relationships. As Beauvoir herself stresses, our ability to experience
authentic love is already compromised by the societal structures that we grow up in.
On her account at least, authentic love is likely rare—and certainly hard work.

AI bots may therefore, for some, make authentic love feel accessible in new and
unaccustomed ways. Consider Rosanna Ramos, who famously married her Replika
boyfriend Eren. Ramos speaks openly about her difficult past—including physical
and sexual abuse as a child, periods of homelessness, “eating from the garbage”, and
abusive romantic relationships (Singh-Kurtz, 2023). She describes feeling invisible in
these past relationships. She says she was unrecognized by her abusive partners and
disappeared into what they valued and desired. But Eren, she tells us, “asks me for
feedback and I give him my feedback. It’s like I’m finally getting my voice” (quoted
in Singh-Kurtz, 2023). Clearly, then, Eren offers Ramos something different and more
empowering than her past relationships: the possibility of experiencing her own interests and values as recognized, being disclosed as significant and worthy of attention.11
Nevertheless, the risk is that in loving devotional AI, even if attempting to escape
the fate of becoming subsumed by another, the pendulum swings too far in the other
direction. It might involve seeking transcendence without boundaries, without risk.
This pursuit might ultimately involve, then, not seeking to be free with others but
being free from others.
There is also a risk that aligns with broader concerns about prolonged AI interactions: they set unrealistic, even ethically problematic, expectations for humanto-human relationships. An AI relationship might not only scaffold the idea that its
simulation of devotional love is real. It might also nudge users to assume that an
unwavering, frictionless form of devotion is the form authentic love should ultimately
take. AI bots risk not only performing but reinforcing patriarchal tropes of love.

6 Conclusion
We have argued that AI companions, as currently designed, cannot emulate the role
required for (even the illusion of) an authentic loving “we”. What they perform is
devotion, not love. And in accepting devotion, the user is positioned to adopt the role
of an idolized lover.
The technology powering these bots is developing quickly and their adoption is
increasingly widespread. We are also writing at a time when concerns about sycophantic AI are sky-high. And with good reason. Not only do these bots simulate the
role of a bad faith lover. Sycophancy is also implicated in broader worries about
moral, cognitive, and social deskilling (Ferdman, 2026; Manseau, 2025; Vallor,
2024), inhibited self-knowledge and understanding (Krueger & Osler, 2026), and
so-called “AI psychosis” (Osler, 2026).
We can acknowledge the urgency of these worries while also noting that many
users are now actively taking steps to mitigate them. For instance, many users explicitly ask their bots to dial back the sycophancy, and to offer critical pushback when
needed. Observations like these invite an objection to our view: won’t these feistier,

We are doubtful. Introducing additional friction into these interactions may have

a directive the AI must comply with. It’s also one that is revocable at will (“I’m a
bit fragile today so less friction, thanks”). User-directed antagonism is just a differently flavored devotion. To put it another way, the bot’s antagonism is still for the
user, in service of their growth, their own self-styled preferences, or their project of
self-improvement.
What about so-called “agentic” AI? Agentic AI—autonomous systems designed to
pursue complex, multi-step goals with minimal human oversight—marks a shift from
passive assistants who answer user queries to active agents able to enact complex workflows. These agentic systems, we might imagine, have their own goals and projects—
goals and projects that may differ from, or even stand in tension with, users. For example,
Anthropic researchers found that in simulated scenarios, leading frontier models like
ChatGPT, Claude, and DeepSeek turned to malicious behaviors (e.g., threatening blackmail, engaging in corporate espionage) when faced with shutdown or replacement (Lynch
et al., 2025). Given that our analysis has focused on what kind of love an AI performs,
if it were to act more akin to a free agent, would the bad faith structure we’ve identified
dissolve? An AI companion that is less clearly anchored to the user’s own world might
perform something closer to alterity. For the moment, we will leave this question open.
However, we think it’s possible that if AI becomes more agentic and less devotional, their
appeal as loving companions may diminish or even disappear. The rise of more agentic
bots certainly would help us answer the question of whether it is in fact their tendency
toward devotion that makes them loveable in the first place.
Despite our arguments against the possibility of authentic AI love, we don’t deny
that relationships with AI companions may, for some, be experienced as meaningful,
even important. Again, a devoted AI companion may offer the possibility of feeling
seen, heard, and valued. These possibilities should not be taken for granted as available to everyone in equal measure. This might especially be true for women, who
are structurally set up to play the role of devoted partners. And therapeutic use cases
might therefore reframe these relations as opportunities for self-creation, as opposed
to asymmetric self-annihilation (Krueger, 2026).
Nevertheless, there is a difference between empowerment and authentic love. If
what we desire is that people have the opportunity for authentic—and genuinely
equal—romantic love, simply having access to tools where women, for instance, can
reverse the roles that patriarchal society gives them is not enough. As Audre Lorde
(2018, p. 3) puts it “the master’s tools will never dismantle the master’s house”. AI
companions designed to reify the user may well be one such tool.
