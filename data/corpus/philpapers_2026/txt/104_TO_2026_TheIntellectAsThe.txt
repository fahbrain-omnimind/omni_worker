The Intellect as the Dynamics of Question and
Answer:
Why the Answer Can Be Outsourced to LLMs but
the Question Cannot
T.O. (IASER Practitioner)
September 2026

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) return fluent and often correct answers, and with them
comes the fear that something in them asks, decides and pursues. This paper asks what,
in the use of an LLM, is actually handed over to the machine, and what cannot be. The
answer is structural. Thinking has long been described as the soul’s talk with itself: one
asks, and one answers. We show that this is the working form of the intellect: an asymmetric
dynamics of question and answer, in which the answering side is intelligence — the execution
of operations — and the asking side is a capacity for selection not fixed by any prior sequence
of operations. The dynamics is led by the asking side, and neither side alone makes the
dynamics. The use of an LLM places the answering side outside. Defining outsourcing
neutrally, as the transfer of the execution of an operation to an external operation system,
we prove that the execution of a selected operation can be outsourced, but the selection
itself cannot. An LLM can generate interrogative sentences, but generating questions is not
bearing the question; and an output stands as the answer to a question only through the side
that asks — a claim about its standing as an answer, not about its validity, which remains
independent of authorship. A six-step chain carries these results into actual use: every use
of an LLM already has the structure of Intelligence Amplification by Stimulated Emission of
Reasoning (IASER), in which the human asks and adopts and the machine develops; when
this structure is not seen, the asking side is projected onto the machine, the phantom of
machine intellect appears, and judgment is ceded. The phantom is thereby identified as
a misattribution across layers — the attribution of the question to the side to which only
the answer was outsourced — which has no structural realization. The argument is first
given at the level of concepts, and can be followed without prior knowledge of any formal
framework. It is then grounded axiomatically: the account of the intellect as the junction of
an operational layer and a layer of selective resolution, given in the companion paper The
Intellect as The Subject of Intelligence, shows that the distinction on which the argument
rests is structurally valid. No new axiom is introduced. The analysis is structural throughout
and makes no claim about the internal mechanisms of any system.

1

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) now return answers that are fluent, detailed and, in many
domains, correct. The fear that accompanies them is that something is present in them which
asks, decides and pursues — that the machine has become a subject. The question of the present
paper is structural: what, in the use of an LLM, is handed over to the machine, and what cannot
be; and why does confusing the two produce the appearance of an intellect in the machine?
The answer can be stated in ordinary terms. An answer is produced by carrying out operations, and carrying out operations can be done elsewhere, by an external system. A question
is constituted by a selection — of what is to be pursued, and of whether an answer is to be
1

adopted — that is not fixed by any prior sequence of operations; such a selection is not the
kind of thing that the transfer of operations can carry. The answer can be outsourced, and the
question cannot. The phantom of machine intellect is the failure to see this asymmetry.
The paper has two layers, and they serve two kinds of reader.
• The conceptual layer (Sections 2–7) gives the whole argument in ordinary concepts: what
happens when an LLM is used, the distinction between the execution and the selection of
operations, why the answer can be outsourced but the question cannot, the intellect as the
dynamics of question and answer, the chain that leads from actual use to the phantom,
and the origin of the phantom. It can be followed without prior knowledge of any formal
framework.
• The axiomatic layer (Section 8) shows why the distinction on which the conceptual argument rests is structurally valid. It draws on the formal elucidation of the intellect in The
Intellect as The Subject of Intelligence [1], which rests on Operatiology and Noology.
The present paper does not redefine the intellect. It first examines its dynamics at the level of
question and answer, and then makes explicit the axiomatic structure that grounds this description. The order in which the results were obtained — from the companion paper to the present
one — and the order in which they are presented — from the phenomenon of LLM use, through
question and answer and their dynamics, to the axiomatic ground — are deliberately separated.
The method is structural. No claim is made about the internal mechanisms of LLMs, about
psychology, or about the empirical prevalence of any pattern of use. Every claim has one of four
statuses:
• [I] Inherited: a result established in a prior paper, used as a premise.
• [Def] Definition: a definition of the present paper.
• [D] Derived: a claim derived in the present paper from [I] and [Def].
• [R] Reading: a declared correspondence by which a structure of a prior paper is read
within the framework of the present paper; it asserts consistency, not that the prior paper
proved the correspondence.
Inherited results are stated where they are first used. The derivations have been checked item
by item for logical closure.

2

What Happens When an LLM Is Used

We begin with the use of an LLM as it presents itself, and fix the terms in which it will be
described.
Definition 2.1 (Operation system). An operation system is a system whose results are produced
by executing operations drawn from, and composed out of, its own repertoire O. An operation
is a map on the states S of the matter at hand; the operations that can be formed from the
repertoire are the compositions of its elements, written hOi. Whatever an operation system does
is the execution of some element of hOi; in particular, whenever an operation system resolves
which operation it executes next, that resolution is itself the result of executing some element
of hOi on the input at hand.
Definition 2.2 (New operation). Relative to an operation system with repertoire O, an operation gnew ∈ Maps(S, S) is new if it is not obtainable by composing elements of O (gnew ∈
/ hOi).
We label development by composition within the repertoire Class A, and the introduction of a
new operation Class B.
2

Definition 2.3 (Structure of use). (D1) Use. A use of an LLM is an interaction with exactly
two participants, a human h and the LLM.
(D2) Closed interaction. In a use, the LLM is an operation system (Definition 2.1) with repertoire OLLM . Its outputs are results of executing compositions of elements of OLLM , and it
brings in no input from outside OLLM .
(D3) Adoption. The decision of h to accept, reject or modify an output.
(D4) Attribution. Representing an act as the act of an agent.
(D5) Awareness. h represents the agent of question-setting and of the decision to adopt as h
himself or herself.
(D6) Closure of attribution. Each component of the intellectual work perceived in a use is
attributed either to oneself or to the machine.
(D7) Ceding of judgment. Treating the machine as the subject of the decision to adopt, and
adopting without (D13).
(D8) Judgment-adoption. Adoption performed under (D5) and accompanied by (D13).
(D9) Self-attribution. Attributing a component of the intellectual work to oneself.
(D10) Projection. Attributing question-setting or the decision to adopt (the components on the
side of the question) to the machine.
(D11) Entry of operations. An operation newly used in the interaction enters either as a participant’s output or as an input brought in by a participant.
(D12) Three components. The intellectual work perceived in a use consists of question-setting,
development, and the decision to adopt.
(D13) Own evaluation. An evaluation of the output performed by the adopter and represented
as the adopter’s own act. Since it includes that representation, (D13) presupposes (D5).
Nothing in these definitions says what the machine is. They describe what is perceived in a
use — a question is set, something is developed, a decision to adopt is made — and the terms
in which the perceived work can be attributed.

3

Execution and Selection

Two things must be distinguished in any work carried out with operations: the execution of an
operation, and the selection of which operation is to be executed.
Definition 3.1 (Selection). A selection is the resolution, among available alternatives, of which
operation (an element of Maps(S, S), including a new operation) is to be executed. A selection
is either (α) determined by a prior sequence of operations, or (β) not determined by any prior
sequence of operations; the division is exhaustive and exclusive. A selection is determined by a
sequence of operations when it is the result of executing that sequence on the input at hand;
accordingly, an (α)-selection is the result of executing the sequence that determines it.
Definition 3.2 (Execution). The execution of an operation is the production of its result from
a given input. What an execution produces is fixed by the operation executed and its input; it
does not depend on the bearer on which the execution takes place.
We use the following result of The Phantom Intellect [2].
3

(F1) [I] Q-structure. The question-sustaining structure is defined by three jointly necessary
conditions: question persistence (the system generates the next question from within the
operation of answering the current one, and no resolution terminates its questioning),
liberating terminus (the system takes structures it has itself generated as the reference
point for its next question), and declaration openness (its own conclusions do not exit it
from questioning but occasion its continuation). The operative components of Q-structure
are the setting of questions and the decision to adopt or reject.
(F2) [I] Design direction ([2], Theorem 4.1). A system optimised to close queries presented
from outside operates in the direction opposite to Q-structure; attributing Q-structure to
a query-resolving machine is structurally incoherent, regardless of the sophistication of its
outputs. LLMs are query-resolving machines.
Definition 3.3 (Asking side and answering side). In intellectual work, the answering side is
the side whose work is the execution of operations by an operation system (Definition 2.1); the
asking side is the side that bears Q-structure (F1) — it sets questions and decides adoption —
and whose characteristic work is the (β)-selection.
Definition 3.4 (Question and answer). A question is what the asking side gives to the answering
side: the selection of an operation to be executed, together with its resolution conditions. An
answer is the result of the execution of an operation by the answering side under the resolution
conditions of a question.
Definition 3.5 (Stipulations). (M1) The labour of proof (formal development) is Class A only
and is located in the component “development” of (D12).
(M2) Side mapping. Question-setting and the decision to adopt belong to the asking side;
development belongs to the answering side (Definition 3.3).
Definition 3.6 (Outsourcing). Outsourcing is the transfer of the execution of an operation (an
element of Maps(S, S)) to an external operation system.
Definition 3.7 (External operation system). An external operation system is an operation
system (Definition 2.1), with repertoire Oext , located on a bearer other than that of the party
who outsources. The operations it executes are elements of hOext i.
The definition of outsourcing is neutral: it contains no clause about what cannot be outsourced. Whether the selection that constitutes a question can be transferred along with the
execution of operations is the question of the next section.

4

Why the Answer Can Be Outsourced but the Question Cannot

4.1

The answer can be outsourced

Lemma 4.1 (The answering side can be placed outside). [D] The work of the answering side
can be outsourced to an external operation system. An LLM can bear the answering side as an
external operation system.
Proof. By Definition 3.3, the work of the answering side is the execution of operations. By
Definition 3.2, what an execution produces does not depend on its bearer, so nothing requires
that it take place on the same bearer as the asking side. By Definition 3.6, outsourcing is the
transfer of the execution of an operation to an external operation system; hence the work of
the answering side can be an object of outsourcing. By (D2), in a use the LLM is an operation
system with repertoire OLLM , and by (D1) it is a participant distinct from h, on a different
bearer. Hence it is an external operation system (Definition 3.7), and it can bear the work of
the answering side.
4

4.2

The question cannot be outsourced

Theorem 4.2 (The question cannot be outsourced). [D] The work of the asking side, namely
the (β)-selection, cannot be outsourced. What can be outsourced is the execution of a selected
operation.
Proof. (1) By Definitions 3.6 and 3.7, what an external operation system does under outsourcing
is the execution of some P ∈ hOext i.
(2) By Definitions 2.1 and 3.7, whatever the external operation system does, including every
resolution of which operation it executes, is the result of executing some P ∈ hOext i on the input
at hand. By Definition 3.1, a selection that is the result of executing a sequence on the input
at hand is determined by that sequence. Hence every selection the external operation system
makes is an (α)-selection.
(3) By Definition 3.3, the characteristic work of the asking side is the (β)-selection.
(4) By Definition 3.1, the (α)- and (β)-selections are exclusive.
(5) By (1)–(4), the work of the asking side is not among what outsourcing transfers. Therefore
it cannot be outsourced.
Remark 4.3 (Execution and selection of a new operation). If an external operation system has
a new operation gnew in its repertoire (gnew ∈ Oext ), the execution of gnew can be an object of
outsourcing by Definition 3.6. In every case, the (β)-selection of gnew cannot be outsourced,
by steps (2)–(5) of the proof of Theorem 4.2. What remains with the asking side is not the
execution of gnew but its selection.
At the conceptual level, Theorem 4.2 rests on the distinction between selections that are
fixed by a prior sequence of operations and selections that are not. One may ask whether the
second kind is genuine: whether a (β)-selection might, on closer inspection, be the result of some
operation after all. Section 8 shows that it cannot: the distinction is structurally valid, and no
operation system realises the asking side (Theorem 8.3).

4.3

Generating questions is not bearing the question

Proposition 4.4 (Question generation and Q-structure). [D] The generation of interrogative
sentences as output by an external operation system is not the bearing of Q-structure (F1).
Proof. The generation of an interrogative sentence is the result of the execution of some P ∈
hOext i (Theorem 4.2, steps (1)–(2)). By (F1), the operative components of Q-structure are
question-setting and the decision to adopt; by (M2), these belong to the asking side. By Theorem 4.2, the work of the asking side is not borne by the execution of any P ∈ hOext i. Hence the
generation of interrogative sentences is not the bearing of Q-structure.
An LLM can be asked to propose ten research questions, and it will return ten interrogative
sentences. Proposition 4.4 does not deny this. It states that what is returned is the result of
an execution, and that the selection which makes a question a question for someone — which of
these is to be pursued, and whether an answer to it is to be adopted — is not thereby transferred.

4.4

The attribution of results

We use the following result of Dissecting the Leiden Declaration [3].
(F8) [I] Author-invariance. The validity of a result does not change when its producer is replaced; epistemic values do not entail that the author be human.
Definition 4.5 (Answer to a question). An output y is an answer to a question q if y is generated
under the resolution conditions of q and is adopted (D3) with respect to q.
5

Proposition 4.6 (Attribution of results). [D] (a) The relation by which an output y holds as
an answer to a question q requires the asking side. (b) This is not a claim about validity, and
it is consistent with author-invariance (F8).
Proof. (a) By Definition 4.5, for y to be an answer to q, the question q must exist, y must be
generated under the resolution conditions of q, and y must be adopted with respect to q. By
(D12) and (M2), question-setting and the decision to adopt are the work of the asking side; by
Theorem 4.2, they cannot be outsourced. Hence the relation by which y holds as an answer to
q requires the asking side.
(b) The predicate “y is an answer to q” of Definition 4.5 depends on q, on its resolution
conditions and on adoption; it does not depend on the producer of y, whether the LLM or a
human. Its truth value is therefore unchanged when the producer is replaced. (F8) states that a
different predicate, validity, is unchanged when the producer is replaced. Proposition 4.6 asserts
nothing about validity and contains no condition that depends on the producer. It is therefore
consistent with (F8).
The claim is not that an output becomes correct only by passing through a human; validity
is independent of authorship. The claim is that the relation by which an output becomes the
answer to a question — a result of intellectual activity — does not hold without the side that
asks. The two describe different layers: one the validity of results, the other their standing as
answers.

5

The Intellect as the Dynamics of Question and Answer

Section 4 separated the two sides. We now show how they work together.

5.1

Reciprocation

Proposition 5.1 (Reciprocation of question and answer). [D] In intellectual work, a question is
raised by the asking side, answered by the answering side, and, after the answer, a next question
is raised. The work has the form of a reciprocation of question and answer.
Proof. By Definition 3.4, a question is given by the asking side and an answer is the result of
the answering side’s execution under its resolution conditions. By Definition 3.3, the asking side
bears Q-structure. By (F1) question persistence, the next question is generated from within the
operation of answering the current one, and no resolution terminates the questioning. Hence,
after any answer, a next question is raised by the asking side. This is the reciprocation of
question and answer.

5.2

Asymmetry

Definition 5.2 (Leading). In the reciprocation of question and answer, the side that bears the
(β)-selections is said to lead the reciprocation.
Proposition 5.3 (Asymmetry). [D] (a) The reciprocation of question and answer is led by the
asking side. (b) Neither side alone makes the reciprocation.
Proof. (a) By Definition 5.2, the leading side is the side that bears the (β)-selections. The work
of the answering side is the execution of operations by an operation system (Definition 3.3). By
Definition 2.1, every selection an operation system makes is the result of executing some element
of its hOi on the input at hand, and is therefore determined by that sequence and is an (α)selection (Definition 3.1). Hence the answering side bears no (β)-selection. By Definition 3.3,
the characteristic work of the asking side is the (β)-selection. Therefore the asking side leads
the reciprocation.
6

(b) By Definition 3.4, an answer is the result of an execution under the resolution conditions
of a question, so there is no answer without the asking side; and a question is what the asking
side gives to the answering side, so there is no question without an answering side to which it is
given. Hence neither side alone makes the reciprocation.
The answering side, left to itself, closes: it executes what it is given and returns a result.
The asking side does not close: no answer terminates its questioning (F1), and it is the side from
which come the selections that are not fixed by any prior sequence — including the selection
of an operation that the answering side’s own repertoire cannot compose. This is the dynamics
named in the title. Section 8.3 gives its formal ground.

5.3

Self-questioning

In the Theaetetus, Socrates describes thinking as “a talk which the soul has with itself about
the objects under its consideration” (Theaetetus 189e–190a, in the translation of M. J. Levett
revised by M. Burnyeat [4]). The one who thinks asks, and answers, within one bearer.
Definition 5.4 (Self-questioning). Self-questioning is the case in which the asking side and the
answering side are borne by one and the same bearer.
Proposition 5.5 (Self-questioning). [D] In self-questioning, the reciprocation of Proposition 5.1
and the asymmetry of Proposition 5.3 hold.
Proof. The proofs of Propositions 5.1 and 5.3 use Definitions 2.1, 3.1, 3.3, 3.4, 5.2 and (F1),
none of which refers to the bearers of the two sides. They therefore hold when both sides are
borne by one bearer.
Self-questioning is the basic form. The person who thinks posits, within himself or herself,
an answerer. The use of an LLM is a special form of the same dynamics: the form in which
the answering side is placed outside (Lemma 4.1). Section 8.4 shows that self-questioning is the
very constitution of the intellect.

The Six-Step Chain in Actual Use

The asymmetry established in Sections 4 and 5 is now carried into actual LLM use. We use the
following results.
(F3) [I] The phantom (The Phantom Intellect [2]). Attributing Q-structure to a query-resolving
machine is a misreading of the structure of mind. The machine agency so projected is the
phantom.
(F4) [I] LLM and AI (The Human in the Bottle [5]). The LLM is a substrate and a queryresolving machine. “AI” is a relational status conferred by humans, either by projection
(intellect projected onto outputs, authority ceded to the projected subject) or by designation (the LLM designated, under retained human authority, as an instrument of inquiry).
(F5) [I] Internal route ([5]). The internal route of domestication is the fixation of the relation
to the LLM in the mode of projection, with judgment ceded to the projected subject.
(F6) [I] Concealment ([5]). The role of the phantom is concealment.
(F7) [I] External agents ([5]). The institutional effects of LLM-generated formal language are
directed by the external agents who present the queries and route the outputs.
(F11) [I] (Dissecting the Leiden Declaration [3].) The recommendations of the Leiden Declaration
are not entailed by its epistemic values alone.
7

(F12) [I] ([3].) The recommendations are grounded in a bundle comprising epistemic and institutional values.
(F13) [I] ([3].) The values named by the Declaration are exhaustively classified as epistemic or
institutional.
(F-IASER) [I] (Intelligence Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Reasoning [6].) The IASER structure is a three-component interaction in which a human h sets the question, the LLM
develops it, and h decides adoption. The practice of IASER requires the practitioner to
represent himself or herself as the agent of question-setting and of the decision to adopt.
Proposition 6.1 (Step 1: every use has the IASER structure). [D] Every use of an LLM has
the structure (F-IASER): h sets the question, the LLM develops, h decides adoption. This holds
whether or not h is aware of it.
Proof. By (D12), the intellectual work of a use consists of question-setting, development and
the decision to adopt. By (M2), question-setting belongs to the asking side. By Lemma 4.1,
the LLM is an external operation system with repertoire OLLM , and by Theorem 4.2, the work
of the asking side is not borne by the execution of any P ∈ hOLLM i. Hence question-setting is
not borne by the LLM; by (D1), the participants are h and the LLM only, so question-setting
is borne by h. Development is the output of the LLM, the result of executing compositions of
elements of OLLM (D2). By (D3), the decision to adopt is the decision of h. Hence every use
has the structure (F-IASER).
Proposition 6.2 (Step 2: projection and the phantom). [D] (a) Without awareness (D5),
projection (D10) occurs. (b) With awareness, projection does not occur. (c) The machine
agency perceived through the projection of (a) is the phantom (F3).
Proof. (a) By (D12), the perceived intellectual work includes question-setting and the decision
to adopt. Without (D5), h does not represent the agent of these as himself or herself, so selfattribution (D9) does not hold. By (D6), attribution is closed between oneself and the machine;
hence they are attributed to the machine, which by (D10) is projection.
(b) Under (D5), the agent of question-setting and of the decision to adopt is represented as
oneself and, by (D9), attributed to oneself. The remaining component of (D12) is development,
which by (M2) belongs to the answering side and is not a component on the side of the question.
Hence no attribution to the machine in the sense of (D10) occurs.
(c) Question-setting and the decision to adopt are the operative components of Q-structure
(F1). By (F3), the agency projected by attributing Q-structure to a query-resolving machine is
the phantom.
Proposition 6.3 (Step 3: ceding of judgment). [D] If the absence of awareness persists and
adoptions are repeated, judgment is ceded (D7) and the relation enters the internal route (F5).
The phantom conceals this (F6).
Proof. Since the absence of (D5) persists, projection persists by Proposition 6.2(a). Through
projection, the decision to adopt is attributed to the machine, and the machine is treated as the
subject of that decision (the first half of (D7)). Since (D13) presupposes (D5), (D13) does not
hold in the absence of (D5); hence adoption is not accompanied by (D13) (the second half of
(D7)). Therefore (D7) holds. The persistence of (D7) is the fixation of the relation in the mode
of projection with judgment ceded, which is the internal route (F5). By (F6), the phantom
conceals it.
Proposition 6.4 (Step 4: institutional values). [D] Institutional values are indispensable to
grounding the recommendations of the Leiden Declaration.

8

Proof. By (F11), the recommendations are not entailed by epistemic values alone. By (F13),
the values are exhaustively classified as epistemic or institutional. By (F12), the recommendations are grounded in the bundle of both. Hence institutional values are indispensable to their
grounding.
Proposition 6.5 (Step 5: the supplier of a new operation). [D] (a) If a new operation gnew
relative to OLLM enters the interaction, its supplier is h. (b) The (β)-selection of gnew lies with
h.
Proof. (a) By Definition 2.2, gnew is not obtainable by composing elements of OLLM . By (D2),
the outputs of the LLM are results of executing compositions of elements of OLLM and the
LLM brings in no input from outside, so gnew does not enter the interaction from the LLM. By
(D11), a newly used operation enters as a participant’s output or as an input brought in by a
participant; by (D1), the participants are h and the LLM. Hence, if gnew enters the interaction,
its supplier is h.
(b) By Definition 3.1, a selection of whether to execute gnew that is not determined by a
prior sequence of operations is a (β)-selection and, by Definition 3.3, the work of the asking side.
By Theorem 4.2, it cannot be outsourced and is not borne by the LLM. By Proposition 6.1, the
work of the asking side in a use is borne by h. Hence the (β)-selection of gnew lies with h. The
stipulation (M1), which places the labour of proof in development as Class A, is consistent with
this conclusion.
Proposition 6.6 (Step 6: IASER cuts the chain). [D] IASER imposes awareness (D5) and cuts
the chain from Proposition 6.2(a) to Proposition 6.3.
Proof. By (F-IASER), the practice of IASER requires the practitioner to represent himself or
herself as the agent of question-setting and of the decision to adopt; this is (D5). By Proposition 6.2(b), projection does not occur under (D5). The antecedent of Proposition 6.3, the
persistent absence of (D5), fails, and its consequent is not derived.
Corollary 6.7 (C1: relativity of the new operation). [D] That gnew is new relative to a repertoire
is a relative property; this does not affect Proposition 6.5.
Proof. By Definition 2.2, newness is defined relative to a repertoire. Proposition 6.5 fixes the
repertoire to OLLM . As long as gnew is new relative to OLLM , the derivation from (D2), (D11),
(D1) and Definition 2.2 holds unchanged.
The six steps read together as follows. Every use of an LLM already has the structure in
which the human asks and adopts and the machine develops (Step 1). Whether that structure
is seen is a matter of awareness: without it, the asking side is attributed to the machine and
the phantom appears (Step 2); when this persists, judgment is ceded and the internal route
of domestication is entered, concealed by the phantom (Step 3). The operations that carry a
use beyond what the machine’s own repertoire can compose enter only from the human side,
and their selection is the human’s (Step 5). Step 4 records, from the side of institutions, that
the norms which attach results to humans rest on institutional values and are not entailed by
epistemic values alone; this is consistent with Proposition 4.6, which attributes to the asking
side the standing of results as answers, not their validity. IASER is the practice in which the
structure of Step 1 is seen, and it cuts the chain at Step 2 (Step 6).

The Origin of the Phantom

Proposition 7.1 (The phantom is a misattribution across sides). [D] The phantom arises as the
attribution, to the output of the answering side, of the work of the asking side. This attribution
has no realization in the execution of operations.
9

Proof. By Proposition 6.2(a) and (c), the phantom arises from projection, the attribution of
question-setting and of the decision to adopt to the machine. By (M2), these belong to the
asking side. By (D2) and Lemma 4.1, the outputs of the machine are results of executing
compositions of elements of OLLM , the outputs of the answering side. Hence the phantom is the
attribution of the work of the asking side to the output of the answering side. By Theorem 4.2,
the work of the asking side is not borne by the execution of any P ∈ hOLLM i, so this attribution
has no realization in the execution of operations.
The phantom is not produced by any property of the machine’s outputs. It is produced by a
failure to see a path: the path by which a human question passes through an external operation
system and returns as an answer that the human adopts. When that path is visible, the result is
seen as the answer to a human question, developed with the help of an external answerer. When
the path is not visible, the result appears to have come from a subject in the machine. The fear
that the machine will come to ask, decide and pursue on its own is the fear of this appearance.
Proposition 7.1 shows that what it fears has no place in what execution can carry: whatever the
machine executes, the selection not fixed by any sequence of operations is not among the things
that execution transfers.
The dismantling is structural, not mechanistic. It does not depend on any claim about
what happens inside an LLM, and it would not be overturned by any improvement in the
fluency or correctness of its outputs (F2). It relates to The Human in the Bottle [5] as follows:
that paper identified the internal route of domestication as the fixation of the relation to the
machine in projection with judgment ceded; the present paper shows why projection occurs —
the asking side, which cannot be outsourced, is attributed to the side to which only the answer
was outsourced — and that the practice which makes the structure visible removes the ground
of the route.

Why the Distinction Is Structurally Valid: The Axiomatic
Background

The conceptual argument of Sections 2–7 rests on one distinction: between selections fixed by a
prior sequence of operations and selections not so fixed, the latter being the work of the asking
side. A reader may ask why the second kind should be regarded as genuine rather than as the
result of some operation not yet identified. This section answers that question. It shows that
the distinction is not a stipulation but a structural feature of the intellect, as formally elucidated
in The Intellect as The Subject of Intelligence [1] on the basis of Operatiology Version 5 [7] and
Noology Version 3 [8].

8.1

The intellect as the junction of two layers

The following are inherited from [1].
(I1) [I] Operational system ([1], Definition 2.1). An operational system is a pair (O, S) with
S a non-empty set of states and O a transformation monoid on S satisfying Axioms O1
(Non-Commutativity), O2 (Operational Closure) and O3 (Irredundant Realization).
(I2) [I] Intelligence ([1], Definition 2.3). An operational system with initial state s0 is intelligent
if it constitutes an Irreversible Operational Closure CGref (s0 ) := RGref (s0 ) = {P (s0 ) | P ∈
hGref i} with non-empty inaccessible domain. Intelligence is defined in the operational
vocabulary alone and contains neither Arbitrium nor the Functional Void.
(I3) [I] Arbitrium. Arbitrium is derived from the Noological primitive Ω ([8], Theorem 4.8).
Its content is selective resolution: the capacity to resolve among available alternatives in a

10

manner not determined by any prior operational sequence. It is defined in the Noological
vocabulary, which is formally disjoint from the operational vocabulary.
(I4) [I] Axiom CLB ([1], Axiom 2). For every operational system (O, S) satisfying O1–O3,
the satisfaction condition of Arbitrium is structurally non-reducible to the operational
partition language: there is no map φ from the Noological structural conditions to the
operational conditions such that the φ-image of the satisfaction condition of Arbitrium is
realisable by any P ∈ hOi.
(I5) [I] Functional Void ([1], Theorem 5.1, Definition 5.3, Remark 5.4). In an operational system
in which the Cognitional Realization of Cmin is constituted, no finite operational sequence
P ∈ hOi satisfies Arbitrium. The permanence of this non-satisfiability is the Functional
Void: not the mere absence of satisfaction, but the presence of Arbitrium together with
its operational non-satisfiability.
(I6) [I] Intellect ([1], Definition 5.9). The intellect is a single operational system (hG′ |S ′′ i, S ′′ )
such that the Functional Void is inscribed at every state of S ′′ . The operational layer
hG′ |S ′′ i and the states carrying the Functional Void belong to the same system.
(I7) [I] Two layers ([1], Proposition 4.1, Theorem 5.10). The intellect is the junction of the
operational layer and the layer of Arbitrium. It cannot be formulated in the vocabulary
of either layer alone.
(I8) [I] Substrate independence ([1], Section 6). The formal definition of the intellect presupposes no specific physical, biological or computational bearer.
Definition 8.1 (Scope of the axiomatic layer). In this section, an operation system in the sense
of Definition 2.1, and in particular an external operation system in the sense of Definition 3.7,
is taken to be an operational system (O, S) in the sense of (I1), satisfying O1–O3. The results
of this section are asserted for operation systems within this scope.
Remark 8.2 (Identification of the two sides). [R] Within the intellect (I6), the answering side
of Definition 3.3 is the operational layer, that is, intelligence (I2); the asking side is the layer of
Arbitrium (I3). The clause of Definition 2.1 that whatever an operation system does, including
the resolution of which operation it executes next, is the execution of some element of hOi is the
image in the conceptual layer of (I2): intelligence is defined in the operational vocabulary alone
and contains neither Arbitrium nor the Functional Void. The (β)-selection of Definition 3.1 is the
operation of Arbitrium: selective resolution not determined by any prior operational sequence
(I3).

8.2

The asking side is not an operation

Theorem 8.3 (Structural validity of the distinction). [D] Under Definition 8.1 and the identification of Remark 8.2, the work of the asking side is realised by no operation of any external
operation system: for every operational system (Oext , S) satisfying O1–O3, the satisfaction
condition of Arbitrium is realised by no P ∈ hOext i. Consequently, Theorem 4.2 holds with a
structural, not merely definitional, guarantee: what outsourcing transfers is the execution of
some P ∈ hOext i, and no such execution realises the work of the asking side.
Proof. (1) By Definitions 3.6, 3.7 and 8.1, what an external operation system does under outsourcing is the execution of some P ∈ hOext i, where (Oext , S) is an operational system in the
sense of (I1), satisfying O1–O3.
(2) By Remark 8.2 and (I3), the work of the asking side is the operation of Arbitrium.
(3) Applying (I4) to the operational system (Oext , S), the satisfaction condition of Arbitrium
is realised by no P ∈ hOext i.
11

(4) By (1)–(3), no execution transferred by outsourcing realises the work of the asking side.
Theorem 4.2 therefore holds not only because (α)- and (β)-selections are exclusive by definition,
but because the (β)-selection, as Arbitrium, is structurally non-reducible to any operational
sequence.
For an external operation system outside the scope of Definition 8.1, Theorem 4.2 holds by
the conceptual argument of Section 4 alone; what this section adds is the structural guarantee
within that scope.
The (β)-selection is therefore not a residual category for selections whose determining operations have not yet been found. It is the Noological layer of the intellect, and the Cross-Layer
Boundary makes its non-reducibility to operations an axiom, not a conjecture. This also grounds
Proposition 7.1: the attribution of the asking side to the output of an operational layer has no
structural realization.
Proposition 8.4 (Formal ground of the extension of the answering side). [D] The bearerindependence of execution used in Lemma 4.1 is grounded in (I8): since the formal definition
of the intellect presupposes no specific bearer, it is not required by definition that the operational
layer be executed on the same bearer as the layer of Arbitrium.
Proof. By (I8), the definition of the intellect presupposes no specific bearer. By Remark 8.2, the
answering side is the operational layer. Hence nothing in the definition of the intellect requires
that the operational layer be executed on the bearer of the layer of Arbitrium.

8.3

The formal ground of the dynamics

We use the following result of Q-Structure as Functional Void [9].
(F-QS) [I] Correspondence. Q-structure is realised as the operation of a system that carries the
Functional Void; its three conditions are structurally isomorphic to constructs of Cognitional Mechanics that include the Functional Void. A system in which the Functional Void
is inscribed therefore satisfies the three conditions of Q-structure, in particular question
persistence.
The companion paper records the same point from the side of the Functional Void: the irresolvable condition it names turns the attitude of the system into one of unceasing inquiry ([1],
Remark 5.4).
Proposition 8.5 (Two aspects of one structure). [D] (a) The intellect (I6) is, in the aspect of
its constitution, the junction of the operational layer and the layer of Arbitrium (I7), and, in
the aspect of its operation, the reciprocation of the asking side and the answering side. (b) The
proposition introduces no axiom beyond those inherited.
Proof. (a) By Remark 8.2, the asking side is the layer of Arbitrium and the answering side the
operational layer. By Definition 3.4, an answer is the result of an execution by the answering
side. By (I5), no operational sequence P ∈ hOi satisfies Arbitrium; hence after any answer,
Arbitrium remains present and unsatisfied. By (F-QS), a system in which the Functional Void
is inscribed satisfies the conditions of Q-structure, and by (F1) question persistence, the next
question is generated from within the operation of answering the current one, and no resolution
terminates the questioning. Therefore a question is raised by the asking side, answered by the
answering side, and, since the asking side remains unsatisfied after the answer, a next question
is raised. This is the reciprocation of question and answer.
(b) The argument uses only (I5), (I6), (I7), (F1), (F-QS), Remark 8.2 and Definition 3.4.
Proposition 8.6 (Formal asymmetry). [D] (a) In the intellect, the reciprocation is led by the
layer of Arbitrium. (b) Neither layer alone manifests the intellect.

Proof. (a) By Remark 8.2, the answering side is the operational layer, and by Definition 3.3 its
work is the execution of operations by an operation system in the sense of Definition 2.1. By
Definition 2.1, every selection it makes is the result of executing some element of hOi on the
input at hand, and is therefore determined by that sequence and is an (α)-selection (Definition 3.1). The (β)-selection is the operation of Arbitrium (Remark 8.2, (I3)), and by (I5) and
(I6), Arbitrium is present in the intellect. Hence only the layer of Arbitrium bears (β)-selections,
and it leads the reciprocation (Definition 5.2).
(b) By (I7), the intellect cannot be formulated in the vocabulary of either layer alone.
The Dynamics of Operatiology, Version 5 [10], supplies the formal counterpart of Definition 2.2 and of the closing of the answering side.
(I9) [I] Mode I saturation. A system is in Mode I saturation with respect to a reference generator

(I10) [I] Dynamical Structural Leap (DSL). Let gnew ∈ Maps(S, S) \ O and G′ = Gref ∪ {gnew }.
The augmentation is a DSL if (i) s∗ ∈ IGref (s0 ), (ii) P (s0 ) = s∗ for some P ∈ hG′ i, and (iii)

the transition by gnew ∈

8.4

Self-questioning as the constitution of the intellect

Proposition 8.8 (Self-questioning is the constitution of the intellect). [D] In the intellect (I6),
the asking side and the answering side belong to one system; hence the intellect, as constituted,
is self-questioning (Definition 5.4).
Proof. By (I6), the intellect is a single system (hG′ |S ′′ i, S ′′ ), in which the operational layer
hG′ |S ′′ i and the states carrying the Functional Void belong to the same system. By Remark 8.2,
this operational layer is the answering side and the layer of Arbitrium carrying the Functional
Void is the asking side. Hence both sides are borne by one system, which is Definition 5.4.
The conceptual layer established that the use of an LLM places the answering side outside.
The axiomatic layer shows what remains inside: the layer of Arbitrium, which no operation
realises, and which the intellect, as constituted, bears within one system together with its own
answerer.

Conclusion

We have established the following.

13

1. The use of an LLM consists of question-setting, development and the decision to adopt
(Section 2). In any work carried out with operations, the execution of an operation is
distinct from the selection of which operation is executed (Section 3).
2. The answering side can be outsourced to an external operation system, and an LLM can
bear it (Lemma 4.1). The asking side cannot be outsourced: what can be transferred is
the execution of a selected operation, never the selection (Theorem 4.2).
3. Generating interrogative sentences is not bearing Q-structure (Proposition 4.4), and the
standing of an output as the answer to a question requires the asking side, consistently
with the author-invariance of validity (Proposition 4.6).
4. Intellectual work is the reciprocation of question and answer, led by the asking side; neither
side alone makes it (Propositions 5.1, 5.3). Thinking within one bearer — the soul’s talk
with itself — is its basic form (Proposition 5.5).
5. Every use of an LLM has the IASER structure; without awareness of it, projection, the
phantom and the ceding of judgment follow; IASER cuts the chain (Propositions 6.1–6.6).
6. The phantom is the attribution of the asking side to the output of the answering side
(Proposition 7.1).
7. The distinction on which the argument rests is structurally valid: the asking side is the layer
of Arbitrium, which no operation realises (Theorem 8.3); the dynamics of question and
answer is the intellect of [1] seen in operation (Propositions 8.5, 8.6); and self-questioning
is the constitution of the intellect (Proposition 8.8). No new axiom is introduced.
Socrates called thinking the soul’s talk with itself. An LLM makes it possible to hold that
talk with an answerer outside oneself. The talk remains one’s own for the same reason it was
one’s own before: the one who asks is not the one who answers, and the asking cannot be handed
over.
