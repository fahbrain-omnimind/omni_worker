What is (an Organic) Analogy?
Borromean Complementarities: Homology & Archetype,
Chance & (De)Convexiﬁcation, and Analogy & Prototype

Julio Michael Stern

Abstract. This article investigates “What is analogy?” from the perspective of the Socratic-Platonic question – τ ι στ ι, ti esti, “what is this?”
With this goal in mind, we develop the Borromean Complementarities
metaphor, represented by three interlinked threads, each of these made
of intertwined strands of conﬂating concepts, for example, Homology &
Archetype, Analogy & Prototype, and Chance & (De)Convexiﬁcation.
The context of application of these concepts includes biological theories
of evolution, the means and methods used for studying divergence and
convergence processes leading to “life forms most beautiful”, and their
organization in a tree of life. Moreover, we suggest innovative deﬁnitions
for the concepts of Analogy and Prototype, and motivate their use for
acquiring pertinent quantitative information leading to statistical methods of analysis that in turn can be helpful for reaching clear conclusions
while avoiding logical traps and pitfalls. Furthermore, we explain how the
concepts of Analogy and Prototype can support functional or teleological interpretations. Finally, we consider the philosophical consequences
of these discussions concerning the use and meaning of language, and
indicate some topics for further research.
Mathematics Subject Classiﬁcation. 03A10, 62A01.
Keywords. Complementarity, Probability & inference, Discrete & convex
sets, Homology, Archetype & structure, Analogy, Prototype & function,

1. Borromean Complementarities: Homology & Archetype,
Chance & (De)Convexiﬁcation, and Analogy & Prototype
This article investigates the question “What is analogy?”, in the SocraticPlatonic tradition of asking – τ ι στ ι, ti esti, or “what is this?”, see [14] and
[135, p.4-5]. The context of our investigation concerns scientiﬁc theories of
stochastic evolution. These theories were ﬁrst developed in Biology, but their
range of application has expanded to areas like linguistics, ethology, psychology, sociology, technology, and the evolution of natural and artiﬁcial systems
of several sorts.
Analogy is a similarity relation based on functional features, and it is
concerned with similarity of purpose. Analogy explains how a system, or a
component thereof, is used for a given end or is able to achieve a given goal.
Moreover, analogy can be useful in comparative studies showing how the evolutionary history of distinct systems converges to the same “functional solution”,
a.k.a. Prototype. In the context of scientiﬁc theories of stochastic evolution,
analogy is contrasted (or confused) with homology. Homology is a similarity
relation based on structural features, and it is concerned with the organization
form of the constituent components of a system, or the interconnection pattern of its building blocks. Moreover, homology can be useful in comparative
studies showing how the evolutionary history of distinct systems diverges from
the same original or “ancestral form”, a.k.a. Archetype.
The following two quotations, by Charles Darwin (1809-1882) and Richard
Owen (1804-1892), point to several perceived paradoxes in stochastic evolution
and the purposeful adaptation of living systems.
First, the paradoxical role played by probability in its theoretical conception. On the one hand, randomness is seen as a source of noise or error; on the
other hand, stochastic processes drive the evolution of well-adapted solutions.
On the one hand, probability describes naturally unpredictable future events;

1.1. Probability as a Discretization or Convexiﬁcation Operator
A distinctive and revolutionary aspect of Darwin’s theory concerns the stochastic nature of biological evolution processes. Accordingly, this subsection
considers some aspects of probabilistic systems that will be useful in following
discussions.
Type conversion and type casting are operations that transform an argument or expression from one type to another, often avoiding a type mismatch
fallacy. For example, in computer programming, the round, ﬂoor or ceil operators are standard alternatives used when “assigning” a ﬂoating-point value to
a variable of integer type. In general, such conversions are not arbitrary operations, for they correspond to real changes in the nature of the objects being
represented, or to reinterpretations of the variables representing the properties of these objects. Random events, Stochastic processes, and other situations
ruled by Probability theory are ubiquitous in modern scientiﬁc models. In some
of these situations, a system described by continuous variables generates a discrete random variable, or a system described by discrete variables generates a
continuous random variable, as discussed in the following examples:
(A) First, let us consider a prismatic spinning top, see Fig. 1.lb; This is
a random number generator or randomization device used for gambling. The
top’s rotation movement is characterized by continuous symmetries, namely,
the radial symmetries of its inertia ellipsoid, see [111, ch.13] and [149]. Once

(B) Next, let us consider a classic example in Game Theory.
In the Odds & Evens game (OEg), a.k.a. Matching-Pennies game, each
of the players, Odd and Even, has to act by showing, simultaneously, a single bit, 0 or 1. If both bits agree, Odd wins, otherwise, Even wins. Hence,
the set of possible actions for Odd and Even is given by the Discrete set
D = {(0, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (1, 1)}. This is also the set of Pure or Deterministic
Strategies in this game, that is, the ways players can choose how to act. Set
D can be represented geometrically as the corners of the unit square in IR2 .
Mixed or Randomized Strategies expand the ways players can choose how to
act by allowing each player to choose probabilities of possible action. In the
OEg, each player deﬁnes his strategy by a (continuous) parameter in the unit
interval, λ ∈ [0, 1], and then selects action 1 with probability λ, or action 0
with probability (1 − λ). This (involuntary and independent) random selection
can be accomplished by using a randomization device, like ﬂipping pennies,
hence the alternative name of this game. Mixed strategies for Odd and Even
can be represented by a point in the unit square, [λO , λE ] ∈ C = [0, 1]2 ⊆ IR2 .
Notice that taking parameters at the extreme points of the continuous set C,
i.e. [λO , λE ] ∈ D = {0, 1}2 recovers the deterministic strategies.
An equilibrium-point of a game, a.k.a. ﬁxed-point, invariant-solution or
eigen-solution, is a set of strategies that leaves each player at a local optimum, that is, each player, having full knowledge of the other player’s strategy,
can gain nothing by unilaterally changing his own. The OEg allowing only
Deterministic strategies does not have any equilibrium point. In this situation, the OEg poses a dilemma - διλημμα - a problem oﬀering two possibilities, none of which is acceptable. Caught in this logical ambush, the players
would be trapped, forever ﬂip-ﬂopping between the discrete options available.
The OEg allowing Mixed strategies has a unique equilibrium point, namely,
[λO , λE ] = [0.5, 0.5]. Expanding the discrete space of deterministic strategies to
the continuous space of mixed or randomized strategies allows the emergence
of an equilibrium-point, hence oﬀering a (eigen) solution to the unsolvable
dilemma posed by the discrete case.
Geometrically, the operation of taking the Convex Hull transforms the
aforementioned discrete space into the continuous space of strategies, while the
operation of taking the Extreme Points goes the other way around. A Convex
Combination of two points in an Euclidean space, x and y, can be interpreted
either as a point z in the line segment joining them, or as a mixture or weighted
average of these two points, namely, z = (1−λ)x+λy , λ ∈ [0, 1]. A Convex Set
contains any convex combinations of its points. The Convex Hull of a set is the
smallest convex set containing it. The Extreme Points of a set are those that
cannot be obtained as convex combinations of other points in the set. Convexity is the essential property that warrants the emergence of eigen-solutions or

(C) Finally, let us consider Francis Galton’s Quincunx machine, see
Fig. 1.rb. This machine uses a symmetrical arrangement of a large number
of binary random events in order to generate (an approximation characterized
by asymptotic convergence to) a continuous Gaussian (a.k.a. Normal) distribution. The Gaussian has some remarkable properties characterizing it as a
stable-distribution; a.k.a. eigen- , equilibrium- or invariant- / -form , -function
or -solution in the space of probability distributions. First, this distribution
has a functional form that is invariant by a speciﬁc coupling operation, in this
case, a linear combination. That is, if X and Y are Gaussian random variables,
then Z = αX + βY is also a Gaussian random variable. Second, the same coupling operation can be used to aggregate a large number of random variables
into a random variable that converges (in distribution) to this stable form. In
this case, under appropriate regularity conditions, the (weighted sum) average
of a large number of independent and identically distributed random variables
converges to a Gaussian random variable. This cornerstone result of statistical
theory is known as the Central Limit Theorem. Similar properties characterize
the Bernoulli, Cauchy, Exponential, Gamma, Gumbel, Pareto, Poisson, and
Weibull distributions; distributions derived therefrom; and others of the most
often used in Statistics. The other way around, one can argue that the characteristic properties of an eigen-solution explain why these distributions are so
important to statistical theory, and also why they are so well-adapted to the
practice of statistical modeling, that is, why these solutions keep “showing-up”
in real applications; see [155, sec.5.5] and [55,98,130,156,188]. For an alternative characterization of (the functional form of) probability distributions,
based on their (discrete or continuous) symmetry and invariance properties,
see [156] and references within.

of complex systems. These examples highlight the rich interplay, but also the
distinct nature and the diﬀerent roles played by discrete and continuous (random) variables and, consequently, the need to avoid type mismatch fallacies.

2. Chance Coincidence, Random Rami, Probabilistic Pruning
In this section, we examine three pairs of complementary concepts involving
probability or improbability arguments, and the roles they play in the context
of scientiﬁc theories of biological evolution; they are:
• “By Chance” explanations & Improbable coincidences in Biology;
• Fortuitous variations (FV) & Likely survival (LS) of well-ﬁtted individuals;
• Random ramiﬁcation (RR) & Probabilistic pruning (PP) in the tree of
life.
The historical development of these concepts covers three millennia of human
history, going from Aristotle (384-322 BC), to Charles Darwin (1809-1882)
and Alfred Wallace’s (1823-1913) classical theory of biological evolution, and
from there to contemporary or extended versions of the theory of evolution
used in biology, ethology, linguistics, computer science, etc. In this and the
following sections we will present some key ideas in these developmental paths,
organizing and focusing the presentation according to the speciﬁc objectives
of this article.

2.2. Fortuitous Variations & Likely Survival of Well-Fitted Individuals;
and Random Ramiﬁcation & Probabilistic Pruning in the Tree of Life
In this subsection, we examine two distinct and complementary roles played
by probability in modern theories of biological evolution, roles that have been
clearly distinguished by Curtis Johnson in his book Darwin’s Dice [83], where
he identiﬁes these two dice by the names: (FV) Fortuitous Variation, and
(LS) Likely Survival. Moreover, we contrast the complementary roles played
by these dice at microscopic vs. macroscopic scales, concerning the survival
of single individuals vs. the survival of collective species. The speciﬁc roles
of these two dice are expressed in Charles Darwin’s letter to Charles Lyell of
August 1, 1861, apud [83], whose core paragraph is the ﬁrst opening quotation
of this paper.
In biology, sex links single individuals and their species by providing a
mechanism for coding, preserving, transmitting, sharing, and mixing information concerning their characteristic capabilities and ways of life. For simpler life

forms, horizontal sexual exchange allows the communication of single genes–
units of information coding relevant characteristics. For complex life forms,
sexual reproduction demands vertical genetic recombination at each reproductive cycle. Furthermore, at least in sexual reproduction, organism similarity at
the level of biological species strongly limits the population in which genetic recombination is possible. Additional constraints, like bounded environments or

diﬀerentiate. See [81], and references therein, for relevant reinterpretations of
these concepts in the context of biological and computational semantics, and
related developments in genetic programming algorithms.
A Tree of Life is, from a logical perspective, a directed acyclic graph
mapping the distinctive branching process resulting from biological speciation,
see Fig. 2. The branching points in the tree represent two or more species
diverging, in their phylogenetic path of evolution, from a single ancestor, or
better said, from a single ancestral species. Pruning points in the tree, or
(past) branch termination points, represent the extinction of a species, an
event that may be caused by gradual or sudden changes in the environment,
new predation or competition from better adapted species, or other adversities.
Since, as explained in the previous paragraph, the phylogenetic history of a
species is interlinked with the ontogenetic development and reproduction of
its individuals, we mimic the aforementioned nomenclature of Darwin’s Dice
for the stochastic branching process forming the Tree of Life using the names:
(RR) Random Ramiﬁcation and (PP) Probabilistic Pruning.
Finally, it is interesting to contrast the historical role of mathematical
probability and statistics in the aforementioned theories. On the one hand,
quantitative probabilistic reasoning had an important role in Statistical Mechanics since its onset, see [54,147]. On the other hand, the methods of mathematical analysis were incorporated in the Theory of Evolution at a much later
time. One contributing factor for this delay is the equally late discovery of
the mechanisms of genetic coding by Watson and Crick in 1953, [183]. Gregor
Mendel (1822–1884) did extensive experiments with controlled hybridization
of pea plants and developed a probability model for the underlying stochastic
process, see [112]. Unfortunately, his work was almost forgotten until rediscovered by Hugo de Vries in the early 20th century; see [162, sec.4.2], [38,62], for
historical comments. Furthermore, DNA sequencing techniques only became
available in the 1970 s and, therefore, statistical models for random mutations,
background noise timing along tree branches, measures of genetic proximity,
etc. were only developed even later in time. Nevertheless, biologists have been
using much older probability arguments for reconstructing the tree of life, and
this is the topic of the next section.

3.1. Richard Owen, Homology, and Archetype
Fossil records, together with relative spatial and temporal arrangements provided by geography and geology, were of fundamental importance in the construction and analysis of the ﬁrst trees of life in the context of evolution theory.
However, in this task, biologists needed additional help for further organizing
and positioning these records in a tree diagram that was itself simultaneously
under construction. This help came in the form of rational and systematic
methods for establishing and evaluating similarity relations between fossils, as
studied in this subsection.
The (internal) skeleton has the hardest parts (bones) in the body of vertebrates, and these get fossilized, i.e. preserved by being incorporated into rock,
much more easily than softer parts, like muscle or internal organs. Similarly,
the exoskeleton has the hardest parts in the body of arthropods, and these are
also more easily and abundantly preserved in fossil records. Figure 4.tc shows
bones in forelimbs of some mammals and vein ﬁelds in wings of some insects,
like they (usually incompletely) appear in fossils. Animals within these groups

4.2. Convergent Evolution to a Functional Solution
Vertebrate and cephalopods are far away from each other in the Tree of Life, see
Fig. 3, situated on branches separated since a distant past when their ancestral
forms had extremely simple eyes (if any). Moreover, arguments in this section
demonstrate that the vertebrates’ and cephalopods’ well-developed eyes at
present times (as well as their constituent components) are based on diﬀerent
structural archetypes. Hence, these eyes’ respective designs evolved independently, in the sense that there was no direct exchange of information concerning
their design along their separate phylogenetic histories. Notwithstanding their
separate evolutionary histories, based on diﬀerent structural archetypes, these
eyes have converged to the same functional prototype (or similar variations
thereof), that is, they are analogous to (i.e., work like) a man-made machine,
namely, a photographic camera equipped with a single lens that is shaped or
positioned in order to achieve optimal focus conditions.
This situation raises the question: Why did the aforementioned eyes converge to the same functional prototype? A plausible answer is: Because the
laws of optics are invariant and universal, optical equipment must work in
accordance with these same laws, no matter when or where it is, as suggested
by the next quotation from R. Owen. Finally, it should be easy to understand

6. Further Research
The following two subsections examine several paradoxes derived from misconceptions about the nature of evolution. These misconceptions, often compounded with logical fallacies, engender all sorts of erroneous conclusions
including, as extreme cases, impossibility arguments against evolution theory. Moreover, careful examination of these paradoxes and related questions
prompts interesting topics for further research.
6.1. Nothing Ever Begins When You Think It Does
Lillian Hellman’s (1969) celebrated aphorism, apud [148], refers to biology’s
habit of (re)deploying a well-developed organ (Oργανoν, instrument, tool)
into a new role, or (re)adapting a former working solution into a new function.
Figure 4.t shows how the same basic 5-digit skeletal structure was readjusted
in forelimbs used for ﬂying, swimming, digging, and object manipulation. All
these examples can be traced back to the forelimb of primitive ﬁshes adapted
for walking in mud or close to water. Other examples of redeployment are
far less obvious, for example: (a) Lungs, developed in ﬁsh as a swim bladder,
a gas-sac used for buoyancy regulation, and later repurposed for breathing
air; (b) Feathers, developed for thermal insulation and regulation, and later
repurposed for gliding and ﬂying. The next quotation from Charles Darwin’s
Origin of Species, 6th ed., ch.VII, Miscellaneous Objections to the Theory of
Evolution, apud [148], addresses this topic.
All Mr. [George] Mivart’s objections will be, or have been,
considered in the present volume. The one new point which
appears to have struck many readers is, ‘That natural selection is incompetent to account for the incipient stages of useful structures.’ This subject is intimately connected with that
of the gradation of the characters, often accompanied by a
change of function, for instance, the conversion of a swimbladder into lungs...
Ch. Darwin (1872, p.177)
• Old Archetypes, New Prototypes, and Organic Retuning (future research)
When an organ is repurposed for a new function, it can maintain many
structural properties; in fact, this is how its evolutionary history can be traced.
However, the description and explanation of how the organ accomplishes its
new function requires, by deﬁnition, the postulation of a new prototype. In

some cases, a sequence of increasingly complex and more sophisticated prototypes may be better suited to follow the readaptation process of an organ
to its new function. This is a topic to be addressed in further research, focusing on the possible methodological contributions brought by the concept of
prototype, as deﬁned in this article.
A closely related topic for further research concerns the re-tuning process
of an organ to its function, as deﬁned by its new (assumed) prototype. Recent
redeployment to a new function suggests an adaptation process in progress
that is, therefore, far from optimal tuning and hence far from equilibrium conditions characteristic of convergence to a stable form. This non-equilibrium
condition also suggests fast rates of change in its recent evolutionary history
and, consequently, large variation of functional parameters in the population.
In contrast, long employment in the same function suggests an already successful adaptation process that, therefore, is close to optimal tuning or close
to equilibrium conditions characteristic of convergence to a stable form. This
equilibrium condition also suggests slow rates of change in its recent evolutionary history and, consequently, small variation of functional parameters in
the population. We believe that the concept of prototype and its accompanying ﬁne-tuning conditions, as deﬁned in this article, can be instrumental
for objective, quantitative, and statistical assessments of the aforementioned
convergence characteristics.
6.2. There are More Ways to the Wood than One, and He Which Knows
Many Ways May Go the Nearest
Joseph Swetnam’s (1617) celebrated aphorism proclaims the advantage of having alternative ways to ﬁght in combat, see [171]. In this paper, it refers to
biology’s talent for inventing diﬀerent tools to do a task, or building alternative organs (instruments) to fulﬁll a need. Just because a given functional
prototype is precisely deﬁned, it does not mean that it is the only possible way
to solve a problem. For example, biologists were able to identify many independent reinventions of “eyes to see”, converging not to one, but to a handful
of viable (i.e. biologically implementable) but diﬀerent functional prototypes,
see [90].
Figure 10 depicts diagrammatic schemata explaining the basic optics of a
Lobster eye (order Decapoda, phylum Arthropoda). In the lobster eye we can
recognize a few familiar structural components—most obviously, a “lens” and a
retina. Nevertheless, the vertebrate and cephalopod eyes previously examined

Vol. 20 (2026)

What is (an Organic) Analogy?

431

are equipped with a refractive lens used to project the captured image onto a
concave retina. In contrast, in the lobster eye, instead of the refractive lens,
there is a collimator, a spherical grid made of square reﬂexive cells, that is used
as a focusing device for projecting the captured image onto a convex spherical
retina half the size of this “reﬂective lens”.
The lobster eye has fascinating histories concerning its phylogenetic evolution, its ontogenetic embryology, its anatomical description, and the (very
late, in the 1970 s) discovery of the prototype explaining how it actually works,
see [1,48,51,63,90,144,181]. Afterwards, this prototype was used to invent new
technological devices in the ﬁeld of x-ray optics, where no adequate materials
are available for making a refractive lens, and even the best mirrors can only
reﬂect light at very shallow (almost tangent) angles. This is an important example of Biomimetics–the art and science of reutilizing biological prototypes
for creating or improving human technology, see [127,176,180].
• Analogies Across Distinct Prototypes (future research)
Although the prototypes explaining the optics of eyes based on refractive and reﬂective lenses are quite diﬀerent, both have some goals in common,
namely, to focus the captured image into an appropriate (concave / convex)
retina, allowing subsequent data acquisition and information processing, with
the ﬁnal purpose of giving a creature the capacity to see its environment. Moreover, the same quality parameters, like magniﬁcation, resolution, contrast, ﬁeld
depth, light sensitivity, etc., can be used to objectively evaluate and compare
the performance of these eyes. The possibility of using well-established benchmarks to objectively relate the performance of diﬀerent prototypes and their
variant implementations has important consequences: First, it allows comparative analyses contrasting how and why each prototype is able to achieve its
goals; Second, it allows the evaluation of how well each prototype eﬀectively
models the biological organ it is supposed to explain; Finally, this hierarchical setting allows the extension of analogical relations across diﬀerent organs
under scrutiny, even in the case where these organs are of diﬀerent (proto-)
types. The systematic development of such hierarchical analogies across different prototypes is a topic we intend to explore in future articles.
6.3. Archetypal Symbolic Language of the Honeybees (future research)

of a waggling run along a straight line, which is then embedded in an 8-shaped
circuit, see Fig. 11.l. The frequency and duration of waggling is interpreted by
the bees as a ﬂight distance, while the running line angle relative to the upward direction is interpreted as a ﬂight direction angle relative to the sun. The
language includes variations of the basic form that are used according to strict

transmitted and inherited, together with similarly pre-programmed behavioral
patterns, giving each individual bee the inborn capacity for its appropriate use.

time spans of generations. Moreover, the bees do their work, and communicate
(using their language) the information they need to share in order to do it,
without ever thinking about it (much less reﬂexively “talking” about why, how
or what they are doing).
In contrast, we humans try to understand all that by building models, i.e.
archetypes and prototypes, that are based on theoretical assumptions and must
be corroborated by empirical observations. While engaged in these studies, we
may use our own language(s) to share our conclusions (with other humans).
Moreover, in so doing, we may agree to adopt certain semantic shifts, introduce
new words, or even adopt new rules for how to articulate (in language) these
words (and the concepts they represent). Nevertheless, some deep features of
human language seem to follow invariant forms — like underlying structures of

6.4. Homology or Analogy as a Source of Knowledge, Rational
Metaphysics, and Ontology of Universals (Future Research)
Can we properly identify or use the same word to refer to two particular objects? Even if they have diﬀerent characteristics? For example, can we properly
speak of (1) a man and an eagle as being ‘vertebrates’ ? or (2) a human and
an octopus as having ‘eyes’ ? In previous sections we have, implicitly, answered
these questions in the aﬃrmative and also provided objective criteria for validating (or not) these identiﬁcations. Figure 12l,r render historical illustrations
of hierarchical abstractions of this kind used in philosophy; Fig. 12l from [36],
and Fig. 12r from [117]. However, our answers required making important distinctions and deﬁning strict conditions for establishing such an identiﬁcation
(Fig. 12). For example, the ﬁrst identiﬁcation concerns a homology referring to
a common archetype, while the second identiﬁcation concerns an analogy referring to a common prototype. Moreover, we established how these similarity
relations can, under adequate circumstances, be properly combined. Furthermore, we discussed how speciﬁc statistical signiﬁcance measures or logical truth
values can be used to objectively evaluate these similarities, and how speciﬁc
statistical tests of hypotheses can be used to validate (or not) these identiﬁcations.
Universal is a term used in philosophy to designate wide-ranging words,
abstract categories, concepts or predicates, general relations or laws, essential
ideas, etc. The issue under scrutiny, namely, the validity of using universals
that generalize or abstract essential characteristics of particular objects, is a
traditional problem in philosophy, debated from classical Greece and India to
the present days. For a variety of concepts and arguments on this topic or
applications related to our context, see [2,28–32,37,39,41,74,80,97,105,143,
145,146,152,153,178,182]. The terms Nominalism and Realism designate polar positions or opposite views concerning the use of universals. In the speciﬁc
context of this article, and having in mind the scope of our present discussions,
we summarize these views as follows:
• Nominalism holds that universals cannot express anything real, whether or
not something real in fact exists. Instead, universals can only express subjective
impressions, linguistic conventions, arbitrary meanings, established habits, etc.

7. Concluding Remarks and Acknowledgments
This article develops parallel and comparable but separate and speciﬁc deﬁnitions for the concepts of Archetype and Prototype, that are considered in
tandem with the derived concepts of Homology and Analogy. These novel
deﬁnitions lead to implementable methods of analysis (statistical inference
and hypothesis testing) that, in turn, facilitate their correct and productive
application in science. Moreover, these deﬁnitions and methods preserve the
possibility of oﬀering simultaneous and complementary explanations, allowing for useful interactions between these concepts, as alluded in the opening
quotations of this article by Niels Bohr, see [15–17].
Furthermore, the proposed deﬁnitions help avoid confounding the structural and functional properties of instruments (whether biological organs or
technological artifacts) and their evolution—mistakes that, in turn, are linked
to confounding discrete and continuous mathematical variables (whether deterministic or random). In this context, a correct and clear understanding of
the distinct roles played by structure vs. function and discrete vs. continuous

Finally, this article brieﬂy considers possible consequences of these conceptual
innovations and methodological developments in the areas of philosophy of
language and science, and suggests several topics for further research.
