Abstract
Although AI can accelerate scientific discovery, it also generates new epistemic risks that
prevailing rule-based governance frameworks are structurally ill-equipped to address. We
present four arguments for the claim that intellectual virtues offer an adequate response to
the challenges of AI-accelerated science. The Adaptability Argument states that rules
cannot keep pace with technological change. The Motivation Gap Argument shows that
compliance does not amount to epistemic motivation. The Human-in-the-Loop Argument
stresses that AI systems require virtuous human interpretation. Finally, the Fallibility-inPrinciple Argument maintains that AI systems remain fallible and, therefore, require
human oversight.
Moreover, we develop the first systematic mapping of intellectual virtues onto the CRediT
(contributor roles taxonomy) stages of the research process and extend this to encompass
the process of scientific evaluation. In doing so, we bridge virtue epistemology and the
governance of AI-accelerated science. We show how virtues such as intellectual justice,
intellectual scrutiny, intellectual humility, curiosity, and intellectual courage constitute the
intellectual infrastructure that enables scientists to evaluate AI-generated outputs and
preserve epistemic agency in an era of increasing outsourcing of cognitive effort. This
virtue-based framework offers a proactive and future-oriented normative foundation for
scientific practice in the age of AI.
Keywords: AI in science; virtue epistemology; intellectual justice; intellectual scrutiny; epistemic agency;
responsible AI; CRediT; curiosity

AI not only speeds up the scientific discovery process, but it can also endanger the

saw (Ji et al. 2023) and reproduce biases (Ananya 2024). However, it has the potential to

is how to optimize its use in science. To address this question, we propose a normative
intellectual virtue framework for maximizing the benefits and minimizing the epistemic
damage.1 More precisely, we study how intellectual virtues help scientists in the quest for
knowledge in the AI era.
The intellectual virtue theory provides an ideal framework for addressing the
challenges associated with AI-accelerated science because it focuses on human capacities. In the scientific context, intellectual virtues such as curiosity, open-mindedness,
and critical thinking help with managing misinformation and finding reliable sources.
For example, scientists using AI should be curious about unexpected results, open to
new methods, and critical of data sources.
The ethical virtue framework can be traced back to Aristotle (Aristotle and Crisp
2014) who defined it as a middle ground between two vices. Intellectual virtue theory, as
a newer framework, is helping to understand the learning process. Exercising intellectual
virtues leads to more justified beliefs and genuine knowledge (e.g., Code 1984;
Montmarquet 1992). It is gaining prominence in epistemology, positive psychology, and
educational studies. For example, Krumrei-Mancuso et al. (2020) showed how intellectual humility can benefit the learning process, and Orona and Pritchard (2022)
investigated ways how teaching intellectual virtues increases curiosity. Our framework is
situated primarily within the responsibilist tradition in virtue epistemology (Code 1987;
Montmarquet 1992), which holds that intellectual virtues are acquired character traits
involving appropriate motivation and effort, rather than mere innate cognitive faculties.
This responsibilist orientation is significant because it entails that virtues can be
developed and strengthened through deliberate practice (Baehr 2013; Krumrei-Mancuso
et al. 2020; Orona and Pritchard 2022). The trainability of intellectual virtues is what
makes a virtue-based framework particularly suitable for an era of rapid technological
change.
Our contribution is threefold. First, we develop four arguments for the necessity of
intellectual virtues in AI-accelerated science: the Adaptability Argument, the Motivation
Gap Argument, the Human-in-the-Loop Argument, and the more general Fallibility-inPrinciple Argument (Section 2). Second, we bridge virtue epistemology and the governance of AI-accelerated science by mapping intellectual virtues onto the ethical
principles of responsible AI (Section 3). Third, we provide the first systematic mapping
of intellectual virtues onto CRediT (contributor roles taxonomy) research stages and
scientific evaluation, showing how different virtues matter at various stages of the
scientific discovery process (Section 4). We discuss the broader implications of our
framework for epistemic training and the preservation of human epistemic agency in
Section 5.
We use ‘intellectual’ to encompass both the epistemic and cognitive dimensions.

2. Why is virtue epistemology needed for AI in science?
2.1. Four arguments for the necessity of intellectual virtues
We contend that rules and intellectual virtues are both useful for the governance of AIaccelerated science, but that they serve different purposes and operate on different levels.
Rules codify shared standards and enable institutional oversight, whereas intellectual
virtues are dispositions that also guide epistemic judgement in situations that rules do
not yet cover. Our aim in this section is not to settle the long-standing debate between
rule- and virtue-based approaches. Rather, we motivate the claim that intellectual virtues
are needed for epistemically responsible AI-accelerated science. We support this claim
through four interlocking arguments.
2.1.1. The Adaptability Argument
Premise 1: AI technologies evolve faster than regulatory systems.
Premise 2: Rule-based frameworks depend on the prior identification of specific
epistemic harms.
Conclusion: Rules alone are insufficient to ensure epistemically responsible AI use.
Intellectual virtues, by contrast, provide adaptable epistemic judgement that can be
exercised across novel and unanticipated contexts. The two mechanisms are complementary rather than competitive. Rules retain their function of consolidating the lessons
learned, while intellectual virtues also carry the epistemic load during the lag, when the
harms are not yet understood. Although some lag may be epistemically valuable because
it allows harms to become visible enough for appropriate rules to be formulated,
intellectual virtues remain necessary during that interval to guide scientists in anticipating and mitigating potential harms.
In contrast to regulations that frequently change, intellectual virtues and vices are
more stable traits, which are also interconnected with human cognition and thinking
styles (Krumrei-Mancuso et al. 2020). Nevertheless, some virtues and vices become
more salient in certain contexts and we can train them during education (e.g., Orona and
Pritchard 2022). For example, the disposition to critically examine AI outputs is a
specification of intellectual scrutiny, which is violated when we surrender our epistemic
effort to an algorithm.
2.1.2. The Motivation Gap Argument
Premise 1: Scientific inquiry requires epistemic motivations, such as curiosity and
wonder, which guide what researchers choose to explore.
Premise 2: Rule-based frameworks and AI systems can regulate behaviour
and suggest directions, but they cannot generate or appropriately guide epistemic
motivation.
Premise 3: Intellectual virtues, especially curiosity and intellectual autonomy, are part
of the epistemic motivation of researchers. They are salient in the AI-accelerated science
as they, for example, help them retain control over the direction of inquiry.
Conclusion: Intellectual virtues are necessary for responsible AI-accelerated science
because they secure the motivation for inquiry that rule-based frameworks and AI
systems cannot provide.
Intellectual virtues ensure that researchers do not merely follow externally imposed
rules or algorithmically suggested paths. They actively help in deciding what is worth
investigating. In doing so, intellectual virtues preserve human epistemic agency.

2.1.3. The Human-in-the-Loop Argument
Premise 1: Sub-symbolic AI systems2 used in science are epistemically opaque; their
internal reasoning processes cannot be fully inspected or understood by human users.
Premise 2: Opaque systems require human epistemic interpretation; scientists must
judge when to trust, question, or override AI outputs without full access to the system’s
reasoning.
Premise 3: Such interpretation is not a mechanical process; it requires intellectual
character traits such as intellectual scrutiny, intellectual courage, and intellectual
humility.
Conclusion: Epistemically responsible use of sub-symbolic AI depends on the
intellectual virtues of the human agents who interpret, evaluate, and act upon AIgenerated outputs.
This connects directly to the problem of epistemic surrender,3 which is the tendency
to defer uncritically to AI recommendations, thereby ceding one’s epistemic agency to
an opaque system. Rezazade Mehrizi et al. (2023) documented such a phenomenon
among radiologists, who followed incorrect AI suggestions 72% of the time. The
Human-in-the-Loop Argument shows that the proper response to the tendency of
surrendering epistemic effort is not more rules about when to trust AI, but the cultivation of intellectual character that sustains independent epistemic judgement.
2.1.4. The Fallibility-in-Principle Argument
This argument extends the Human-in-the-Loop Argument from the specific case of
opaque systems to the general principle that all AI in science requires virtuous human
oversight.
Premise 1: Even the most advanced AI systems are fallible in principle. They can
produce errors, or have epistemic limitations, for example, consider only a limited
number of hypotheses.
Premise 2: Even if AI systems were to outperform human scientists on average across
a wide range of tasks, their epistemic fallibility in any particular case would remain.
Conclusion: Human epistemic oversight is indispensable because an independent
epistemic check reduces the probability of undetected error.
For human oversight to be meaningful rather than ceremonial, the human in the loop
must possess the intellectual virtues necessary to exercise critical judgement. Therefore,
the scientists need intellectual scrutiny, courage, and autonomy to serve as a genuine
epistemic safeguard not as a mere rubber stamp.
Our framework treats responsible AI use not as a matter of rule-following but as a
matter of good intellectual agency. This shift in framing from compliance to character is
significant. It means that the question is not merely ‘Are scientists following the right
rules?’ but ‘Are scientists the kind of epistemic agents who can be trusted to use AI well?’
The virtue-based approach prioritizes the latter question and thereby provides a more
robust foundation for responsible AI governance in science.

By emphasizing researchers’ character and judgement, the virtue-epistemology
framework offers a complementary perspective to other approaches in the epistemology
of AI, which have focused primarily on ethics (Hagendorff 2022), explainability
(Sullivan 2022; Zednik 2021), or the ethical virtuousness of AI solutions themselves
(Farina et al. 2024). Although these approaches provide important normative constraints, they tend to frame scientists as compliance agents rather than as epistemic
agents exercising judgement under uncertainty.
We aim to fill this gap by showing how virtuous epistemic conduct can underpin the
responsible and effective use of AI in scientific research. This emphasis on virtues is not
meant to replace regulatory or technical solutions, but to ground them in the qualities of
scientists themselves (Vallor 2016). By focusing on virtues, we not only respond to
today’s challenges but also prepare scientists to navigate the unforeseen challenges of
tomorrow with sound judgement and integrity.
3. Intellectual virtues corresponding to core ethical principles of responsible AI
Hagendorff (2022) analysed the ethical virtues corresponding to the four frequently
cited principles of responsible AI, namely to the principles of fairness, transparency,
accountability, and privacy. He connects them to the virtues of justice, honesty,
responsibility, and care. These ethical virtues and principles have their epistemic
counterparts. For example, the virtue of intellectual justice corresponds to the fairness
principle. Intellectual justice, as we understand it here, is the disposition to include the
perspective of diverse knowers, particularly those structurally disadvantaged (Anderson
2012; Fricker 2007). Its corresponding vice is intellectual injustice: ungrounded intellectual exclusion of speakers from the underprivileged. In the context of AI, intellectual
justice is especially salient because training data bias can reproduce and amplify exactly
these forms of injustice at scale. The core epistemic function of intellectual justice is the
preservation of knowledge diversity and ensuring that the epistemic contributions of
under-represented communities are neither lost nor distorted by algorithmic mediation.
In the following, we discuss the intellectual counterparts to Hagendorff’s ethical
framework in detail. In Section 4, we further develop the intellectual virtue framework
by mapping key intellectual virtues onto the stages of the scientific process where AI can
contribute.
Although intellectual virtues are often attributed to individuals, they can also
characterize scientific communities as collective epistemic agents (Anderson 2012;
Lahroodi 2007). For example, a scientific community can be intellectually just when it
systematically includes disadvantaged perspectives and intellectually responsible when it
establishes procedures for scrutinizing evidence and correcting collective error. In AIassisted science, such group virtues are especially important because epistemic harms
often arise from shared algorithms and datasets. As I have argued elsewhere, shared
algorithms can create algorithmic monocultures that homogenize scientific judgements
and amplify errors across the community (Sikimić 2025).
Intellectual honesty (Byerly 2023) and intellectual humility (Krumrei-Mancuso and
Rouse 2016) guide our need for transparency in research. These virtues help scientists
properly assess the limits of their results and motivate them to strive for their
explainability. Intellectual humility refers to recognizing our own limitations in learning,
including the fallibility of our senses. In the context of AI, it stands for the ability to use
the machine with critical reasoning. Intellectual honesty facilitates the transparency of
research. It is particularly important when it comes to the use of AI because many AI

concerns the models themselves rather than their individual outputs, and it comes in
different kinds with different sources (Alvarado 2021). Moreover, the problem is not
merely that explanations of these models are hard to obtain. As Boge (2022) argues, the
difficulty is structural. We do not know how the training process arrives at the final
parameter values, we do not know what the individual parameters mean, and there is no
established way to find out, so we do not know what knowledge, if any, the model

large language model (LLM) GPT-3 (Brown et al. 2020), is therefore not the source of
the problem by itself, but it illustrates the scale at which this structural opacity arises.
The principle of accountability and the ethical virtue of responsibility have the direct
intellectual counterpart in intellectual responsibility (Montmarquet 1992) and intellectual conscientiousness (Montmarquet 1987). A person who exercises intellectual
responsibility only accepts beliefs that are supported by evidence, for example
AI-generated outputs with real references and sufficient explanation. An intellectually
conscientious person ensures that they gather sufficient evidence before forming
judgement. These virtues have a positive impact on science. A model of scientific
communities demonstrates that collecting sufficient evidence prior to advocating for a
hypothesis significantly improves scientific accuracy of findings in comparison with
communicating preliminary results immediately (Sikimić and Herud-Sikimić 2022).
Curiosity clusters virtues such as curiosity (Whitcomb 2010), inquisitiveness
(Watson 2015) and love of knowledge (Roberts and Wood 2007). The obvious related
vice is lack of interest. More interestingly, curiosity and love of knowledge also involve
focusing on necessary and fruitful information rather than on epistemic junk, such as
gossip. In other words, the other vice related to curiosity is being interested and spending
cognitive effort on irrelevant and maybe even epistemically harmful content. Thus,
curiosity is of exceptional importance for science. It is both linked to the motivation of
researchers, and it helps in choosing which hypotheses to explore. In the context of AI, it
has to do with data collections and trustworthy research. Collecting or wrongly
interpreting data, violating the privacy of participants, etc. are on the antipode of
curiosity similar to reading gossip newspapers. Finally, in the misinformation age, which
is accelerated with AI technologies, self-regulation and proper direction in the domain of
curiosity are becoming a virtue of the future. In Table 1, we summarize these insights.
4. Intellectual virtues across the stages of AI-supported research
As AI reshapes scientific practice, researchers must cultivate intellectual virtues that
enable its effective use. The relevance of particular virtues varies across stages of the

scientific process, including research design and execution, communication of results,
peer review, and grant evaluation. We propose a framework of intellectual virtues that
are especially important for engaging with AI at these different stages of scientific work.
To structure this proposal, we draw on, and go beyond, the CRediT nomenclature (Allen
et al. 2014), which offers a widely accepted framework for distinguishing different types
of scientific contributions beyond the traditional focus on authorship alone. This
mapping constitutes our primary constructive contribution; it provides a structured,
stage-specific normative tool that can guide intellectual virtue cultivation in practice.
CRediT defines specific roles for the authors of a manuscript, such as conceptualization, data curation, formal analysis, and supervision. Please note that some parts of
the CRediT nomenclature are condensed for better overview. In addition, we include
scientific tasks that fall outside the authoring of papers, such as peer review and
dissemination activities. These activities play a central role in maintaining the epistemic
quality of science, for example by filtering unreliable results. This framework allows us to
connect intellectual virtues with specific scientific practices, while also highlighting
forms of epistemic labour that are essential for collective knowledge production in
science but remain under-recognized.
The intellectual virtues that are important at various stages of the discovery process
can differ depending on whether these stages are carried out by human researchers or
delegated to AI systems. Consider, for example, the CRediT item ‘Visualization’. Human
researchers need to be intellectually honest while they are visualizing data and while
promoting AI to plot data. They may be tempted to exaggerate their results, for instance
by choosing plot axes that make differences between biological populations appear
larger than they are, or by selecting so-called representative images that in fact depict
extreme cases. When researchers use AI, they should prompt it to visualize the data as it
is, rather than to produce visualizations designed to support a favoured hypothesis.
When data visualization is handled by AI, an additional risk arises. AI systems may
generate visualizations that include fabricated or distorted data. In this case, the scientist
working with AI must exercise intellectual responsibility and intellectual justice. This
requires awareness of potential biases and errors introduced by AI systems and a
willingness to take responsibility for the epistemic quality of the visualization, including
critically questioning and correcting it when necessary.
At the same time, we acknowledge that there are limits to what individual scientists
can evaluate. Some models resist understanding in principle, and many algorithms are
proprietary and cannot be inspected at all. In such cases, intellectual humility and
intellectual honesty call for second-order strategies. For example, instead of asking an
LLM to visualize the data directly, virtuous scientists can ask for the code to visualize it
themselves with tools suitable to visualize data such as R.

Additionally, intellectual autonomy is important for ensuring that researchers
remain in the driver’s seat of the scientific enterprise. It enables scientists using AI to
choose their own research direction, reach their own conclusions, and explore their own
hypotheses despite convincing sounding explanations from an algorithm. This is
connected to scientific integrity because only humans can bear responsibility for it, and,
therefore, they must remain responsible for and in charge of AI solutions. Furthermore,
intellectual autonomy affects the well-being of researchers because it ensures a sense of
ownership. An over-reliance on AI would reduce the intellectual autonomy of
researchers, especially if the reasonings of the machine are not transparent. For example,
an intellectual autonomous researcher would include information it got from an AI to
refine their own hypothesis, but they would not leave the hypothesis formulation itself to
a machine. The risk of ceding intellectual autonomy connects directly to the Fallibilityin-Principle Argument; even when AI generates plausible hypotheses, the scientist’s
independent judgement remains the indispensable safeguard against systematic error in
the conceptualization stage.
4.2. Methodology
For a given scientific question, the most appropriate methods should be chosen. This
choice should be driven by different intellectual virtues. Again, intellectual justice plays a
key role. Intellectual justice should ensure that a methodology is chosen with the impact
on different populations in mind. For example, in medical science, Cortina et al. (2025)
noticed that data filters commonly used for selecting electronic health records disproportionately affect minorities. When filter criteria are chosen without the impact on
underprivileged populations in mind, these populations can be excluded from the
training data, and hence the AI can perform worse for them. A scientist who is
intellectually just will always monitor the impact of their choices on different
populations.
Furthermore, AI is reproducing data from the training dataset. Hence, AI will in most
cases suggest the methods used in the past to address research questions. The virtue of
open-mindedness helps researchers to consider innovative methods that could better
address a new research question, rather than relying on the same methods they have
used in the past. Finally, open-mindedness helps researchers profiting from the
advantages from AI. For example, they can employ AI tools which can help choose the
best statistical analysis for a task (DeBruine and Lakens 2025).
4.3. Investigation, data collection, and curation
Investigation covers the performance of experiments and the collection of data, whereas
data curation is concerned with annotating these data and making them available for use
and reuse. The main intellectual virtue important for data collection with AI is curiosity.
Curiosity helps researchers to address meaningful research questions and to investigate
unexpected AI outputs. The corresponding vices related to curiosity are indifference
towards what is new or unplanned and a disregard for privacy. In particular, the latter is
frequently challenged by AI aided research. Van Noorden (2020) found that a significant
minority of facial recognition researchers held surprisingly lax attitudes towards data
ethics. Curiosity also helps researchers to investigate why particular data are collected
and what data might be missing from an AI collected dataset.

Additionally, data collection and curation are strongly connected to intellectual
justice. Data collection and curation are performed with a specific purpose in mind
(Leonelli 2016, 2019). This purpose is not necessarily aligned with the subject of another
academic study. Many researchers rely on datasets that are known to be biased (Paullada
et al. 2021). For example, Crawford (2021) discusses how data used for training AI are
disproportionately sourced from the Global North, thereby shaping the epistemic and
cultural assumptions embedded in these models. In contrast, an intellectually just
researcher would go the extra mile and collect data that are most meaningful for the
study in a responsible way.
4.4. Analysis and interpretation of the results
Scientific analysis is the evaluation of data using formal techniques, such as statistics.
The main virtues important for scientists using AI for this task are intellectual honesty
and intellectual humility. Intellectual honesty and humility help to ensure transparency
and explainability by honestly reporting of the inputs, outputs, the algorithm, and
modelling choices. Intellectual humility requires prudence with the AI-generated results
because of the black-box challenge.
Using AI and machine learning involves certain modelling decisions, such as how to
deal with missing data and how to define sufficient data quality. These decisions
influence the results produced by AI systems and therefore need to be reported honestly.
In addition, analyses conducted by AI need to be explainable. This is one of the main
normative guidelines governing the use of AI (e.g., Zednik 2021). Furthermore, intellectual honesty is essential for scientists using AI for formal analysis because they need to
include all collected data, not only the data that support their hypothesis. The use of AI,
for example for pattern recognition, can make it more difficult to detect whether all data
were used in an analysis, whereas classical statistical tools follow more established rules
and are often easier for reviewers and readers to evaluate.
Although intellectual honesty is required to analyse data in the most appropriate way,
intellectual humility helps researchers to acknowledge the limits of their own understanding of AI-generated outputs. Some AI tools support scientists in following best
practices for data analysis (e.g., DeBruine and Lakens 2025), and an intellectually
humble researcher is willing to follow such guidances. Moreover, when AI tools are used
to identify patterns in data, intellectual humility helps researchers to admit when they do
not understand the model’s reasoning and to investigate this reasoning either independently or in collaboration with appropriate experts, thereby increasing the transparency of the decision-making process. The Human-in-the-Loop Argument is directly
operative here; when the AI’s reasoning is opaque, the scientist’s intellectual character,
not a checklist, determines whether the resulting interpretation is intellectually
responsible.
4.5. Writing
Writing covers the drafting and editing of the manuscript as well as the presentation of
the study. The key virtues for scholarly communication are intellectual responsibility
and intellectual honesty.
Although an AI system can produce a convincing sounding argument when
prompted with scientific results, the human in the loop needs to exercise intellectual
responsibility. Scientists remain accountable for the work and must carefully evaluate

the evidence to understand the implications and limits of the study. Intellectual
responsibility cannot be outsourced to an algorithm or an external AI provider. This
view is reflected in many journal policies, which do not allow generative AI systems to be
listed as authors because they cannot be held responsible for the content of a study.
A second important aspect is intellectual honesty. It is essential for scholarly
communication because claims that are not sufficiently supported by evidence risk
polluting the scientific literature. For example, if an AI system extrapolates results and
produces claims that are not supported by the available evidence. It can even hallucinate
convincingly sounding references that explain any given behaviour (Ji et al. 2023).
Hence, the scientist must be intellectually honest and correct the AI whenever necessary.
Only when the limitations of a study are honestly reported can readers properly evaluate
the relevance of the findings for their own research questions. Sikimić and HerudSikimić (2022) use agent-based models to illustrate how honest communication of
evidence helps scientific communities reach the optimal conclusions more quickly.
Their results show that intellectual honesty is an important virtue for collective
knowledge acquisition.
4.6. Peer review
Peer review is currently the main method of scientific evaluation. Usually, peer review
defines which project is funded and which paper is published. Studies have shown that
reviewers are already turning to AI to assist them in the review of manuscripts, even
when they are not supposed to (Naddaf 2025). However, automatizing part of the peer
review process can be very attractive because of the high workload associated with it.
Studies show a very low inner consistency of the reviewers (Bornmann et al. 2010).
If carefully designed, AI can be less biased than human reviewers (Sikimić 2025).
Therefore, some organizations aim to profit from its potential. For example, the
Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence started a pilot programme to
include an additional AI-generated commentary while evaluating abstract submissions
for its conference (Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence 2025). The
AI evaluation is one of the three, where the other two are given by humans. However, if
the peer review process would be more automated, it is essential to allow a rebuttal from
the authors which should be judged by human reviewers or review boards because of the
persistent concerns about the transparency of AI-based recommendations.
Although researchers should remain open to new ideas and new ways of generating
evidence, the produced evidence must be reliable. A researcher exercising intellectual
scrutiny asks whether data collection was appropriate, whether analytical choices were
adequate, and whether the conclusions drawn from the results are justified. Intellectual
scrutiny, as we understand it, is closely related to but not identical with critical thinking.
It is the cultivated disposition to subject evidence, reasoning, and sources to rigorous
evaluation, what classical epistemology also calls the virtue of intellectual carefulness,
now exercised in the specific context of evaluating algorithmically mediated claims. This
practice helps to distinguish well-supported hypotheses from mere speculation and is
essential in peer review for maintaining the integrity of the scientific record. Intellectual
scrutiny is essential, given the low transparency of many AI applications. Because the
internal processes of many AI tools are opaque, the responsibility falls on the researchers
using these tools to carefully evaluate their outputs. In particular, decisions about which
data are included in the training of a model have a substantial influence on the results it

produces. This requirement applies not only to generative AI but also to more traditional forms of machine learning.
4.7. Funding allocation
The Spanish La Caixa Foundation already uses AI as a pre-screening method to identify
applications with a low chance of success, in an attempt to reduce the workload for
reviewers (Adam 2025). Because funding allocation largely relies on the peer review of
research ideas and researcher profiles, similar intellectual virtues play a role. In addition,
the virtue of collective intellectual responsibility is particularly salient. Collective
intellectual responsibility is an intellectual virtue that applies not only to individual
agents but also to scientific communities and institutions. It consists in a shared
commitment to upholding epistemic standards.
This collective virtue can be challenged by AI because of its scalability. For example, a
single biased reviewer can only affect a limited number of applications from a small
number of funding agencies. In contrast, AI-based solutions could systematically discriminate against applications with specific features. This risk is particularly high when
the same AI system or training dataset is used across different funding bodies. Hence,
the use of AI in grant review needs to be implemented with particular care (Sikimić
2025). To practise collective intellectual responsibility, funding agencies and everybody
involved therefore need to monitor algorithms and datasets, allow for rebuttals, and
ideally combine AI assessment with human review. For example, the La Caixa
Foundation assigned two reviewers to recheck applications that are filtered out by the
algorithm (Adam 2025).
In addition, the use of AI for funding allocation should be strongly guided by the
virtue of intellectual justice. Algorithmic systems can reproduce and amplify biases that
are difficult to detect. For example, generative AI systems exhibit gender bias when
asked to write letters of reference for male and female applicants (Kaplan et al. 2024). To
counteract such effects, those using AI for funding allocation must practise intellectual
justice and carefully examine how these systems affect marginalized populations.
Finally, AI can discourage novel ideas, particularly when it is used for research
evaluation, as it performs best when assessing work that is close to its training data.
Radically new ideas may fall outside the scope of AI-based evaluation and therefore risk
not receiving the necessary funding (Sikimić 2025). Consequently, those involved in
review processes that use AI should practise intellectual courage and remain willing to
support bold and unconventional ideas, especially within funding schemes designed to
promote innovation. However, humans are often inclined to outsource intellectual work
to algorithms. As a result, researchers may follow AI recommendations even when these
conflict with their own judgements. For example, Rezazade Mehrizi et al. (2023) showed
that radiologists largely followed AI suggestions, leading to incorrect decisions in 72% of
cases when the system was primed with a wrong recommendation, compared with 78%
correct decisions when primed with a correct one. This finding suggests that users often
fail to sufficiently practise intellectual courage and instead follow AI in a relatively
uncritical manner. However, Van der Sijs et al. (2006) showed a high tendency of
clinicians to override computational drug safety alerts. To balance these tendencies,
researchers need to exercise intellectual courage while working with AI systems. This
includes the willingness to challenge, override, or reject algorithmic outputs when they
conflict with well-grounded scientific knowledge, empirical evidence, or sound methodological reasoning but not completely ignoring it for other reasons.

4.8. Replication and meta-studies
One of the challenges that generative AI brings to science is its ability to generate
convincing sounding arguments and to hallucinate information it has never encountered. Hence, replication efforts aimed at independently validating important claims are
becoming increasingly important. At the same time, modern technology can also
facilitate replication. For example, AI systems can extract and standardize methods
sections from published papers, and robotic platforms such as the mobile robotic
chemist (Burger et al. 2020) can be used to automatically attempt replications of
experimental procedures. To use AI in the best possible way, researchers should be
intellectually open and intellectually humble.
Intellectual openness is required to adopt novel tools such as AI for scientific inquiry.
Moreover, this virtue helps scientists to facilitate the replication of their results, for
example by sharing data, materials, and protocols. Replication should not be understood
as an attack on one’s work but rather as recognition that the work is sufficiently
important to warrant independent verification. In addition, intellectual openness
enables researchers to consider new methods proposed by algorithmic systems, especially when these methods promise to yield the same results if the underlying hypothesis
is correct.
One of the strengths of generative AI is its ability to synthesize written text from
multiple sources. This capability is particularly valuable for replication studies and metastudies. Although replication studies aim to reproduce previous findings to strengthen
the robustness of scientific claims, meta-studies aggregate evidence from many different
studies to support or challenge broader conclusions. In both cases, scientists using AI
must practise the virtue of intellectual humility. This virtue allows researchers to recognize the limits of their own knowledge and abilities. For example, although replication
is often considered the gold standard in biology, it is far from straightforward. In
particular, when advanced methods are involved, researchers may lack the technical
skills required to replicate a finding, and a failure to replicate in such cases does not by
itself warrant refuting the original hypothesis. Similarly, if an AI system fails to identify
supporting studies for a given claim, this does not mean that the claim should be
rejected. AI systems do not have exhaustive knowledge, and researchers using AI for
replication should interpret AI outputs with intellectual humility.
4.9. Science communication
AI’s ability to summarize data and to generate engaging social media content about
research can help scientists communicate their findings and reach audiences beyond the
academic community. However, effective communication is not achieved automatically.
Researchers still need to develop communication skills to disseminate their work
effectively. When AI is used to support the dissemination, the virtue of intellectual
honesty is particularly important.
Intellectual honesty requires acknowledging uncertainty, reporting limitations, and
being transparent about methodological choices. A lack of intellectual honesty can erode
public trust and harm researchers and scientific institutions in the long term. If not
carefully guided, AI systems tend to oversell findings or present speculative results as
established facts, thereby challenging this virtue. At the same time, AI-based tools such
as automated translation systems can facilitate communication with non-academic
audiences and help overcome language barriers (Vučković & Sikimić, 2023). With

5. Implications for intellectual training of researchers and the future of scientific
virtues
Our framework has two major implications that merit separate discussion: the feasibility
of virtue cultivation and the necessary shift in the virtue profile of future scientists.
5.1. Virtue cultivation and epistemic training
On the responsibilist account we endorse, intellectual virtues are not fixed personality
traits, but cultivable dispositions that can be developed, strengthened, and refined
through targeted interventions. This claim finds support both in philosophical argument
and empirical evidence. Philosophically, Baehr (2013) has proposed a pedagogical
framework for intellectual virtue education. Empirically, Orona and Pritchard (2022)
demonstrated that curiosity can be increased through a structured educational intervention, and Krumrei-Mancuso et al. (2020) showed that intellectual humility is positively correlated with knowledge acquisition and can be enhanced through reflective
practice. These findings from psychology corroborate the responsibilist thesis: virtues
are learnable.
This has direct implications for AI governance. If intellectual virtues can be trained,
then institutional investment in intellectual character formation, through graduate
curricula, professional development programs, and research ethics training, becomes a
viable and indeed necessary component of responsible AI governance. Our CRediTmapped framework (Table 2) can serve as the basis for such training programmes;
researchers can be trained in the specific virtues most relevant to their stage of the
research process. For instance, a workshop on AI-assisted data analysis could focus on
cultivating intellectual humility and honesty, while training for AI-assisted peer review
could emphasize intellectual scrutiny and courage.
5.2. The shifting virtue profile of future scientists
AI will transform not only what scientists do but also who they need to be as epistemic
agents. As AI systems take over routine cognitive tasks such as literature search, statistical analysis, code generation, and even initial hypothesis formulation, the distinctive
epistemic contribution of human scientists will increasingly reside in the intellectual
capacities that cannot be automated, such as critical judgement, interpretive creativity,
intellectual courage, and the ability to recognize the limits of algorithmic reasoning.
Arguable, future scientists will be more prolific as AI acceleration will enable them to
produce more research outputs more quickly, but the epistemic quality of their work will
depend more heavily on their intellectual character than ever before.
This suggests a reevaluation of the virtue profile for scientific training. Although
traditional scientific education emphasized technical skills and domain knowledge, the
AI era demands equal investment in the cultivation of intellectual virtues. Our
framework provides the normative structure for this reevaluation. By mapping virtues to
research stages, it identifies where and how the scientific community should invest in
developing these capacities.

6. Conclusion
AI can help researchers complete routine tasks efficiently, it provides unprecedented
access to knowledge, including automatic translations of scientific literature from
around the globe, and enables them to find patterns in large datasets. For example, in
2024 the developers of the AI-based protein structure prediction tool AlphaFold were
awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Given these capabilities, AI has become a
valuable asset for many scientists in a remarkably short time.
The skills scientists need in the era of AI differ from those required in earlier periods.
For example, widely used LLMs allow many researchers to write efficient code with
minimal programming background, reducing the need to invest heavily in learning such
technical skills. Similarly, automatic translation tools enable access to scientific literature
from diverse linguistic contexts without the long process of language acquisition. At the
same time, a basic understanding of AI systems, and of LLMs in particular, has become
essential for scientific practice.
Alongside these changes in skills, certain intellectual virtues are becoming salient in
the age of AI. In particular, intellectual scrutiny and intellectual justice have become
more important than ever. Although human researchers typically make deliberate
choices about which sources they consult, LLMs often present information without clear
references, making the validation of claims more difficult. A scientist who exercises
intellectual scrutiny, therefore, goes beyond surface-level outputs by identifying relevant
sources and verifying claims against original data. Moreover, AI systems are commonly
trained on historical data that may reflect biases or stereotypes. For this reason, the
virtue of intellectual justice is crucial to ensure that diverse perspectives are taken into
account, and that the scientific process remains value driven, rather than becoming a
self-reinforcing feedback loop based on biased data.
Given the rapid pace of technological change, it is essential that scholars are prepared
to engage with new tools in a virtuous manner, guided by intellectual virtues rather than
relying solely on external rules or technical safeguards. Intellectual virtues can be
cultivated through targeted interventions and strengthening critical thinking.
The intellectual virtues of the scientists using AI should be considered as one part of
the puzzle to strive for a better science in the age of AI. For the most holistic perspective,
we need to consider the virtues of AI developers and the collective virtues of institutions
as well. The virtues most important for the developers of AI for science differ from those
of scientists using AI for research. Some intellectual virtues are important for both users
and developers. For example, intellectual justice ensures that the training data represent
diverse perspectives and do not stay focused on the Global North, and that we intellectually mitigate algorithmic biases. However, virtues such as foresight are more salient
for developers because they should consider how their technology could develop in
future. From the perspective of institutions, the effect of AI on the scientific community
should be addressed, in particular, considering the potential of AI monocultures and
monopolies.4 This represents a fruitful avenue for future research.
The ease with which AI systems can overstate results or even generate entirely false
claims increases the complexity of peer review and heightens the need for replication
efforts and meta-studies. Alongside the cultivation of intellectual virtues in individual
researchers, systematic institutional measures are, therefore, required to maintain the
